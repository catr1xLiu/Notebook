> [!quote] Definition
> ## Functions Between Vector Spaces

Recall that for sets $U$ and $W$, a <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">function</strong></u> $f: U \to W$ is a rule which takes any <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">input</strong></u> from $U$ and associates it with a unique <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">output</strong></u> in $W$. Often denoted as $f(u) \in W$ for $u \in U$.

- $U$ is called the <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">domain</strong></u> of $f$
- $W$ is called the <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">codomain</strong></u> of $f$  
- $f(U) \subseteq W$ is called the <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">image</strong></u> (or range) of $f$

If we let $U = \mathbb{R}^n$ and $W = \mathbb{R}^m$ and $\vec{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, we can define $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ by $f(\vec{x}) = A\vec{x}$ for some $m \times n$ matrix $A$.

> [!example] Example
> ## Matrix Mapping

Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & -1 \\ 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$. Define $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ as $f(\vec{x}) = A\vec{x}$.

Then: 
$$f\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & -1 \\ 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 1 \\ 12 \end{bmatrix}$$

In general, for any $\begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^2$:
$$f\left(\begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & -1 \\ 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a + 2b \\ 3a - b \\ 2a + 5b \end{bmatrix}$$

> [!quote] Definition
> ## Linear Transformation (Linearity Test)

A function $L: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ that satisfies

$$L(b\vec{x} + c\vec{y}) = bL(\vec{x}) + cL(\vec{y})$$

for all $\vec{x}, \vec{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $b, c \in \mathbb{R}$ is called a <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">linear transformation</strong></u> (or <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">linear mapping</strong></u>).

**Geometrically:** Linear transformations preserve gridlines, keep them evenly spaced and parallel.

> [!example] Example
> ## Checking Linearity

Are the following mappings linear?

**a)** $L: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3$, $L\left(\begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} a+b \\ a-b \\ ab \end{bmatrix}$

**b)** $H: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$, $H\left(\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ \vdots \\ x_n \end{bmatrix}\right) = \vec{x} \cdot \vec{a}$ for a fixed $\vec{a} \in \mathbb{R}^n$

**c)** $F: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2$, $F\left(\begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} a+b+c \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> 
> **Important Note:** To prove linearity, we must show the linearity test holds in general. However, if you have a feeling that it's not linear, you just need a single counterexample.
> 
> ### Part (a): Not Linear
> 
> Consider $\vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $\vec{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Does $L(\vec{x} + \vec{y}) = L(\vec{x}) + L(\vec{y})$?
> 
> $$L(\vec{x} + \vec{y}) = L\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> While:
> $$L(\vec{x}) + L(\vec{y}) = L\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right) + L\left(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> Since $L(\vec{x} + \vec{y}) \neq L(\vec{x}) + L(\vec{y})$, so $L$ is **not** a linear transformation.
> 
> **Prediction:** Not linear because it has the product term $ab$.
> 
> ### Part (b): Linear
> 
> Let $\vec{x}, \vec{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, $b, c \in \mathbb{R}$, and $\vec{a} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be fixed. Then using properties of dot products:
> 
> $$\begin{aligned}
> H(b\vec{x} + c\vec{y}) &= (b\vec{x} + c\vec{y}) \cdot \vec{a} \\
> &= (b\vec{x}) \cdot \vec{a} + (c\vec{y}) \cdot \vec{a} \\
> &= b(\vec{x} \cdot \vec{a}) + c(\vec{y} \cdot \vec{a}) \\
> &= bH(\vec{x}) + cH(\vec{y})
> \end{aligned}$$
> 
> Therefore $H$ is **linear**.
> 
> **Prediction:** Linear, since $\vec{a}$ is a fixed matrix (can be viewed as a $1 \times n$ matrix).
> 
> ### Part (c): Not Linear
> 
> Let $\vec{x}, \vec{y} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ be arbitrary and choose $b = c = 0$. Then the linearity test says that $L(\vec{0}) = \vec{0}$.
> 
> However in our case:
> $$L\left(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \neq \vec{0}$$
> 
> So $F$ is **not linear**.
> 
> **Prediction:** Not linear because it has to keep the grid center at the origin (constant term of $1$ shifts everything).

> [!info] Info
> ## Zero Vector Property

Note that in examples (a) and (b), the <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">zero vector</strong></u> $\vec{0}$ does indeed map to the zero vector $\vec{0}$, yet one is linear and one is not. This shows that $L(\vec{0}) = \vec{0}$ is a <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">necessary condition</strong></u> of linearity, but it is **not sufficient**.

> [!fact] Theorem
> ## Zero Vector Mapping

Given $L: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$, if $L$ is linear, then $L(\vec{0}) = \vec{0}$.

**Warning:** As previously mentioned, $L(\vec{0}) = \vec{0}$ does **not** guarantee linearity.

> [!fact] Theorem  
> ## Matrix Mappings Are Linear

All matrix mappings are linear. That is, if $L: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ where $A \in M_{m \times n}$ and $L(\vec{x}) = A\vec{x}$, then $L$ is <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">linear</strong></u>.

**Proof:** Exercise. Just use properties of matrix multiplication.

> [!fact] Theorem
> ## Linear Mappings Are Matrix Mappings

All linear mappings are matrix mappings. That is, if $L: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">linear</strong></u>, then we can find a matrix $A$ such that $L(\vec{x}) = A\vec{x}$.

> [!fact]- Proof (Click to expand)
> 
> Let $L: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be linear. Since the domain of $L$ is $\mathbb{R}^n$, let $\vec{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ \vdots \\ x_n \end{bmatrix}$ and use the <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">standard basis</strong></u> to write it as:
> 
> $$\vec{x} = x_1\vec{e}_1 + x_2\vec{e}_2 + \cdots + x_n\vec{e}_n$$
> 
> Now:
> $$\begin{aligned}
> L(\vec{x}) &= L(x_1\vec{e}_1 + x_2\vec{e}_2 + \cdots + x_n\vec{e}_n) \\
> &= x_1L(\vec{e}_1) + x_2L(\vec{e}_2) + \cdots + x_nL(\vec{e}_n) \\
> &= \begin{bmatrix} L(\vec{e}_1) & L(\vec{e}_2) & \cdots & L(\vec{e}_n) \end{bmatrix} \vec{x}
> \end{aligned}$$
> 
> **Note:** In other words, we can encode any linear transformation by tracking where the basis vectors land.
> 
> So if we let $A = \begin{bmatrix} L(\vec{e}_1) & L(\vec{e}_2) & \cdots & L(\vec{e}_n) \end{bmatrix}$, we have that:
> 
> $$A\vec{x} = L(\vec{x})$$

> [!quote] Definition
> ## Standard Matrix

Given a linear map $L: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$, the matrix

$$[L] = \begin{bmatrix} L(\vec{e}_1) & L(\vec{e}_2) & \cdots & L(\vec{e}_n) \end{bmatrix}$$

is called the <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">standard matrix of $L$</strong></u> and satisfies $L(\vec{x}) = [L]\vec{x}$.

> [!example] Example
> ## Finding the Standard Matrix

Consider the transformation $L: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2$, $L\left(\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}$.

**a)** Show that $L$ is linear.

**b)** Find the standard matrix $[L]$.

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> 
> ### Part (a): Proving Linearity
> 
> Let $\vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix}$, $\vec{y} = \begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \\ y_3 \end{bmatrix}$.
> 
> $$L(c\vec{x} + d\vec{y}) = L\left(\begin{bmatrix} cx_1 + dy_1 \\ cx_2 + dy_2 \\ cx_3 + dy_3 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} cx_1 + dy_1 \\ cx_2 + dy_2 \end{bmatrix} \quad (1)$$
> 
> While:
> $$\begin{aligned}
> cL(\vec{x}) + dL(\vec{y}) &= c\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} + d\begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \end{bmatrix} \\
> &= \begin{bmatrix} cx_1 + dy_1 \\ cx_2 + dy_2 \end{bmatrix} \quad (2)
> \end{aligned}$$
> 
> Since $(1) = (2)$, we can conclude that $L$ is **linear**.
> 
> ### Part (b): Finding the Standard Matrix
> 
> $$[L] = \begin{bmatrix} L(\vec{e}_1) & L(\vec{e}_2) & L(\vec{e}_3) \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> $$L(\vec{e}_1) = L\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> $$L(\vec{e}_2) = L\left(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> $$L(\vec{e}_3) = L\left(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> Therefore:
> $$[L] = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> **Check:**
> $$[L]\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 + 0x_2 + 0x_3 \\ 0x_1 + x_2 + 0x_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} \quad \checkmark$$
