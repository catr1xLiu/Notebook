> [!quote] Definition
> ## Projections

Recalling the definitions of $\text{proj}_{\vec{d}}\vec{x}$ and $\text{perp}_{\vec{d}}\vec{x}$, let us define the <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">transformations</strong></u>:

$$P(\vec{x}) = \text{proj}_{\vec{d}}\vec{x}$$

$$N(\vec{x}) = \text{perp}_{\vec{d}}\vec{x}$$

for some non-zero vector $\vec{d}$.

> [!info] Info
> ## Linearity of Projections

**Exercise:** Show that $P$ and $N$ are <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">linear</strong></u>.

> [!success]- Proof (Click to expand)
> 
> Let $\vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}$, $\vec{y} = \begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \end{bmatrix}$. We have to show $P(a\vec{x} + b\vec{y}) = aP(\vec{x}) + bP(\vec{y})$.
> 
> $$\begin{aligned}
> P(c\vec{x} + d\vec{y}) &= \text{proj}_{\vec{d}}(c\vec{x} + d\vec{y}) \\
> &= \frac{(c\vec{x} + d\vec{y}) \cdot \vec{d}}{\|\vec{d}\|^2}\vec{d} \\
> &= \frac{c(\vec{x} \cdot \vec{d}) + d(\vec{y} \cdot \vec{d})}{\|\vec{d}\|^2}\vec{d} \\
> &= \frac{c(\vec{x} \cdot \vec{d})}{\|\vec{d}\|^2}\vec{d} + \frac{d(\vec{y} \cdot \vec{d})}{\|\vec{d}\|^2}\vec{d} \\
> &= c \cdot \text{proj}_{\vec{d}}\vec{x} + d \cdot \text{proj}_{\vec{d}}\vec{y} \\
> &= cP(\vec{x}) + dP(\vec{y})
> \end{aligned}$$
> 
> Since $(1) = (2)$, we can conclude that $P$ is **linear**.
> 
> For $N(\vec{x}) = \vec{x} - P(\vec{x})$: Since $P$ is linear, $N$ must also be linear.

> [!fact] Theorem
> ## Standard Matrix for Projection

For a generic non-zero vector $\vec{d} = \begin{bmatrix} d_1 \\ d_2 \end{bmatrix}$, the standard matrix for the projection transformation $P(\vec{x}) = \text{proj}_{\vec{d}}\vec{x}$ is:

$$[P] = \frac{1}{\|\vec{d}\|^2}\begin{bmatrix} d_1^2 & d_1d_2 \\ d_1d_2 & d_2^2 \end{bmatrix}$$

> [!success]- Derivation (Click to expand)
> 
> Recall that $P(\vec{x}) = \text{proj}_{\vec{d}}\vec{x} = \frac{\vec{x} \cdot \vec{d}}{\|\vec{d}\|^2}\vec{d}$. To find $[P]$ we need to calculate $P(\vec{e}_1)$ and $P(\vec{e}_2)$.
> 
> $$P(\vec{e}_1) = \left(\frac{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} d_1 \\ d_2 \end{bmatrix}}{\|\vec{d}\|^2}\right)\begin{bmatrix} d_1 \\ d_2 \end{bmatrix} = \left(\frac{d_1}{\|\vec{d}\|^2}\right)\begin{bmatrix} d_1 \\ d_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} d_1^2/\|\vec{d}\|^2 \\ d_1d_2/\|\vec{d}\|^2 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> $$P(\vec{e}_2) = \left(\frac{d_2}{\|\vec{d}\|^2}\right)\begin{bmatrix} d_1 \\ d_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} d_1d_2/\|\vec{d}\|^2 \\ d_2^2/\|\vec{d}\|^2 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> Thus:
> $$[P] = \frac{1}{\|\vec{d}\|^2}\begin{bmatrix} d_1^2 & d_1d_2 \\ d_1d_2 & d_2^2 \end{bmatrix}$$

> [!example] Example
> ## Computing a Projection

Use the projection matrix to calculate the projection of $\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$ onto $\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$.

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> 
> If $\vec{d} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$, then $\|\vec{d}\|^2 = 25$ and:
> $$[P] = \frac{1}{25}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 25 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> Then if $\vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$, we get:
> $$P(\vec{x}) = [P]\vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> as expected. $\checkmark$

A similar (but uglier) computation can be used to find $[N]$.

> [!quote] Definition
> ## Reflections

**Reflection Across a Line:**

Given a line $L$ through the origin with direction vector $\vec{d}$, we can <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">reflect</strong></u> $\vec{x}$ across this line using:

$$R_{\vec{d}}(\vec{x}) = \vec{x} - 2\text{perp}_{\vec{d}}\vec{x} = \vec{x} - 2(\vec{x} - \text{proj}_{\vec{d}}\vec{x}) = 2\text{proj}_{\vec{d}}\vec{x} - \vec{x}$$

**Reflection Across a Plane:**

Similarly, given a plane $P$ through the origin with normal vector $\vec{n}$, we can reflect $\vec{x}$ across this plane using:

$$R_P(\vec{x}) = \vec{x} - 2\text{proj}_{\vec{n}}\vec{x}$$

[Insert graph: 3D diagram showing vector $\vec{x}$, plane $P$ with normal $\vec{n}$, projection onto $\vec{n}$, and reflection $R_P(\vec{x})$]

Both of these can be shown to be <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">linear</strong></u> and thus have a <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">standard matrix</strong></u>.

> [!example] Example
> ## Reflection Matrix

Find the standard matrix for reflecting across the line $y = x$.

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> 
> The line $y = x$ has direction vector $\vec{d} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Using $R_{\vec{d}}(\vec{x}) = 2\text{proj}_{\vec{d}}\vec{x} - \vec{x}$ with $\vec{e}_1$ and $\vec{e}_2$ yields:
> 
> $$R_{\vec{d}}(\vec{e}_1) = 2\text{proj}_{\vec{d}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = 2\left(\frac{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}}{2}\right)\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> Similarly:
> $$R_{\vec{d}}(\vec{e}_2) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> Therefore:
> $$[R_{\vec{d}}] = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

> [!quote] Definition
> ## Rotations

Imagine rotating a vector of length $r$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ **counterclockwise (CCW)** by an amount $\theta$. That is, given a point $(x, y)$, we want to determine the new point $(x', y')$ that results after the rotation.

[Insert graph: 2D coordinate system showing rotation of point $(x,y)$ by angle $\theta$ to $(x',y')$, with angle $\alpha$ from x-axis to original point]

> [!fact] Theorem
> ## Rotation Matrix

The standard matrix for rotating vectors counterclockwise by angle $\theta$ is:

$$[R_{\theta}] = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$$

> [!success]- Derivation (Click to expand)
> 
> Let $\alpha$ be the initial angle made by $\vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}$ with the positive x-axis. Using trigonometry, we see that:
> 
> $$x = r\cos\alpha, \quad y = r\sin\alpha$$
> 
> $$x' = r\cos(\alpha + \theta), \quad y' = r\sin(\alpha + \theta)$$
> 
> Using the sum formulas for sine and cosine, we get:
> 
> $$x' = r\cos\alpha\cos\theta - r\sin\alpha\sin\theta = x\cos\theta - y\sin\theta$$
> 
> $$y' = r\sin\alpha\cos\theta + r\cos\alpha\sin\theta = y\cos\theta + x\sin\theta$$
> 
> Or if we define $R_{\theta}$ as the mapping that yields $\begin{bmatrix} x' \\ y' \end{bmatrix}$ from a given $\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}$, then:
> 
> $$R_{\theta}\left(\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} x\cos\theta - y\sin\theta \\ x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> Therefore:
> $$[R_{\theta}] = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$$

This transformation is of course <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">linear</strong></u> since it's already a <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">matrix transformation</strong></u>.

> [!info] Info
> ## Common Transformations in $\mathbb{R}^2$

Below is a list of common transformations in $\mathbb{R}^2$ (often used in computer graphics). Assume $\vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}$.

| **Name** | **Mapping** | **Standard Matrix** | **Notes** |
|----------|-------------|---------------------|-----------|
| **Horizontal Stretch/Compression** | $T(\vec{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} hx_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}$ | $\begin{bmatrix} h & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ | $h > 1$ (stretch)<br>$0 < h < 1$ (compression) |
| **Vertical Stretch/Compression** | $T(\vec{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ bx_2 \end{bmatrix}$ | $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & b \end{bmatrix}$ | $b > 1$ (stretch)<br>$0 < b < 1$ (compression) |
| **Dilation/Contraction** | $T(\vec{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} mx_1 \\ mx_2 \end{bmatrix}$ | $\begin{bmatrix} m & 0 \\ 0 & m \end{bmatrix}$ | $m > 1$ (dilation)<br>$0 < m < 1$ (contraction) |
| **Horizontal Shear** | $T(\vec{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 + sx_2 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}$ | $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & s \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ | $s > 0$ |
| **Vertical Shear** | $T(\vec{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 + sx_1 \end{bmatrix}$ | $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ s & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ | $s > 0$ |
| **Rotation (CCW by $\theta$)** | $R_{\theta}(\vec{x})$ | $\begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$ | Counterclockwise |

All of these are of course <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">linear</strong></u>.
