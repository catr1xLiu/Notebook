> [!info] Info
> ## Setting Up the Problem

The <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">eigenpair</strong></u> $(\lambda, \vec{x})$ is, by definition, assumed to satisfy:

$$A\vec{x} = \lambda\vec{x}$$

Note that this is equivalent to:

$$A\vec{x} - \lambda\vec{x} = \vec{0}$$
$$(A - \lambda I)\vec{x} = \vec{0}$$

That is, we are looking for the <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">non-trivial</strong></u> vectors in the <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">null space</strong></u> of the matrix $A - \lambda I$. But how do we find these without first knowing $\lambda$?

> [!fact] Theorem
> ## Key Insight: Non-Invertibility Condition

The trick is to remember that in order for the equation $B\vec{x} = \vec{0}$ to have <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">non-trivial solutions</strong></u>, we require that the matrix $B$ be <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">non-invertible</strong></u>.

(Recall: if $B$ was <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">invertible</strong></u>, then the <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">inverse</strong></u> would exist and we could solve $B\vec{x} = \vec{0}$ by multiplying both sides by $B^{-1}$, giving only the trivial solution $\vec{x} = \vec{0}$.)

Then we recall that if an $n \times n$ matrix $B$ is non-invertible, this means it satisfies $\det(B) = 0$ (i.e., $\text{rank}(B) < n$).

> [!quote] Definition
> ## Characteristic Polynomial

Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix. The <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">characteristic polynomial</strong></u> of $A$ is given by:

$$C_A(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I)$$

---

> [!info] Info
> ## Procedure for Finding Eigenpairs

Thus, to solve $(A - \lambda I)\vec{x} = \vec{0}$:

1. First <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">force</strong></u> $A - \lambda I$ to be non-invertible by requiring $C_A(\lambda) = 0$
2. Then for each value of $\lambda$ we obtain, calculate $\vec{x}$ (i.e., find the <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">null space</strong></u>)

> [!example] Example
> ## Finding Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors of a 2×2 Matrix

Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 1 \\ 1 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$. Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $A$.

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> 
> **Step 1: Find the eigenvalues** by setting $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$.
> 
> We have:
> $$A - \lambda I = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 1 \\ 1 & 5 \end{bmatrix} - \lambda\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5-\lambda & 1 \\ 1 & 5-\lambda \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> And so:
> $$\det(A - \lambda I) = (5-\lambda)(5-\lambda) - 1 = (5-\lambda)^2 - 1$$
> $$= (5-\lambda+1)(5-\lambda-1) = (6-\lambda)(4-\lambda)$$
> 
> To force non-invertibility, we set this to $0$:
> $$(6-\lambda)(4-\lambda) = 0$$
> 
> So that $\lambda = 6$ or $\lambda = 4$ are the eigenvalues.
> 
> ---
> 
> **Step 2: Find the eigenvectors** by using each eigenvalue in turn and solving for $\vec{x}$.
> 
> **Case $\lambda = 4$:**
> 
> We want to solve $A\vec{x} = \lambda\vec{x}$, or equivalently $(A - 4I)\vec{x} = \vec{0}$:
> 
> $$\begin{bmatrix} 5-4 & 1 \\ 1 & 5-4 \end{bmatrix}\vec{x} = \vec{0} \quad \xrightarrow{\text{row reduce}} \quad \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\vec{x} = \vec{0}$$
> 
> Let $x_2 = t$, then $x_1 = -t$, yielding:
> $$\vec{x} = t\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad t \in \mathbb{R}$$
> 
> **Case $\lambda = 6$:**
> 
> Again, starting with $(A - 6I)\vec{x} = \vec{0}$:
> 
> $$\begin{bmatrix} 5-6 & 1 \\ 1 & 5-6 \end{bmatrix}\vec{x} = \vec{0} \quad \xrightarrow{\text{row reduce}} \quad \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\vec{x} = \vec{0}$$
> 
> Let $x_2 = t$, then $x_1 = t$, yielding:
> $$\vec{x} = t\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad t \in \mathbb{R}$$
> 
> ---
> 
> **Summary:** The eigenpairs are:
> $$\lambda = 4, \quad \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \text{ (or any scalar multiple)}$$
> $$\lambda = 6, \quad \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \text{ (or any scalar multiple)}$$

---

> [!quote] Definition
> ## Eigenspace

Note that each eigenvalue generates a <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">new</strong></u> matrix for which we are trying to find its <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">null space</strong></u>. We define the following:

If $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$, we call:

$$E_\lambda(A) = \text{Null}(A - \lambda I)$$

the <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">eigenspace</strong></u> of $A$ corresponding to $\lambda$. This is the set of all eigenvectors associated with $\lambda$, together with the zero vector.

> [!hint] Hint
> ## Note on the Zero Vector

$E_\lambda(A)$ includes $\vec{0}$ by definition of null space, but we still don't allow $\vec{0}$ as an <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">eigenvector</strong></u>.

> [!example] Example
> ## Eigenspaces from the Previous Example

From the previous example:

- The eigenspace of $\lambda = 4$ is $E_4(A) = \text{span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$, and a basis for $E_4(A)$ is $\left\{\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$.

- The eigenspace of $\lambda = 6$ is $E_6(A) = \text{span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$, and a basis for $E_6(A)$ is $\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$.

> [!example] Example
> ## Finding Eigenspace Dimensions of a 3×3 Matrix

Find the dimension of each eigenspace of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$.

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> 
> We need to find each <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">eigenvalue</strong></u> and then a <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">basis</strong></u> for each eigenspace.
> 
> **Step 1:** First construct $A - \lambda I$:
> 
> $$A - \lambda I = \begin{bmatrix} 1-\lambda & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1-\lambda & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2-\lambda \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> **Step 2:** Next we need the <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">determinant</strong></u> of this. Because the matrix is <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">triangular</strong></u>, we have:
> 
> $$C_A(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I) = (1-\lambda)(1-\lambda)(2-\lambda)$$
> 
> So the roots of $C_A(\lambda)$ are $\lambda = 1$ (double root) or $\lambda = 2$ (single root).
> 
> **Step 3:** Solve $(A - \lambda I)\vec{x} = \vec{0}$ for each value of $\lambda$.
> 
> ---
> 
> **Case $\lambda = 1$:**
> 
> $$\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\vec{x} = \vec{0} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \vec{x} = s\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + t\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> Thus a basis for $E_1(A)$ is $\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$, and so $\dim(E_1(A)) = 2$.
> 
> ---
> 
> **Case $\lambda = 2$:**
> 
> $$\begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\vec{x} = \vec{0} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \vec{x} = t\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> Thus a basis for $E_2(A)$ is $\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$, and so $\dim(E_2(A)) = 1$.
