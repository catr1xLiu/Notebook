> [!quote] Definition
> ## Subspace

A subset $U$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is called a <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">subspace</u></strong> of $\mathbb{R}^n$ if:

1. $\vec{0} \in U$ (non-empty, contains the zero vector)
2. $\vec{x}, \vec{y} \in U \Rightarrow \vec{x} + \vec{y} \in U$ (closed under addition)
3. $\vec{x} \in U \Rightarrow c\vec{x} \in U$ for any $c \in \mathbb{R}$ (closed under scalar multiplication)

> [!hint] Hint
> ## Geometric Interpretation

Graphically, a subspace is just a <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">portion</u></strong> of the larger space $\mathbb{R}^n$ that "passes through the origin."

Note that $\mathbb{R}^n$ is itself a subspace. However, **not all** portions of $\mathbb{R}^n$ will be subspaces (see examples below).

**Some examples:**

- A <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">line</u></strong> through the origin: $\vec{x} = t \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, t \in \mathbb{R}$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^2$

- The <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">xy-plane</u></strong>: $\vec{x} = t\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + s\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, t,s \in \mathbb{R}$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$

- A <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">plane</u></strong> through the origin: $\vec{x} = t\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + s\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, t,s \in \mathbb{R}$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$

> [!example] Example
> ## Non-Subspaces (Counterexamples)

**Example 1:** The parabola $\vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} t \\ t^2 \end{bmatrix}$ is **NOT** a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^2$.

**Why?** Let $U = \left\{\vec{x} \mid \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} t \\ t^2 \end{bmatrix}, t \in \mathbb{R}\right\}$ and choose $\vec{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$.

Then $\vec{u} + \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$, but $\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix}$ would be needed for it to lie on the parabola. Since $5 \neq 9$, we have $\vec{u} + \vec{v} \notin U$, violating closure under addition.

**Example 2:** The line $\vec{x} = s\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, s \in \mathbb{R}$ is **NOT** a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^2$.

**Why?** This line does not pass through the origin: when $s = 0$, we get $\vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \neq \vec{0}$. Therefore $\vec{0} \notin U$.

> [!example] Example
> ## Verifying a Subspace

Determine whether $U = \left\{\begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \\ y_3 \end{bmatrix} \mid y_1 + y_2 \geq 0\right\}$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
>
> **Step 1:** Check if $\vec{0} \in U$
>
> Since $0 + 0 = 0 \geq 0$, we have $\vec{0} \in U$. ✓
>
> **Step 2:** Check closure under addition
>
> Let $\vec{u}, \vec{v} \in U$. Then $\vec{u} + \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 + v_1 \\ u_2 + v_2 \\ u_3 + v_3 \end{bmatrix}$.
>
> Is $\vec{u} + \vec{v} \in U$? We need $(u_1 + v_1) + (u_2 + v_2) \geq 0$.
>
> Since $\vec{u} \in U$, we know $u_1 + u_2 \geq 0$. Similarly, since $\vec{v} \in U$, we know $v_1 + v_2 \geq 0$.
>
> Thus: $(u_1 + v_1) + (u_2 + v_2) = (u_1 + u_2) + (v_1 + v_2) \geq 0$. ✓
>
> Therefore $\vec{u} + \vec{v} \in U$.
>
> **Step 3:** Check closure under scalar multiplication
>
> For any $c \in \mathbb{R}$ and $\vec{v} \in U$, do we have $c\vec{v} \in U$?
>
> Consider $\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$. We know this belongs to $U$ since $1 + 1 = 2 \geq 0$.
>
> However, choose $c = -1$ to get $c\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$, and $(-1) + (-1) = -2 \not\geq 0$.
>
> Therefore $c\vec{v} \notin U$, violating closure under scalar multiplication. ✗
>
> **Conclusion:** $U$ is **NOT** a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

> [!fact] Theorem
> ## Every Span is a Subspace

In $\mathbb{R}^n$, all spans are subspaces.

That is, if $U = \text{Span}\left(\{\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2, \ldots, \vec{v}_k\}\right)$ where each $\vec{v}_i \in \mathbb{R}^n$, then $U$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$.

> [!success]- Proof (Click to expand)
>
> Let $\vec{x} \in U$ and $\vec{y} \in U$. We need to show:
> 1. $\vec{0} \in U$
> 2. $\vec{x} + \vec{y} \in U$
> 3. $c\vec{x} \in U$ for any $c \in \mathbb{R}$
>
> **Part 1:** By definition of span, choosing all coefficients to be $0$ gives $\vec{0} = 0\vec{v}_1 + 0\vec{v}_2 + \cdots + 0\vec{v}_k \in U$. ✓
>
> **Part 2:** By definition of span, there exist $c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_k \in \mathbb{R}$ such that:
> $$\vec{x} = c_1\vec{v}_1 + c_2\vec{v}_2 + \cdots + c_k\vec{v}_k$$
>
> Similarly, there exist $d_1, d_2, \ldots, d_k \in \mathbb{R}$ such that:
> $$\vec{y} = d_1\vec{v}_1 + d_2\vec{v}_2 + \cdots + d_k\vec{v}_k$$
>
> Therefore:
> $$\vec{x} + \vec{y} = (c_1 + d_1)\vec{v}_1 + (c_2 + d_2)\vec{v}_2 + \cdots + (c_k + d_k)\vec{v}_k$$
>
> This shows $\vec{x} + \vec{y}$ matches the form required for membership in $U$, thus $\vec{x} + \vec{y} \in U$. ✓
>
> **Part 3:** For any $a \in \mathbb{R}$:
> $$a\vec{x} = a(c_1\vec{v}_1 + c_2\vec{v}_2 + \cdots + c_k\vec{v}_k) = (ac_1)\vec{v}_1 + (ac_2)\vec{v}_2 + \cdots + (ac_k)\vec{v}_k$$
>
> This matches the form for $U$, thus $a\vec{x} \in U$. ✓
>
> **Conclusion:** $U$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$.

> [!fact] Theorem
> ## Every Subspace is a Span

The converse is also true: All subspaces have <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">spanning sets</u></strong>.

That is, given a subspace $U$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$, we can find a set $B = \{\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2, \ldots, \vec{v}_k\}$ such that:
$$U = \text{Span}(B)$$
This theorem tells us that **subspaces and spans are equivalent concepts** — every span is a subspace, and every subspace can be expressed as a span.
