> [!quote] Definition
> ## Triangular Matrices

A square matrix $A$ is called <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">upper triangular</strong></u> if $a_{ij} = 0$ whenever $i > j$.

A square matrix $A$ is called <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">lower triangular</strong></u> if $a_{ij} = 0$ whenever $i < j$.  

We say $A$ is <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">diagonal</strong></u> if $a_{ij} = 0$ whenever $i \neq j$.

> [!fact] Theorem
> ## Determinant of Triangular Matrices

If $A$ is triangular (upper, lower, or diagonal), then:
$$\det(A) = a_{11} \cdot a_{22} \cdot \cdots \cdot a_{nn}$$

The determinant is simply the product of the diagonal entries.

> [!info] Info
> ## EROs and Determinants

Let $A$ be a square matrix and $A^*$ be the result after applying an ERO to $A$. The ERO affects the determinant as follows:

1. **Multiply a row by $c$**: $\det(A^*) = c \cdot \det(A)$
2. **Swap two rows**: $\det(A^*) = -\det(A)$  
3. **Add a multiple of one row to another**: $\det(A^*) = \det(A)$

**Note**: When finding the original determinant, we use the inverse relationships:
- For (i): $\det(A) = \frac{1}{c} \det(A^*)$
- For (ii): $\det(A) = -\det(A^*)$
- For (iii): $\det(A) = \det(A^*)$

> [!example] Example
> ## Finding Determinant Using EROs

Find the determinant of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 & 4 \\ 1 & 1 & 3 \\ 6 & 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Apply EROs to reduce to REF:
> 
> $$\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 & 4 \\ 1 & 1 & 3 \\ 6 & 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_1 \leftrightarrow R_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 3 \\ 4 & 1 & 4 \\ 6 & 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$$
> Determinant changes: $(-1)$
> 
> $$\xrightarrow{R_2 - 4R_1, R_3 - 6R_1} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & -3 & -8 \\ 0 & -5 & -20 \end{bmatrix}$$
> No change
> 
> $$\xrightarrow{-\frac{1}{5}R_3} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & -3 & -8 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$$
> Determinant changes: $(-5)$
> 
> $$\xrightarrow{R_2 \leftrightarrow R_3} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 \\ 0 & -3 & -8 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_3 + 3R_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$$
> Determinant changes: $(-1)$, then no change
> 
> Final determinant of REF = $1 \cdot 1 \cdot 4 = 4$
> 
> Therefore: $\det(A) = (-1)(-5)(-1)(4) = -20$

> [!fact] Theorem
> ## Determinant of Matrix Products

For $n \times n$ matrices $A$ and $B$:
$$\det(AB) = \det(A) \cdot \det(B)$$

This multiplicative property is one of the most fundamental properties of determinants.

> [!hint] Hint
> ## Warning About Addition

In general, $\det(A + B) \neq \det(A) + \det(B)$. The determinant does NOT distribute over matrix addition!

> [!fact] Theorem  
> ## Properties of Determinants

#### <u>Invertibility and Determinants</u>
If $A$ is invertible, then:
$$\det(A^{-1}) = \frac{1}{\det(A)}$$

**Proof**: Since $AA^{-1} = I$, we have:
$$\det(AA^{-1}) = \det(I) = 1$$
$$\det(A) \cdot \det(A^{-1}) = 1$$
$$\det(A^{-1}) = \frac{1}{\det(A)}$$

#### <u>Powers of Matrices</u>
For any integer $k$ (positive, negative, or zero):
$$\det(A^k) = [\det(A)]^k$$

This holds for:
- Positive $k$: by repeated application of the product rule
- Negative $k$: using the inverse property above
- $k = 0$: since $A^0 = I$ and $\det(I) = 1$

#### <u>Scalar Multiples</u>
For an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ and scalar $c$:
$$\det(cA) = c^n \cdot \det(A)$$

**Note**: The power of $c$ equals the dimension of the matrix!

#### <u>Transpose</u>
$$\det(A^T) = \det(A)$$

> [!info] Info
> ## Identical Rows or Columns

If matrix $A$ has two identical rows, then $\det(A) = 0$.

**Proof**: If rows $i$ and $j$ are identical, swap them. This gives:
- $\det(A^*) = -\det(A)$ (due to the swap)
- But $A^* = A$ (since the rows are identical)
- Thus $\det(A) = -\det(A)$, which implies $\det(A) = 0$

The same property holds for identical columns.

> [!hint] Hint
> ## Column Operations

All the ERO results also apply to column operations:
- Swapping columns changes the sign of the determinant
- Multiplying a column by $c$ multiplies the determinant by $c$
- Adding a multiple of one column to another doesn't change the determinant

However, be careful: column operations can affect the solution set of linear systems!

> [!example] Example
> ## Product of Matrices

Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ -1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -2 \\ 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. 

Verify that $\det(AB) = \det(A) \cdot \det(B)$.

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> First, compute the products:
> $$AB = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & -3 \\ 7 & 5 \end{bmatrix}, \quad BA = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & -4 \\ 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> Now compute determinants:
> - $\det(A) = 2(3) - 1(-1) = 7$
> - $\det(B) = 2(1) - (-2)(3) = 8$  
> - $\det(AB) = 7(5) - (-3)(7) = 35 + 21 = 56$
> - $\det(BA) = 6(6) - (-4)(5) = 36 + 20 = 56$
> 
> Indeed: $\det(AB) = \det(BA) = 56 = 7 \times 8 = \det(A) \cdot \det(B)$ ✓

> [!fact] Theorem
> ## Invertibility of Products

If $AB$ is invertible, then both $A$ and $B$ must be individually invertible.

**Proof**: If $AB$ is invertible, then $\det(AB) \neq 0$.
Since $\det(AB) = \det(A) \cdot \det(B)$, we must have both $\det(A) \neq 0$ and $\det(B) \neq 0$.
Therefore, both $A$ and $B$ are invertible.

> [!info] Info
> ## Areas and Cross Products

#### <u>Cross Product in </u>$\mathbb{R}^3$
For vectors $\vec{u} = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 \\ u_2 \\ u_3 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ v_3 \end{bmatrix}$, the cross product is:

$$\vec{u} \times \vec{v} = \begin{vmatrix} \vec{i} & \vec{j} & \vec{k} \\ u_1 & u_2 & u_3 \\ v_1 & v_2 & v_3 \end{vmatrix} = \vec{i}\begin{vmatrix} u_2 & u_3 \\ v_2 & v_3 \end{vmatrix} - \vec{j}\begin{vmatrix} u_1 & u_3 \\ v_1 & v_3 \end{vmatrix} + \vec{k}\begin{vmatrix} u_1 & u_2 \\ v_1 & v_2 \end{vmatrix}$$

#### <u>Geometric Interpretation</u>
- **Magnitude**: $\|\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\| = \|\vec{u}\| \|\vec{v}\| \sin\theta$
- **Area of Parallelogram**: Generated by $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ is $\|\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\|$
- **Area of Triangle**: With sides $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ is $\frac{1}{2}\|\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\|$

> [!example] Example  
> ## Area of Triangle in 2D

Find the area of the triangle with vertices $A = (x_1, y_1)$, $B = (x_2, y_2)$, $C = (x_3, y_3)$.

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Form vectors from point $A$:
> - $\vec{u} = \vec{AB} = \begin{bmatrix} x_2 - x_1 \\ y_2 - y_1 \end{bmatrix}$
> - $\vec{v} = \vec{AC} = \begin{bmatrix} x_3 - x_1 \\ y_3 - y_1 \end{bmatrix}$
> 
> For 2D vectors (with $z = 0$), the cross product simplifies to:
> $$\|\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\| = |u_1v_2 - u_2v_1| = |(x_2 - x_1)(y_3 - y_1) - (y_2 - y_1)(x_3 - x_1)|$$
> 
> This can be rewritten using determinants:
> $$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}\left|\det\begin{bmatrix} x_1 & y_1 & 1 \\ x_2 & y_2 & 1 \\ x_3 & y_3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\right|$$
