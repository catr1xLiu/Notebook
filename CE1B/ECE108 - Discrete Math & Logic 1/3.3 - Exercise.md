
## Problem 1

> Given a function, prove or disprove it is a bijection. 
> $$f: (\mathbb{Z^+} \times \mathbb{Z^+}) \rightarrow \mathbb{Z^+} \quad f(x,y) = x + \frac{(x+y)(x+y+1)}{2}$$

We can rewrite the this expression as 
$$
f(x,y) = x + \sum_{1}^{x+y}i \quad (i\in\mathbb{Z^{+}})$$
Now, we observe its properties, let's write some of the function values.

```python
def f(x:int, y:int) -> int:
	return x + (x+y) * (x+y+1) // 2
for a in range(0, 5):
	for b in range(0, 5):
		print(f"f({a}, {b}) = {f(a,b)}", end="  ")
	print()
```

#### Step 1: Prove Injection

That is to prove that:

> $(x_1,y_1) \neq (x_2, y_2) \implies f(x_1, y_1) \neq f(x_2, y_2)$.

We assume, without loss in generality, that $(x_1, y_1)$ is the pair with smaller (or equal) sum:
$$
f(x_2, y_2) - f(x_1, y_1) = x_2-x_1 + \sum_{x_1+y_1}^{x_2+y_2}i
$$
We prove by case analysis: 
 - **Case 1: when $x_1+y_1 \leq x_2+y_2$.** $$\begin{aligned} \implies & \sum_{x_1+y_1}^{x_2+y_2}i \geq x_1+y_1 > x_1 > x_1-x_2 \\ \implies & f(x_2, y_2) - f(x_1, y_1) > 0 \\ \implies & f(x_1, y_1) \neq f(x_2, y_2)\end{aligned}$$
 - **Case 2: when $x_1 + y_1 = x_2+y_2$.**  $$\begin{aligned} & \implies \sum_{x_1+y_1}^{x_2+y_2}i = 0 \\ \text{Also, }\implies & x_2 \neq x_1 \implies x_2-x_1 \neq 0 \\ \implies & x_2 - x_1 + \sum_{x_1+y_1}^{x_2+y_2}i \neq 0 \\ \implies & f(x_1, y_1) \neq f(x_2, y_2) \end{aligned}$$

#### Step 2: Prove Surjection

That is to prove 

> $\forall y \in \mathbb{Z^+}$ there exists at least one $(a,b)$ exists that $f(a,b) = y$.

We prove by induction on $y$. Assuming that $\exists (a,b) \in (\mathbb{Z^+} \times \mathbb{Z^+}), \exists y \in \mathbb{Z^+}$ that $f(a,b) = y$. Thus, $$a + \sum_{1}^{a+b}i = y$$
For $b\neq0$, if we let $a' = a+1, b' = b-1$, compute:
$$f(a',b') = a' + \sum_{1}^{a'+b'}i = a+1 + \sum_{1}^{a+1+b-1}i = y+1$$
For $b = 0$, if we let $a'=0, b' = a+1$, compute: $$f(a',b') = a' + \sum_{1}^{a'+b'}i = 0 + \sum_{1}^{a+1}i = \sum_{1}^{a}+a+1 = y+1$$

## Problem 2

> Given $a, b \in \mathbb{N}$. Prove:
> $$a \neq b \iff \{(a-1)^2+1, (a-1)^2+2,...,a^2\} \cap \{(b-1)^2+1,(b-1)^2+2,...,b^2\} = \emptyset$$

First, we prove the forward $\implies$. We can write the RHS to:
$$([(a-1)^2+1, a^2] \cap \mathbb{N}) \cap ([(b-1)^2+1, b^2] \cap \mathbb{N}) = \emptyset \iff a^2 < (b-1)^2+1$$
We assume, without loss of generality, that $a$ is smaller one of $a,b$. Since $a < b$, $a, b \in \mathbb{N}$, we can show:
$$a \leq b-1 \implies a^2 \leq (b-1)^2 \implies a^2 < (b-1)^2+1$$
Now we prove the converse: 
$$\text{RHS} \implies a^2 \neq b^2 \implies a \neq b$$

## Problem 3

> Prove $(A \setminus B) \setminus C = A \setminus (B \cup C)$

That is to prove that $x \in ((A \setminus B) \setminus C) \iff x \in A \setminus (B \cup C)$.
We prove it step-by-step:
$$
\begin{aligned}
x \in ((A \setminus B) \setminus C) \\
\iff & x \in (A\setminus B) \land \lnot (x\in C) \\
\iff & (x \in A) \land \lnot(x\in B) \land \lnot(x \in C) \\
\iff & (x\in A) \land \lnot (x\in B \lor x \in C) \\
\iff & (x\in A) \land \lnot(x\in (B \cup C)) \\
\iff & x \in A \setminus (B\cup C)
\end{aligned}
$$
