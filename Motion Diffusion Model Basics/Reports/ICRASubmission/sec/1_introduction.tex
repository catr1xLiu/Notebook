The intersection of generative artificial intelligence and human biomechanics represents a critical frontier in modern robotics and human-robot interaction (HRI).
Over the past decade, deep generative architectures, including Diffusion Models and Spatial-Temporal Graph Convolutional Networks (ST-GCNs)~\cite{yan2018stgcn, tevet2023human, chen2022mld}, have demonstrated an unprecedented ability to synthesize complex human motion.
However, current motion capture datasets are composed predominantly of young, able-bodied actors in controlled environments~\cite{umagami2025intend}.
Consequently, state-of-the-art models suffer from a pervasive demographic bias: they inherently reflect the vigorous kinematics of youth, effectively marginalizing the biomechanical realities of aging, such as reduced joint flexibility and altered balance strategies.

Standard generative frameworks frequently neglect strict biomechanical constraints, leading to generated motions that violate physical laws and fail to generalize to pathological or elderly demographics~\cite{Adeli_2026_WACV, biomodiffuse2025}.
This poses a significant safety risk for assistive robots and intelligent systems designed to predict human motion and navigate dynamically around older populations. 

Concurrently, clinical biomechanics has transitioned from subjective visual assessments to objective, sensor-driven data science.
Leveraging machine learning algorithms, clinicians can now extract highly granular spatiotemporal metrics that quantify the physiological signature of an aging gait~\cite{diaz2025evaluating, galasso2024development}.
For example, studies utilize regression-based machine learning on wearable sensor data to measure the spatiotemporal Coefficient of Variation (CV) as an indicator of neuromuscular noise and stride instability~\cite{bailey2023smartwatch}.
Similarly, constraint-based synthetic data methodologies assess the Margin of Stability (MoS) and specific velocity-stability relationships to evaluate dynamic fall risk~\cite{cordeiro2025constraint}. 

For researchers developing age-aware generative motion models, these clinical techniques offer a vital benchmarking tool.
Bridging the gap between generative motion synthesis and clinical gait analysis requires moving beyond subjective visual fidelity.
Instead, synthesized motions must be evaluated against strict clinical thresholds---such as elevated stride CV, conservative MoS, and reduced Minimum Foot Clearance (MFC)~\cite{bailey2023smartwatch, cordeiro2025constraint, galasso2024development}---to mathematically verify that a model has successfully learned the physical constraints of aging.
