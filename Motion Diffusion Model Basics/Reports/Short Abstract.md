Aging alters human movement in ways that are clinically meaningful but visually subtle: walking speed and joint range of motion decline, and stride-to-stride variability increases; these changes are directly related to self-care ability and fall risk. Generative motion models offer a possible remedy to synthesize movement on demand, and graph convolutional networks can be used to analyze motion data from sensors; but most public motion demonstration data are dominated by young, healthy actors. We test whether these learning-based models can analyze and reproduce the biomechanical structure that a clinician would measure.

We converted a clinical gait dataset (138 adults, marker-based capture at 100 Hz) into a learning-ready skeletal representation, removing sensor noise using a multi-stage VPoser-regularized SMPL fitting pipeline designed in the hope of preserving biomechanical detail; this yields 420 walking clips of subjects of the entire lifespan (ages 21–86).  We then explore the two questions:

First, does the aging signal survive conversion? A Spatial–Temporal Graph Convolutional Network classifies clips into three age bands at 64.6% accuracy under strict subject-disjoint splits, with errors concentrated in the mid-age band and near-clean separation of young from elderly — the confusion clinical literature predicts. Per-clip regressions against chronological age reproduce the expected direction on every headline metric: walking speed falls (1.22→1.10 m/s), stride length falls (1.34→1.26 m), knee range of motion falls (54.9°→51.5°), and stride-time variability rises (CV 8.9%→15.0%).

Second, can a generative model reproduce those signatures? We adapted a pre-trained Motion Diffusion Model with a rank-12 low-rank adapter conditioned on discrete age tokens, then evaluated 9,000 generated clips with the same training-free clinical metrics. Spatial signatures transfer: elderly-conditioned clips show constrained hip range of motion (21.5° vs. 24–25°) and reduced peak joint speeds across the gait cycle. Temporal signatures do not: generated stride-time variability sits uniformly at 16–19% regardless of age condition, overshooting clinical reference values and erasing the very contrast that carries fall-risk information.

The practical message is that visual plausibility is a poor proxy for biomechanical fidelity. Synthetic older-adult movement produced this way can support work sensitive to posture and joint excursion, but should not yet substitute for real recordings in anything sensitive to gait timing or regularity. We identify dataset scale, conditioning strength, and temporal-axis model capacity as the binding constraints, and propose continuous age conditioning as the next step.
