> [!quote] Definition
> ## Special Linear Transformations

**The Zero Map:**

The map $L: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ given by $L(\vec{x}) = \vec{0}$ is called the <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">zero map</strong></u>.

**The Identity Map:**

The map $\text{Id}: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ given by $\text{Id}(\vec{x}) = \vec{x}$ is called the <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">identity map</strong></u>.

When it's not clear from context, we may write $\text{Id}_n$ to identify the dimension.

> [!fact] Theorem
> ## Properties of Zero and Identity Maps

Let $L$ be any linear transformation. Both the zero map and identity map are <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">linear</strong></u>. Furthermore:

$$[L_{\text{zero}}] = O_{m \times n} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & & \vdots \\ 0 & \cdots & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$[\text{Id}] = I = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & \cdots & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

> [!fact] Theorem
> ## Properties of Composition

Assume $S, L, R$ are linear maps with appropriate domains and codomains. Then:

1. $\text{Id} \circ L = L$ (left identity composition)
2. $L \circ \text{Id} = L$ (right identity composition)
3. $S \circ (L \circ R) = (S \circ L) \circ R$ (associative)
4. $S \circ (L + R) = S \circ L + S \circ R$ (left distributive)
5. $(L + R) \circ S = L \circ S + R \circ S$ (right distributive)
6. $(kS) \circ R = k(S \circ R) = S \circ (kR)$ for $k \in \mathbb{R}$

These all follow from properties of matrix multiplication.

> [!info] Info
> ## Non-Commutativity of Composition

Because composition of linear maps is linked to matrix multiplication, it is important to note that in general $L \circ R \neq R \circ L$. For one, the dimensions might not agree, but even if they did, recall $AB \neq BA$ for matrices.

> [!example] Example
> ## Rotation and Projection Don't Commute

For example, recall the rotation and projection maps:

$$R_{\theta}\left(\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} x\cos\theta - y\sin\theta \\ x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta \end{bmatrix}, \quad \text{proj}_{\vec{d}}\left(\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}\right) = \frac{\vec{x} \cdot \vec{d}}{\|\vec{d}\|^2}\vec{d}$$

Let $\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$ and $\vec{d} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> 
> Computing $(R_{\theta} \circ \text{proj}_{\vec{d}})\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right)$:
> 
> First apply projection: $\text{proj}_{\vec{d}}\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$
> 
> Then rotate: $R_{\pi/2}\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$
> 
> But $(\text{proj}_{\vec{d}} \circ R_{\theta})\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right)$:
> 
> First rotate: $R_{\pi/2}\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$
> 
> Then project: $\text{proj}_{\vec{d}}\left(\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$
> 
> In matrix terms we have:
> $$[R_{\theta}] = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad [\text{proj}_{\vec{d}}] = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> and we see that:
> $$[R_{\theta}][\text{proj}_{\vec{d}}] = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \neq \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = [\text{proj}_{\vec{d}}][R_{\theta}]$$

> [!quote] Definition
> ## Inverse Transformations

Let $L, M$ be linear mappings from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^n$. If

$$L \circ M = M \circ L = \text{Id}$$

then we say $L$ and $M$ are <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">invertible</strong></u> and that $M$ is an <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">inverse</strong></u> of $L$ (and $L$ is an inverse of $M$).

> [!fact] Theorem
> ## Uniqueness of Inverses

Inverse mappings are <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">unique</strong></u>.

> [!success]- Proof (Click to expand)
> 
> Let $L$ be invertible and assume both $M_1$ and $M_2$ are inverses. By definition we have:
> 
> $$M_1 = M_1 \circ \text{Id} = M_1 \circ (L \circ M_2) = (M_1 \circ L) \circ M_2 = \text{Id} \circ M_2 = M_2$$
> 
> Therefore the inverse is unique.

Because of this fact we usually write $L^{-1}$ as <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">the</strong></u> inverse of an invertible map $L$. We then have $L \circ L^{-1} = L^{-1} \circ L = \text{Id}$.

> [!example] Example
> ## Inverse Rotations

Show that in $\mathbb{R}^2$ a clockwise rotation of $\theta$ is the inverse of a counterclockwise rotation of $\theta$.

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> 
> This is of course obvious geometrically. Algebraically, let $L$ be the counterclockwise rotation and $T$ be the clockwise rotation.
> 
> Recall that:
> $$R_{\theta}\left(\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} x\cos\theta - y\sin\theta \\ x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> If we let $L = R_{\theta}$, then:
> $$[L] = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> Similarly if $T = R_{-\theta}$, then:
> $$[T] = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(-\theta) & -\sin(-\theta) \\ \sin(-\theta) & \cos(-\theta) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> Recall that for a composite map $M \circ L$ we have $[M \circ L] = [M][L]$, thus we have for any $\vec{x} \in \mathbb{R}^2$:
> 
> $$\begin{aligned}
> (L \circ T)\vec{x} &= [L \circ T]\vec{x} = [L][T]\vec{x} \\
> &= \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}\vec{x} \\
> &= \begin{bmatrix} \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta & 0 \\ 0 & \sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta \end{bmatrix}\vec{x} = I\vec{x} = \vec{x}
> \end{aligned}$$
> 
> Since $(L \circ T)\vec{x} = \vec{x}$, this means $L \circ T = \text{Id}$ and so $T = L^{-1}$.

> [!fact] Theorem
> ## Invertibility and Standard Matrices

Recall from [[3.5 - Matrix Inverse]] that Matrices can be invertible, and from [[5.1 - Linear Transformation]] that Linear Transformations can be written as Matrices.

Let $L: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ be linear. $L$ is invertible if and only if $[L]$ is invertible. Furthermore, $[L^{-1}] = [L]^{-1}$ and $L^{-1}$ is <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">linear</strong></u>.

> [!success]- Proof (Click to expand)
> 
> **($\Rightarrow$) If $L$ is invertible with inverse $M$, then $[L]$ is invertible:**
> 
> By definition $M$ satisfies $L \circ M = \text{Id}$. From this we get:
> $$[L][M] = [L \circ M] = [\text{Id}] = I$$
> 
> and so by definition of matrix inverse $[L]$ is invertible and we see that $[L]^{-1} = [M] = [L^{-1}]$.
> 
> To show $M$ is linear:
> $$L(M(b\vec{x} + c\vec{y})) = b\vec{x} + c\vec{y} \quad \text{since } L \circ M = \text{Id}$$
> 
> and
> $$L(bM(\vec{x}) + cM(\vec{y})) = bL(M(\vec{x})) + cL(M(\vec{y})) \quad \text{since } L \text{ is linear}$$
> $$= b\vec{x} + c\vec{y}$$
> 
> Thus $L(M(b\vec{x} + c\vec{y})) = L(bM(\vec{x}) + cM(\vec{y}))$. Apply $M$ to both sides to get that $M$ is linear.
> 
> **($\Leftarrow$) If $[L]$ is invertible, then $L$ is invertible:**
> 
> Now assume $[L]$ is invertible, i.e. $[L]^{-1}$ exists. We need to show that the map $L$ is invertible and $[L^{-1}] = [L]^{-1}$, i.e. we need to show that $M$ exists and its standard matrix is the inverse of the standard matrix $[L]$.
> 
> Define the map $M = [L]^{-1}$. That is, $M(\vec{x}) = [L]^{-1}\vec{x}$. For $L$ to be invertible we need $L \circ M = \text{Id}$. Consider $L \circ M$, we have:
> 
> $$(L \circ M)\vec{x} = L(M(\vec{x})) = L([L]^{-1}\vec{x}) = [L][L]^{-1}\vec{x} = \vec{x}$$
> 
> Thus $L \circ M = \text{Id}$. (Similar argument works for $M \circ L$) and so $L^{-1}$ exists and is given by $M$.
> 
> Finally $[L^{-1}] = [M]$ and since $M$ is a matrix mapping defined by $M(\vec{x}) = [L]^{-1}\vec{x}$, we have that $[M] = [L]^{-1}$. Thus $[L^{-1}] = [L]^{-1}$.

> [!example] Example
> ## Finding an Inverse Transformation

Let $L\left(\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 - x_2 \\ 2x_1 + x_2 \end{bmatrix}$, find $L^{-1}\left(\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}\right)$.

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> 
> Using the theorem we need to find $[L]$, then compute $[L]^{-1}$, and finally use the fact that $L^{-1}(\vec{x}) = [L]^{-1}\vec{x} = [L^{-1}]\vec{x}$.
> 
> $$[L] = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> Using inverse matrix algorithm we have:
> 
> $$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & | & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & | & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_2 - 2R_1} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & | & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & | & -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> $$\xrightarrow{R_2/3} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & | & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & | & -2/3 & 1/3 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_1 + R_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & | & 1/3 & 1/3 \\ 0 & 1 & | & -2/3 & 1/3 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> Thus $[L]^{-1} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = [L^{-1}]$ and we have:
> 
> $$L^{-1}\left(\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{x_1 + x_2}{3} \\ \frac{x_2 - 2x_1}{3} \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> e.g. $L\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$ and we see that $L^{-1}\left(\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$ $\checkmark$

> [!example] Example
> ## Projection is Not Invertible

Determine whether the projection map $\text{proj}_{\vec{d}}(\vec{x})$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is invertible.

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> 
> Recall for a given vector $\vec{d}$ by definition:
> $$\text{proj}_{\vec{d}}(\vec{x}) = \frac{\vec{x} \cdot \vec{d}}{\|\vec{d}\|^2}\vec{d}$$
> 
> We previously found that:
> $$[\text{proj}_{\vec{d}}] = \frac{1}{\|\vec{d}\|^2}\begin{bmatrix} d_1^2 & d_1d_2 \\ d_1d_2 & d_2^2 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> Based on the result from last class, the map $\text{proj}_{\vec{d}}$ is invertible if and only if $[\text{proj}_{\vec{d}}]$ is invertible.
> 
> Note that:
> $$\begin{bmatrix} d_1^2 & d_1d_2 \\ d_1d_2 & d_2^2 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{d_1R_2} \begin{bmatrix} d_1^2 & d_1d_2 \\ d_1^2d_2 & d_1d_2^2 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_2 - d_2R_1} \begin{bmatrix} d_1^2 & d_1d_2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> and so $\text{rank}([\text{proj}_{\vec{d}}]) = 1 < 2$, so $\text{proj}_{\vec{d}}$ is **not invertible**.

> [!fact] Theorem
> ## Invertible Maps Only Map Zero to Zero

If $L$ is invertible, linear, and $L(\vec{x}) = \vec{0}$, then $\vec{x} = \vec{0}$.

> [!success]- Proof (Click to expand)
> 
> We know $L(\vec{x}) = \vec{0}$ which means $[L]\vec{x} = \vec{0}$ (since linear).
> 
> Because $L$ is invertible so is $[L]$, thus $[L]^{-1}$ exists.
> 
> Applying this to both sides yields:
> $$[L]^{-1}[L]\vec{x} = [L]^{-1}\vec{0}$$
> $$I\vec{x} = \vec{0}$$
> $$\vec{x} = \vec{0}$$
