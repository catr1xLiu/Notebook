> [!info] Info
> ## Non-Invertible Maps Have Non-Trivial Null Space

It's important to note that for <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">non-invertible</strong></u> mappings, other vectors (besides $\vec{0}$) will map to $\vec{0}$.

For example the map $L\left(\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 + x_3 \\ x_2 - x_1 \\ x_2 + x_3 \end{bmatrix}$ satisfies $L\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \vec{0}$.

And if the map is from $\mathbb{R}^m$ with $m > n$, then the System Rank theorem guarantees non-trivial solutions to $[L]\vec{x} = \vec{0}$.

For example the map $L: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}$, $L\left(\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix}\right) = 3x_1 + 2x_2 - 3x_3$ satisfies:

$$L\left(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \vec{0} \quad \text{and} \quad L\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \vec{0}$$

among others.

> [!quote] Definition
> ## Kernel

Let $L: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be linear. The set of vectors $\vec{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ satisfying $L(\vec{x}) = \vec{0}$ is called the <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">kernel</strong></u> of $L$, denoted as $\text{Ker}(L)$.

> [!example] Example
> ## Finding the Kernel

If $\vec{d} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$, find $\text{Ker}(\text{proj}_{\vec{d}})$.

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> 
> We need to find all $\vec{x}$ such that $\text{proj}_{\vec{d}}(\vec{x}) = \vec{0}$. Thus we need to solve $[\text{proj}_{\vec{d}}]\vec{x} = \vec{0}$. Since $\vec{d} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ we have:
> 
> $$[\text{proj}_{\vec{d}}] = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{2R_1 - R_2} \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_1 \cdot 5/4} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1/2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> This means that the system $[\text{proj}_{\vec{d}}]\vec{x} = \vec{0}$ has **1 free variable**.
> 
> Let $x_2 = t$. Then from the first row we get $x_1 + \frac{t}{2} = 0 \Rightarrow x_1 = -\frac{t}{2}$.
> 
> Thus the solution is $\vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} -t/2 \\ t \end{bmatrix} = t\begin{bmatrix} -1/2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \, t \in \mathbb{R}$ or $\vec{x} = s\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \, s \in \mathbb{R}$.
> 
> That is, $\text{Ker}(\text{proj}_{\vec{d}}) = \text{span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$. This should make sense geometrically.
>

> [!quote] Definition
> ## Range

Let $L: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be linear. The set of all $\vec{y} \in \mathbb{R}^m$ for which there is an $\vec{x}$ such that $L(\vec{x}) = \vec{y}$ is called the <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">range</strong></u> of $L$, denoted as $\text{Range}(L)$.

> [!info] Info
> ## Computing Kernel and Range

Determining whether a given $\vec{b} \in \text{Range}(L)$ requires determining whether $L(\vec{x}) = \vec{b}$ has a solution, which reduces to determining whether $[L]\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ is consistent.

Furthermore, since both the kernel and range are determined by solving a system, their result will be a <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">span</strong></u> of vectors and thus a <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">subspace</strong></u>. We are thus often asked to find a <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">basis</strong></u> for each.

> [!fact] Theorem
> ## Kernel and Range as Matrix Spaces

Let $L: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be linear.

- $\text{Range}(L) = \text{Col}([L])$
- $\text{Ker}(L) = \text{Null}([L])$

**Proof idea:** Use definitions and convert to matrices.

> [!example] Example
> ## Kernel and Range of Plane Projection

Let $P$ be the $xz$-plane. Determine a basis for the kernel and range of the plane projection map $T_P(\vec{x}) = \vec{x} - \text{proj}_{\vec{n}}\vec{x}$ where $\vec{n}$ is normal to $P$.

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> 
> A normal to $P$ is $\vec{n} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$. To find $[T_P]$ we use $\vec{e}_1, \vec{e}_2, \vec{e}_3$:
> 
> $$T_P(\vec{e}_1) = \vec{e}_1 - \frac{\vec{e}_1 \cdot \vec{n}}{\|\vec{n}\|^2}\vec{n} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> (note: $\|\vec{n}\| = 1$)
> 
> $$T_P(\vec{e}_2) = \text{"with } \vec{e}_2\text{"} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> $$T_P(\vec{e}_3) = \ldots = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> Thus:
> $$[T_P] = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> **Finding the Kernel:**
> 
> $\text{Ker}(T_P) = \text{Null}([T_P])$ which means solving $[T_P]\vec{x} = \vec{0}$.
> 
> We identify **1** free variable: $x_2 = t$, and thus:
> $$\vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ t \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = t\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> **Finding the Range:**
> 
> $\text{Range}(T_P) = \text{Col}([T_P])$ which asks to find the span of the columns of $[T_P]$. We can clearly identify:
> 
> $$\text{Col}([T_P]) = \text{span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$$
> 
> Therefore:
> - A basis for $\text{Ker}(T_P)$ is $\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$
> - A basis for $\text{Range}(T_P)$ is $\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$
> 
> Does this make sense geometrically? Yes! The kernel is the $y$-axis (perpendicular to the $xz$-plane), and the range is the $xz$-plane itself.

> [!fact] Theorem
> ## The Rank-Nullity Theorem

Given a linear $L: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$:

$$\dim(\text{Range}(L)) + \dim(\text{Ker}(L)) = n$$

This is a restatement of the System Rank Theorem: $\text{rank}(A) + \text{nullity}(A) = n$ for any $m \times n$ matrix $A$.

> [!fact] Theorem
> ## Invertibility and Trivial Kernel

If $L: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ is invertible, then $\text{Ker}(L) = \{\vec{0}\}$.

**Proof:** This follows directly from our earlier theorem that invertible maps only map $\vec{0}$ to $\vec{0}$.
