> [!quote] Definition
> ## Algebraic Multiplicity

Given a matrix $A$ with characteristic polynomial $C_A(\lambda)$, the <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">algebraic multiplicity</u></strong> of an eigenvalue $\lambda_0$ is the number of times $(\lambda - \lambda_0)$ appears as a factor when $C_A(\lambda)$ is fully factored. In other words, it is the degree of each root. It is denoted by $a_\lambda$.

> [!quote] Definition
> ## Geometric Multiplicity

The <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">geometric multiplicity</u></strong> of an eigenvalue $\lambda$ is the dimension of the eigenspace $E_\lambda$ associated with that eigenvalue. It is denoted by $g_\lambda$.

In other words, $g_\lambda$ is the number of vectors in a basis for $E_\lambda$.

> [!example] Example
> ## Finding Algebraic and Geometric Multiplicities

The matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ has characteristic polynomial $C_A(\lambda) = (\lambda - 3)^2(\lambda - 1)$ and thus eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 3$ and $\lambda_2 = 1$.

**Algebraic multiplicities:**
$$a_{\lambda_1} = a_3 = 2 \quad \text{and} \quad a_{\lambda_2} = a_1 = 1$$

By solving $(A - \lambda I)\vec{x} = \vec{0}$ for each eigenvalue, we obtain bases $B_{\lambda_1}$ and $B_{\lambda_2}$ for the eigenspaces $E_{\lambda_1}$ and $E_{\lambda_2}$:

$$B_{\lambda_1} = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\}, \quad B_{\lambda_2} = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \right\}$$

**Geometric multiplicities:**
$$g_{\lambda_1} = \dim(E_{\lambda_1}) = 2 \quad \text{and} \quad g_{\lambda_2} = \dim(E_{\lambda_2}) = 1$$

> [!fact] Theorem
> ## Bounds on Geometric Multiplicity

If a matrix $A$ has eigenvalue $\lambda$, then:
$$1 \leq g_\lambda \leq a_\lambda$$

In other words, it is possible for the eigenspace to have fewer basis vectors (i.e., smaller dimension) than the number of times $\lambda$ appears as a root of $C_A(\lambda)$.

> [!info] Info
> ## Motivation for Diagonalization

Continuing with $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$, we have:
$$E_3 = \text{span}\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\}, \quad E_1 = \text{span}\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \right\}$$

Note that for any $\vec{x} \in E_3$, we have $A\vec{x} = 3\vec{x}$, and for any $\vec{y} \in E_1$, we have $A\vec{y} = \vec{y}$.

Let $\vec{x}_1, \vec{x}_2$ be two linearly independent vectors in $E_3$ and let $\vec{y} \in E_1$. Then:
$$\begin{bmatrix} A\vec{x}_1 & A\vec{x}_2 & A\vec{y} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3\vec{x}_1 & 3\vec{x}_2 & \vec{y} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \vec{x}_1 & \vec{x}_2 & \vec{y} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

This can be written as:
$$A \begin{bmatrix} \vec{x}_1 & \vec{x}_2 & \vec{y} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \vec{x}_1 & \vec{x}_2 & \vec{y} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

If we let $P = \begin{bmatrix} \vec{x}_1 & \vec{x}_2 & \vec{y} \end{bmatrix}$ be the matrix of linearly independent eigenvectors and $D = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ be the diagonal matrix of corresponding eigenvalues, then:
$$AP = PD$$

Since $P$ is made of linearly independent vectors, $\text{rank}(P) = 3$ and $P$ is <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">invertible</u></strong>. Therefore:
$$A = PDP^{-1}$$

> [!quote] Definition
> ## Diagonalizable Matrix

Given $A \in M_{n \times n}(\mathbb{R})$, if there exists an <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">invertible</u></strong> $n \times n$ matrix $P$ and a <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">diagonal</u></strong> $n \times n$ matrix $D$ such that:
$$A = PDP^{-1}$$
then we say $A$ is <u><strong style="color:#dab1da">diagonalizable</u></strong>.

> [!info] Info
> ## Powers of a Matrix

Diagonalization is very helpful when computing powers of a matrix.

If $A$ is diagonalizable with $A = PDP^{-1}$, then $A^2$ can be computed as:
$$A^2 = (PDP^{-1})(PDP^{-1}) = PD(P^{-1}P)DP^{-1} = PDIDP^{-1} = PD^2P^{-1}$$

In general:
$$A^n = PD^nP^{-1}$$

The key benefit is that powers of diagonal matrices are easy to compute. If:
$$D = \begin{bmatrix} d_1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & d_2 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & d_n \end{bmatrix}$$

then:
$$D^k = \begin{bmatrix} d_1^k & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & d_2^k & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & d_n^k \end{bmatrix}$$

For example, if $D = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$, then $D^3 = \begin{bmatrix} 2^3 & 0 \\ 0 & 3^3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 8 & 0 \\ 0 & 27 \end{bmatrix}$.

> [!info] Info
> ## Steps to Compute $A^k$ via Diagonalization

To compute $A^k$ for a diagonalizable matrix $A$:

**Step 1: Find the eigenvalues**
Solve $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$ to find all eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \ldots, \lambda_n$.

**Step 2: Find bases for each eigenspace**
For each eigenvalue $\lambda_i$, solve $(A - \lambda_i I)\vec{x} = \vec{0}$ to find a basis for $E_{\lambda_i}$.

Step 1 and 2: [[8.2 - Finding Eigen Pairs]]

**Step 3: Form the matrix $P$**
Construct $P$ by using the eigenvectors as columns:
$$P = \begin{bmatrix} \vec{v}_1 & \vec{v}_2 & \cdots & \vec{v}_n \end{bmatrix}$$
where each $\vec{v}_i$ is an eigenvector corresponding to eigenvalue $\lambda_i$.

**Step 4: Form the diagonal matrix $D$**
Construct $D$ with the corresponding eigenvalues on the diagonal (in the same order as the eigenvectors in $P$):
$$D = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & \lambda_n \end{bmatrix}$$

**Step 5: Compute $P^{-1}$**
Use any matrix inversion method (e.g., row reduction on $[P | I]$).
Refer to [[3.5 - Matrix Inverse]]

**Step 6: Compute $A^k = PD^kP^{-1}$**
Since $D^k$ is simply the diagonal matrix with entries $\lambda_i^k$, this computation is straightforward.
Refer to [[3.4 - Matrix Multiplication]]
