> [!example] Question 830 · Citadel Securities
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

What happens to the R-squared (R^2) value if you double the data in a regression analysis? What about the t-value?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=831)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Consider a linear regression estimated on a dataset of size $n$. Now suppose every observation is duplicated once, so the new dataset has size $2n$, with each original $(x_i,y_i)$ appearing twice.
>
> ## Effect on R-squared
>
> Recall
> $$
> R^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}},
> $$
> where $\text{SSE}$ is the sum of squared residuals and $\text{SST}$ is the total sum of squares.
>
> If every observation is duplicated:
>
> - each residual appears twice, so $\text{SSE}$ doubles
> - each deviation from the sample mean appears twice, so $\text{SST}$ also doubles
>
> Thus the ratio $\text{SSE}/\text{SST}$ is unchanged, and therefore
> $$
> R^2 \text{ is unchanged.}
> $$
>
> ## Effect on the t-value
>
> For a coefficient estimate $\hat\beta_j$, the $t$-statistic is
> $$
> t_j = \frac{\hat\beta_j}{\operatorname{se}(\hat\beta_j)}.
> $$
>
> When all observations are duplicated exactly:
>
> - the estimated coefficients $\hat\beta_j$ do not change, because both $X'X$ and $X'Y$ double, leaving $\hat\beta=(X'X)^{-1}X'Y$ unchanged
> - the matrix $X'X$ doubles, so $(X'X)^{-1}$ is halved
> - the sum of squared residuals $\text{SSE}$ doubles, while the degrees of freedom go from $n-k$ to $2n-k$, where $k$ is the number of estimated parameters including the intercept
>
> So the estimated error variances before and after duplication are
> $$
> \hat\sigma^2_{\text{old}} = \frac{\text{SSE}}{n-k},
> \qquad
> \hat\sigma^2_{\text{new}} = \frac{2\,\text{SSE}}{2n-k}.
> $$
>
> Using $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_j) \approx \hat\sigma^2 (X'X)^{-1}$ together with $(X'X)^{-1}_{\text{new}}=\frac12 (X'X)^{-1}_{\text{old}}$,
> $$
> \frac{\operatorname{Var}_{\text{new}}(\hat\beta_j)}{\operatorname{Var}_{\text{old}}(\hat\beta_j)}
> =
> \frac{\hat\sigma^2_{\text{new}}\cdot \frac12 (X'X)^{-1}_{\text{old}}}{\hat\sigma^2_{\text{old}}(X'X)^{-1}_{\text{old}}}
> =
> \frac{\hat\sigma^2_{\text{new}}}{2\,\hat\sigma^2_{\text{old}}}
> =
> \frac{\dfrac{2\,\text{SSE}}{2n-k}}{2\cdot \dfrac{\text{SSE}}{n-k}}
> =
> \frac{n-k}{2n-k}.
> $$
>
> Hence
> $$
> \frac{\operatorname{se}_{\text{new}}(\hat\beta_j)}{\operatorname{se}_{\text{old}}(\hat\beta_j)}
> =
> \sqrt{\frac{n-k}{2n-k}},
> $$
> and since $\hat\beta_j$ itself is unchanged, the exact factor by which the $t$-statistic changes is
> $$
> \frac{t_j^{\text{new}}}{t_j^{\text{old}}}
> =
> \sqrt{\frac{2n-k}{n-k}}.
> $$
>
> For $n$ large relative to $k$, this ratio approaches
> $$
> \sqrt{\frac{2n-k}{n-k}} \; \longrightarrow \; \sqrt{2},
> $$
> so in the usual large-sample approximation,
> $$
> t_j^{\text{new}} \approx \sqrt{2}\, t_j^{\text{old}}.
> $$
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> If the entire dataset is duplicated exactly:
>
> - $R^2$ stays the same
> - coefficient estimates stay the same
> - standard errors shrink by the exact factor $\sqrt{(n-k)/(2n-k)}$, approximately $1/\sqrt2$ for large $n$
> - $t$-statistics increase by the exact factor $\sqrt{(2n-k)/(n-k)}$, approximately $\sqrt2$ for large $n$
>
> This increase in the $t$-value is mechanical and does not reflect genuinely new information, since the added observations are exact copies.
