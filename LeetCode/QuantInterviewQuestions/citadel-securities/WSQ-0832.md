> [!example] Question 832 · Citadel Securities
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

Given an array representing the price of a stock on each day, and an integer k, determine the maximum profit you can achieve by completing at most k buy-sell transactions (you must sell before buying again).

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=833)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> Let $prices[i]$ be the stock price on day $i$, and suppose we may complete at most $k$ transactions, where each transaction consists of one buy followed by one sell.
>
> The goal is to maximize total profit.
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. Dynamic programming formulation
>
> A transaction is defined by a buy and a later sell, so at any point we must know:
>
> - how many transactions have been completed, and
> - whether we are currently holding a stock.
>
> Define:
>
> - $dp[t][0]$: maximum profit after processing the current day, having completed at most $t$ transactions, and **not holding** a stock.
> - $dp[t][1]$: maximum profit after processing the current day, having completed at most $t$ transactions, and **holding** a stock.
>
> Here $t \in \{0,1,\dots,k\}$.
>
> ### State transitions
>
> For each price $p$:
>
> - If we are **not holding** after today, then either:
>   - we were already not holding, or
>   - we sell today from a holding state.
>
> Thus,
> $$
> dp[t][0] = \max\bigl(dp[t][0],\ dp[t][1] + p\bigr).
> $$
>
> - If we are **holding** after today, then either:
>   - we were already holding, or
>   - we buy today from a non-holding state with one fewer completed transaction available for selling later.
>
> A cleaner equivalent formulation is to let the buy consume the transaction slot. Then:
> $$
> dp[t][1] = \max\bigl(dp[t][1],\ dp[t-1][0] - p\bigr), \qquad t \ge 1.
> $$
>
> This means:
> - after buying, we are in the holding state associated with transaction count $t$,
> - after selling, we return to the non-holding state for the same $t$.
>
> ### Initialization
>
> Before any day:
>
> - $dp[0][0] = 0$
> - $dp[0][1] = -\infty$
> - for $t \ge 1$:
>   - $dp[t][0] = 0$
>   - $dp[t][1] = -\infty$
>
> In implementation, instead of a full $2$D table, we keep two arrays:
>
> - $buy[t]$: best profit after buying the $t$-th stock
> - $sell[t]$: best profit after selling the $t$-th stock
>
> with transitions:
> $$
> buy[t] = \max\bigl(buy[t],\ sell[t-1] - p\bigr),
> $$
> $$
> sell[t] = \max\bigl(sell[t],\ buy[t] + p\bigr).
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Important optimization for large $k$
>
> A transaction needs at least $2$ days, so the maximum number of possible transactions is at most
> $$
> \left\lfloor \frac{n}{2} \right\rfloor,
> $$
> where $n = prices.length$.
>
> If
> $$
> k \ge \left\lfloor \frac{n}{2} \right\rfloor,
> $$
> then the constraint is effectively irrelevant, and this reduces to the unlimited-transactions problem.
>
> In that case, the optimal strategy is to sum all positive day-to-day increases:
> $$
> \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \max(0, prices[i] - prices[i-1]).
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Algorithm
>
> ### Case 1: large $k$
>
> If $k \ge n/2$, return:
> $$
> \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \max(0, prices[i] - prices[i-1]).
> $$
>
> ### Case 2: general $k$
>
> Maintain:
>
> - $buy[1 \dots k]$, initialized to $-\infty$
> - $sell[0 \dots k]$, initialized to $0$
>
> For each price $p$, for each transaction number $t = 1$ to $k$:
> $$
> buy[t] = \max(buy[t],\ sell[t-1] - p),
> $$
> $$
> sell[t] = \max(sell[t],\ buy[t] + p).
> $$
>
> The answer is:
> $$
> sell[k].
> $$
>
> Since $sell[t]$ is nondecreasing in $t$, this is the best profit with at most $k$ transactions.
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. Correctness argument
>
> We prove that the algorithm computes the maximum possible profit.
>
> ### Lemma 1
> After processing any day, $buy[t]$ equals the maximum profit achievable after performing the buy step of the $t$-th transaction and currently holding one stock.
>
> #### Proof
> A state contributing to $buy[t]$ can arise in exactly two ways:
>
> 1. We were already holding after the buy of the $t$-th transaction, so profit remains $buy[t]$.
> 2. We buy today after having completed at most $t-1$ sells, giving profit $sell[t-1] - p$.
>
> Taking the maximum of these two possibilities gives
> $$
> buy[t] = \max(buy[t],\ sell[t-1] - p).
> $$
> Thus the invariant holds. $\square$
>
> ### Lemma 2
> After processing any day, $sell[t]$ equals the maximum profit achievable after completing at most $t$ transactions and currently holding no stock.
>
> #### Proof
> A state contributing to $sell[t]$ can arise in exactly two ways:
>
> 1. We were already not holding, so profit remains $sell[t]$.
> 2. We sell today a stock previously represented by $buy[t]$, yielding profit $buy[t] + p$.
>
> Therefore,
> $$
> sell[t] = \max(sell[t],\ buy[t] + p),
> $$
> and the invariant holds. $\square$
>
> ### Theorem
> At the end of processing all days, $sell[k]$ is the maximum profit obtainable with at most $k$ transactions.
>
> #### Proof
> By Lemma $1$, each $buy[t]$ tracks the optimal holding-state profit for transaction number $t$. By Lemma $2$, each $sell[t]$ tracks the optimal non-holding-state profit after at most $t$ transactions. Since final profit must be realized with no stock in hand, the optimal answer is exactly the best non-holding profit with at most $k$ transactions, namely $sell[k]$. $\square$
>
> ---
>
> ## 5. Complexity analysis
>
> Let $n = prices.length$.
>
> - If $k \ge n/2$, the time complexity is
>   $$
>   O(n),
>   $$
>   and the space complexity is
>   $$
>   O(1).
>   $$
>
> - Otherwise, the time complexity is
>   $$
>   O(nk),
>   $$
>   and the space complexity is
>   $$
>   O(k).
>   $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 6. Reference implementation
>
> ```python
> class Solution:
>     def maxProfit(self, k: int, prices: List[int]) -> int:
>         n = len(prices)
>         if n == 0 or k == 0:
>             return 0
>
>         # If k is large, treat as unlimited transactions.
>         if k >= n // 2:
>             profit = 0
>             for i in range(1, n):
>                 if prices[i] > prices[i - 1]:
>                     profit += prices[i] - prices[i - 1]
>             return profit
>
>         buy = [float('-inf')] * (k + 1)
>         sell = [0] * (k + 1)
>
>         for price in prices:
>             for t in range(1, k + 1):
>                 buy[t] = max(buy[t], sell[t - 1] - price)
>                 sell[t] = max(sell[t], buy[t] + price)
>
>         return sell[k]
> ```
>
> ---
>
> ## Final answer
>
> Use dynamic programming with two $O(k)$ arrays:
>
> - $buy[t]$: best profit while holding after the $t$-th buy,
> - $sell[t]$: best profit while not holding after the $t$-th sell,
>
> with transitions
> $$
> buy[t] = \max(buy[t],\ sell[t-1] - price), \qquad
> sell[t] = \max(sell[t],\ buy[t] + price).
> $$
>
> If $k \ge \lfloor n/2 \rfloor$, use the unlimited-transactions shortcut by summing all positive adjacent differences.
>
> This yields:
>
> - time complexity $O(nk)$ and space complexity $O(k)$ in general,
> - time complexity $O(n)$ when $k$ is large.
