> [!example] Question 864 · Citadel Securities
> ## Quant Research Intern Interview

Derive and explain the concept of put-call parity in options pricing.

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=865)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Put–Call Parity
>
> Put–call parity is a no-arbitrage relationship between the prices of a European call and a European put written on the same underlying asset, with the same strike price and expiration date.
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. Setting
>
> Let
>
> - $S_0$ = current price of the underlying asset,
> - $K$ = strike price,
> - $T$ = time to maturity,
> - $r$ = continuously compounded risk-free interest rate,
> - $C$ = price of a European call,
> - $P$ = price of a European put.
>
> Assume no arbitrage and that the options are European, so they can be exercised only at time $T$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Construct two portfolios
>
> Consider the following two portfolios:
>
> ### Portfolio A
> - one European call,
> - cash equal to the present value of the strike, namely $K e^{-rT}$.
>
> Its value today is
>
> $$
> C + K e^{-rT}.
> $$
>
> At maturity, the cash grows to $K$, so the payoff is
>
> $$
> \max(S_T - K, 0) + K.
> $$
>
> This can be simplified by cases:
>
> - If $S_T > K$, then the call is exercised, so payoff is
>   $$
>   (S_T - K) + K = S_T.
>   $$
> - If $S_T \le K$, then the call expires worthless, so payoff is
>   $$
>   0 + K = K.
>   $$
>
> Hence Portfolio A has terminal payoff
>
> $$
> \max(S_T, K).
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### Portfolio B
> - one European put,
> - one share of the underlying.
>
> Its value today is
>
> $$
> P + S_0.
> $$
>
> At maturity, the payoff is
>
> $$
> \max(K - S_T, 0) + S_T.
> $$
>
> Again by cases:
>
> - If $S_T < K$, then the put is exercised, so payoff is
>   $$
>   (K - S_T) + S_T = K.
>   $$
> - If $S_T \ge K$, then the put expires worthless, so payoff is
>   $$
>   0 + S_T = S_T.
>   $$
>
> Hence Portfolio B also has terminal payoff
>
> $$
> \max(S_T, K).
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Apply no-arbitrage
>
> Since the two portfolios have exactly the same payoff at time $T$, no-arbitrage implies that they must have the same value today:
>
> $$
> C + K e^{-rT} = P + S_0.
> $$
>
> Rearranging gives the standard put–call parity formula:
>
> $$
> C - P = S_0 - K e^{-rT}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. Interpretation
>
> The quantity $C - P$ equals the value of a **forward-like position** in the underlying:
>
> - long one call,
> - short one put,
>
> with strike $K$ and maturity $T$.
>
> Indeed, at maturity,
>
> $$
> \max(S_T - K, 0) - \max(K - S_T, 0) = S_T - K.
> $$
>
> Thus a long call and short put replicate a long forward contract with delivery price $K$. The present value of that payoff is consistent with
>
> $$
> C - P = S_0 - K e^{-rT}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 5. Version with dividends
>
> If the underlying pays known dividends with present value $D$, then the stock in Portfolio B should be replaced by the stock net of the dividend effect. The parity becomes
>
> $$
> C - P = S_0 - D - K e^{-rT}.
> $$
>
> If the asset pays a continuous dividend yield $q$, then
>
> $$
> C - P = S_0 e^{-qT} - K e^{-rT}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 6. Why this must hold
>
> If put–call parity were violated, an arbitrageur could buy the cheaper of the two equivalent portfolios and sell the more expensive one, locking in a riskless profit today with zero net payoff at maturity.
>
> For example, if
>
> $$
> C + K e^{-rT} > P + S_0,
> $$
>
> then one could:
>
> - sell Portfolio A,
> - buy Portfolio B.
>
> This generates a positive cash inflow today, and since both portfolios have identical terminal payoffs, all future obligations cancel exactly. That is an arbitrage, so such a price discrepancy cannot persist in an arbitrage-free market.
>
> ---
>
> ## Final Formula
>
> For European options on a non-dividend-paying stock,
>
> $$
> \boxed{C - P = S_0 - K e^{-rT}}.
> $$
>
> Equivalently,
>
> $$
> \boxed{C + K e^{-rT} = P + S_0}.
> $$
>
> This is the put–call parity relation.
