> [!example] Question 824 · Citadel Securities
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

You regress X1 against Y and obtain an R-squared value of 0.1. Then, you regress X2 against Y and obtain an R-squared value of 0.2. What is the maximum possible R-squared when regressing both X1 and X2 against Y together?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=825)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let $Y$ be the dependent variable, and suppose:
>
> - Regressing $Y$ on $X_1$ alone gives $R^2 = 0.1$.
> - Regressing $Y$ on $X_2$ alone gives $R^2 = 0.2$.
>
> We seek the maximum possible $R^2$ from regressing $Y$ on both $X_1$ and $X_2$.
>
> ## Step 1: Interpret the given R-squared values
>
> In a simple regression, $R^2$ equals the squared correlation between the regressor and the dependent variable. Thus,
>
> $$
> \rho_{Y,X_1}^2 = 0.1, \qquad \rho_{Y,X_2}^2 = 0.2.
> $$
>
> Write $r_1=\rho_{Y,X_1}$ and $r_2=\rho_{Y,X_2}$; without loss of generality take $r_1,r_2\ge 0$, so
>
> $$
> r_1=\sqrt{0.1}, \qquad r_2=\sqrt{0.2}.
> $$
>
> ## Step 2: Express the joint R-squared in terms of the pairwise correlations
>
> Let $r_{12}=\operatorname{Corr}(X_1,X_2)$. For two regressors, the multiple $R^2$ can be written entirely in terms of $r_1,r_2,r_{12}$:
>
> $$
> R^2 = \frac{r_1^2+r_2^2-2r_1r_2r_{12}}{1-r_{12}^2}.
> $$
>
> If $X_1$ and $X_2$ were uncorrelated, $r_{12}=0$ and this reduces to the naive sum
>
> $$
> R^2 = r_1^2+r_2^2 = 0.1+0.2=0.3.
> $$
>
> But $r_{12}$ is not fixed by the problem, so $0.3$ is only the value at one particular choice of $r_{12}$, not the maximum over all valid joint distributions.
>
> ## Step 3: R-squared can be pushed all the way to 1
>
> The three pairwise correlations $r_1,r_2,r_{12}$ must form a valid, positive semidefinite correlation matrix for $(Y,X_1,X_2)$. Writing $\det$ for the determinant of that $3\times 3$ correlation matrix,
>
> $$
> \det = 1-r_1^2-r_2^2-r_{12}^2+2r_1r_2r_{12},
> $$
>
> and the two quantities are linked by
>
> $$
> 1-R^2=\frac{\det}{1-r_{12}^2}.
> $$
>
> So $R^2=1$ exactly when $\det=0$, which happens whenever
>
> $$
> r_{12}=r_1r_2\pm\sqrt{(1-r_1^2)(1-r_2^2)}.
> $$
>
> Substituting $r_1^2=0.1$, $r_2^2=0.2$,
>
> $$
> r_1r_2=\sqrt{0.02}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{10}, \qquad
> \sqrt{(1-r_1^2)(1-r_2^2)}=\sqrt{0.72}=\frac{3\sqrt{2}}{5}.
> $$
>
> Taking the minus sign,
>
> $$
> r_{12}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{10}-\frac{6\sqrt{2}}{10}=-\frac{5\sqrt{2}}{10}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\approx -0.707.
> $$
>
> This value lies in $[-1,1]$, so it is a feasible correlation: $X_1$ and $X_2$ are themselves fairly strongly negatively correlated even though each is only weakly correlated with $Y$. At this value of $r_{12}$ the determinant vanishes, and the two predictors' information combines to explain all of the variance in $Y$ — a classic suppressor effect, in which the correlation between the regressors, not just their individual correlations with $Y$, drives the joint fit.
>
> ## Step 4: Confirm the value at this correlation
>
> With $r_1^2=0.1$, $r_2^2=0.2$, $r_1r_2=\sqrt{0.02}$, and $r_{12}=-1/\sqrt{2}$ so that $r_{12}^2=0.5$,
>
> $$
> r_1^2+r_2^2-2r_1r_2r_{12}=0.3-2\sqrt{0.02}\left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)=0.3+2\sqrt{0.01}=0.3+0.2=0.5,
> $$
>
> and $1-r_{12}^2=0.5$, so
>
> $$
> R^2=\frac{0.5}{0.5}=1.
> $$
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> The naive sum $0.1+0.2=0.3$ is only what results if $X_1$ and $X_2$ happen to be uncorrelated; it is not an upper bound. Because the correlation between $X_1$ and $X_2$ is free to take any feasible value, the joint $R^2$ can be driven all the way up to its theoretical maximum:
>
> $$
> \boxed{R^2_{\max}=1}.
> $$
