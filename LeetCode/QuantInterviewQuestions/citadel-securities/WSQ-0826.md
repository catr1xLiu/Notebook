> [!example] Question 826 · Citadel Securities
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

Starting at the origin on a grid, you can move either up/down or left/right at each step. After N steps, how far can you be from the origin? Additionally, if there are two boundaries, what is the probability that you reach one boundary before the other?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=827)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## 1. Random walk on the line: how far after $N$ steps?
>
> Interpret the motion as a simple symmetric random walk.
>
> ### Case A: moving only left/right
> Let $X_1,\dots,X_N$ be independent steps with
> $$
> P(X_i=1)=P(X_i=-1)=\frac12.
> $$
> Then the position after $N$ steps is
> $$
> S_N=\sum_{i=1}^N X_i.
> $$
>
> Since
> $$
> E[X_i]=0,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_i)=1,
> $$
> we have
> $$
> E[S_N]=0,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(S_N)=N.
> $$
> Thus the typical distance from the origin is of order
> $$
> \sqrt{N}.
> $$
>
> More precisely:
>
> - The mean signed position is
>   $$
>   E[S_N]=0.
>   $$
> - The mean squared distance is
>   $$
>   E[S_N^2]=N.
>   $$
> - Hence the root-mean-square distance is
>   $$
>   \sqrt{E[S_N^2]}=\sqrt{N}.
>   $$
>
> The exact distribution is
> $$
> P(S_N=k)=
> \begin{cases}
> \displaystyle \binom{N}{(N+k)/2}2^{-N}, & k\equiv N \pmod 2,\ |k|\le N,\\[1ex]
> 0, & \text{otherwise.}
> \end{cases}
> $$
>
> So in one dimension, after $N$ steps, the distance reached is typically about
> $$
> \sqrt{N}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### Case B: moving on the plane, one unit in one of four directions
> If at each step you choose uniformly among
> $$
> (1,0),\ (-1,0),\ (0,1),\ (0,-1),
> $$
> then after $N$ steps the position is
> $$
> (S_N^{(x)},S_N^{(y)}).
> $$
> Each coordinate has mean $0$, and for one step,
> $$
> E[(X^{(1)})^2]=E[(Y^{(1)})^2]=\frac12,
> $$
> so after $N$ steps,
> $$
> E[(S_N^{(x)})^2]=E[(S_N^{(y)})^2]=\frac{N}{2}.
> $$
> Therefore
> $$
> E\!\left[(S_N^{(x)})^2+(S_N^{(y)})^2\right]=N.
> $$
> Thus the root-mean-square Euclidean distance from the origin is again of order
> $$
> \sqrt{N}.
> $$
>
> So in both the one-dimensional and planar symmetric random walk, the typical distance after $N$ steps is proportional to
> $$
> \sqrt{N}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Two boundaries: probability of hitting one before the other
>
> Now consider the one-dimensional walk with two absorbing boundaries.
>
> Let the walk start at position $i$, where
> $$
> a<i<b,
> $$
> and define
> $$
> \tau_a=\inf\{n\ge 0:S_n=a\},\qquad
> \tau_b=\inf\{n\ge 0:S_n=b\}.
> $$
> We want
> $$
> P_i(\tau_b<\tau_a),
> $$
> the probability of reaching $b$ before $a$.
>
> ### Symmetric walk
> Suppose
> $$
> P(\text{step }+1)=P(\text{step }-1)=\frac12.
> $$
> Let
> $$
> u(i)=P_i(\tau_b<\tau_a).
> $$
> Then for $a<i<b$,
> $$
> u(i)=\frac12 u(i+1)+\frac12 u(i-1),
> $$
> with boundary conditions
> $$
> u(a)=0,\qquad u(b)=1.
> $$
>
> The difference equation
> $$
> u(i+1)-2u(i)+u(i-1)=0
> $$
> has linear solutions, so
> $$
> u(i)=A i+B.
> $$
> Using the boundary conditions:
> $$
> u(a)=Aa+B=0,\qquad u(b)=Ab+B=1.
> $$
> Subtracting gives
> $$
> A(b-a)=1,\qquad A=\frac{1}{b-a}.
> $$
> Then
> $$
> B=-\frac{a}{b-a}.
> $$
> Hence
> $$
> u(i)=\frac{i-a}{b-a}.
> $$
>
> Therefore,
> $$
> P_i(\tau_b<\tau_a)=\frac{i-a}{b-a},
> $$
> and similarly
> $$
> P_i(\tau_a<\tau_b)=\frac{b-i}{b-a}.
> $$
>
> ### Special case: starting at the origin
> If the boundaries are at
> $$
> -L \quad \text{and} \quad U,
> $$
> with $L,U>0$, and the walk starts at $0$, then set
> $$
> a=-L,\qquad b=U,\qquad i=0.
> $$
> Thus
> $$
> P_0(\tau_U<\tau_{-L})
> =\frac{0-(-L)}{U-(-L)}
> =\frac{L}{L+U}.
> $$
> Similarly,
> $$
> P_0(\tau_{-L}<\tau_U)=\frac{U}{L+U}.
> $$
>
> If the boundaries are symmetric at $\pm m$, then
> $$
> P_0(\tau_m<\tau_{-m})=\frac12.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Biased walk
> If instead
> $$
> P(\text{step }+1)=p,\qquad P(\text{step }-1)=q=1-p,
> $$
> then the same hitting probability
> $$
> u(i)=P_i(\tau_b<\tau_a)
> $$
> satisfies
> $$
> u(i)=p\,u(i+1)+q\,u(i-1),
> $$
> with
> $$
> u(a)=0,\qquad u(b)=1.
> $$
>
> For $p\ne q$, the solution is
> $$
> u(i)=\frac{1-(q/p)^{\,i-a}}{1-(q/p)^{\,b-a}}.
> $$
> Thus
> $$
> P_i(\tau_b<\tau_a)=\frac{1-(q/p)^{\,i-a}}{1-(q/p)^{\,b-a}}.
> $$
>
> Starting at $0$ with boundaries at $-L$ and $U$:
> $$
> P_0(\tau_U<\tau_{-L})
> =
> \frac{1-(q/p)^{L}}{1-(q/p)^{L+U}}.
> $$
>
> When $p=\frac12$, this reduces to the linear formula
> $$
> P_0(\tau_U<\tau_{-L})=\frac{L}{L+U}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Final answer
>
> - After $N$ steps of a symmetric random walk, the typical distance from the origin is of order
>   $$
>   \sqrt{N}.
>   $$
>   In one dimension,
>   $$
>   E[S_N^2]=N,
>   $$
>   so the root-mean-square distance is exactly
>   $$
>   \sqrt{N}.
>   $$
>
> - For a symmetric one-dimensional walk with absorbing boundaries at $a<b$ and starting point $i$,
>   $$
>   P_i(\text{hit }b\text{ before }a)=\frac{i-a}{b-a},
>   $$
>   and
>   $$
>   P_i(\text{hit }a\text{ before }b)=\frac{b-i}{b-a}.
>   $$
>
> - In particular, starting at $0$ with boundaries at $-L$ and $U$,
>   $$
>   P_0(\text{hit }U\text{ before }-L)=\frac{L}{L+U},
>   \qquad
>   P_0(\text{hit }-L\text{ before }U)=\frac{U}{L+U}.
>   $$
>
> - For a biased walk with right-step probability $p$ and left-step probability $q=1-p$,
>   $$
>   P_i(\text{hit }b\text{ before }a)=
>   \frac{1-(q/p)^{\,i-a}}{1-(q/p)^{\,b-a}}
>   \qquad (p\ne q).
>   $$
