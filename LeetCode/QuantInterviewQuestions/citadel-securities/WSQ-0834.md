> [!example] Question 834 · Citadel Securities
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

Randomly generate points (x, y) on the circumference of a circle in two dimensions. What is Var(x)? What if you randomly generate points on the surface of a sphere instead?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=835)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let the circle or sphere be centered at the origin with radius $R$, and choose points uniformly on the circumference or surface.
>
> ## 1. Point chosen uniformly on the circumference of a circle
>
> A uniform point on the circle can be written as
> $$
> x = R\cos\theta,\qquad y = R\sin\theta,
> $$
> where
> $$
> \theta \sim \text{Uniform}(0,2\pi).
> $$
>
> We want
> $$
> \operatorname{Var}(x)=E[x^2]-(E[x])^2.
> $$
>
> By symmetry,
> $$
> E[x]=E[R\cos\theta]=R\,E[\cos\theta]=0.
> $$
>
> Next,
> $$
> E[x^2]=E[R^2\cos^2\theta]=R^2E[\cos^2\theta].
> $$
> Since the average value of $\cos^2\theta$ over $[0,2\pi]$ is $\tfrac12$,
> $$
> E[x^2]=R^2\cdot \frac12.
> $$
>
> Therefore,
> $$
> \operatorname{Var}(x)=\frac{R^2}{2}.
> $$
>
> For the unit circle $(R=1)$,
> $$
> \operatorname{Var}(x)=\frac12.
> $$
>
> ## 2. Point chosen uniformly on the surface of a sphere
>
> Now let $(x,y,z)$ be uniformly distributed on the surface of a sphere of radius $R$.
>
> By symmetry,
> $$
> E[x]=0.
> $$
>
> Also, every point on the sphere satisfies
> $$
> x^2+y^2+z^2=R^2.
> $$
> Taking expectations,
> $$
> E[x^2]+E[y^2]+E[z^2]=R^2.
> $$
> By rotational symmetry,
> $$
> E[x^2]=E[y^2]=E[z^2].
> $$
> Hence
> $$
> 3E[x^2]=R^2,
> $$
> so
> $$
> E[x^2]=\frac{R^2}{3}.
> $$
>
> Therefore,
> $$
> \operatorname{Var}(x)=E[x^2]-(E[x])^2=\frac{R^2}{3}.
> $$
>
> For the unit sphere $(R=1)$,
> $$
> \operatorname{Var}(x)=\frac13.
> $$
>
> ## Final answer
>
> - On the circumference of a circle of radius $R$:
>   $$
>   \operatorname{Var}(x)=\frac{R^2}{2}.
>   $$
>
> - On the surface of a sphere of radius $R$:
>   $$
>   \operatorname{Var}(x)=\frac{R^2}{3}.
>   $$
>
> In particular, for radius $1$:
> $$
> \operatorname{Var}(x)=\frac12 \quad \text{(circle)}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(x)=\frac13 \quad \text{(sphere)}.
> $$
