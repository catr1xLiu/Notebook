> [!example] Question 831 · Citadel Securities
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

We have n switches, each with probability p\_i of being on and 1-p\_i of being off. What is the probability that C or more switches are on? What if C is a uniform random variable between 0 and n?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=832)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let $X_i$ be the indicator that switch $i$ is on, so that
> $$
> X_i \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(p_i), \qquad \Pr(X_i=1)=p_i,\quad \Pr(X_i=0)=1-p_i,
> $$
> and assume the switches act independently. Define the total number of switches that are on by
> $$
> S=\sum_{i=1}^n X_i.
> $$
>
> The question asks for the probability
> $$
> \Pr(S \ge C).
> $$
>
> ## 1. Probability that at least $C$ switches are on
>
> Since the $X_i$ are independent but may have different success probabilities $p_i$, the random variable $S$ has the **Poisson binomial distribution**. Thus
> $$
> \Pr(S=k)
> =
> \sum_{\substack{A \subseteq \{1,\dots,n\}\\ |A|=k}}
> \left(\prod_{i\in A} p_i\right)
> \left(\prod_{j\notin A} (1-p_j)\right),
> \qquad k=0,1,\dots,n.
> $$
>
> Therefore,
> $$
> \Pr(S\ge C)
> =
> \sum_{k=C}^n \Pr(S=k),
> $$
> so explicitly
> $$
> \Pr(S\ge C)
> =
> \sum_{k=C}^n
> \sum_{\substack{A \subseteq \{1,\dots,n\}\\ |A|=k}}
> \left(\prod_{i\in A} p_i\right)
> \left(\prod_{j\notin A} (1-p_j)\right).
> $$
>
> This is the general exact formula.
>
> ### Equivalent generating-function form
>
> The probability generating function of $S$ is
> $$
> G(z)=\prod_{i=1}^n \bigl((1-p_i)+p_i z\bigr).
> $$
> Then $\Pr(S=k)$ is the coefficient of $z^k$ in $G(z)$:
> $$
> \Pr(S=k)=[z^k]\prod_{i=1}^n \bigl((1-p_i)+p_i z\bigr),
> $$
> and hence
> $$
> \Pr(S\ge C)=\sum_{k=C}^n [z^k]\prod_{i=1}^n \bigl((1-p_i)+p_i z\bigr).
> $$
>
> ## 2. Special case: all $p_i=p$
>
> If all switches have the same probability $p$, then $S\sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n,p)$, and
> $$
> \Pr(S\ge C)=\sum_{k=C}^n \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}.
> $$
>
> ## 3. If $C$ is a discrete uniform random variable on $\{0,1,\dots,n\}$
>
> Assume now that $C$ is independent of the switches and
> $$
> \Pr(C=c)=\frac{1}{n+1}, \qquad c=0,1,\dots,n.
> $$
> We want
> $$
> \Pr(S\ge C).
> $$
>
> Condition on $S=s$. Given $S=s$, the event $S\ge C$ is equivalent to $C\le s$, which happens for exactly $s+1$ values of $C$ among the $n+1$ possible values. Therefore
> $$
> \Pr(S\ge C\mid S=s)=\frac{s+1}{n+1}.
> $$
>
> Taking expectation over $S$,
> $$
> \Pr(S\ge C)
> =
> \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{S+1}{n+1}\right]
> =
> \frac{\mathbb{E}[S]+1}{n+1}.
> $$
> Since
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[S]=\sum_{i=1}^n p_i,
> $$
> it follows that
> $$
> \Pr(S\ge C)=\frac{1+\sum_{i=1}^n p_i}{n+1}.
> $$
>
> This gives a remarkably simple answer.
>
> ## Final answers
>
> For a fixed threshold $C$,
> $$
> \Pr(\text{at least } C \text{ switches are on})
> =
> \Pr(S\ge C)
> =
> \sum_{k=C}^n
> \sum_{\substack{A \subseteq \{1,\dots,n\}\\ |A|=k}}
> \left(\prod_{i\in A} p_i\right)
> \left(\prod_{j\notin A} (1-p_j)\right).
> $$
>
> If $C$ is independent and uniformly distributed on $\{0,1,\dots,n\}$, then
> $$
> \Pr(S\ge C)=\frac{1+\sum_{i=1}^n p_i}{n+1}.
> $$
>
> If additionally all $p_i=p$, this becomes
> $$
> \Pr(S\ge C)=\frac{1+np}{n+1}.
> $$
>
> If instead by “uniform on $[0,n]$” one means a **continuous** uniform random variable on the interval $[0,n]$, then since $S$ is integer-valued,
> $$
> \Pr(S\ge C\mid S=s)=\Pr(C\le s)=\frac{s}{n},
> $$
> so
> $$
> \Pr(S\ge C)=\frac{\mathbb{E}[S]}{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n p_i.
> $$
