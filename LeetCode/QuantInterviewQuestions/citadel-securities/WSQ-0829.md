> # Logistic Regression: The Basics
>
> Logistic regression is a supervised learning method used for **classification**, especially when the outcome has two possible values, such as:
>
> - $0$ or $1$
> - yes or no
> - spam or not spam
> - diseased or healthy
>
> Despite its name, logistic regression is used for predicting **probabilities** and **class labels**, not continuous values.
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. The Main Idea
>
> Suppose we want to predict a binary response variable $Y \in \{0,1\}$ from input features $x = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_p)$.
>
> In linear regression, one might try
> $$
> \hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_p x_p.
> $$
>
> However, this is not suitable for classification because the predicted value can be less than $0$ or greater than $1$, so it cannot be interpreted as a probability.
>
> Logistic regression solves this by modeling the probability
> $$
> P(Y=1 \mid x).
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. The Logistic Function
>
> The model uses the **logistic function** (also called the sigmoid function):
> $$
> \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}.
> $$
>
> This function maps any real number $z$ into the interval $(0,1)$, making it ideal for probabilities.
>
> In logistic regression, we define
> $$
> z = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_p x_p,
> $$
> and then model
> $$
> P(Y=1 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}.
> $$
>
> Equivalently,
> $$
> P(Y=1 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_p x_p)}}.
> $$
>
> Letting
> $$
> p(x) = P(Y=1 \mid x),
> $$
> we have
> $$
> p(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_p x_p)}}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Odds and Log-Odds
>
> A key interpretation comes from **odds**.
>
> If the probability of success is $p$, then the odds are
> $$
> \frac{p}{1-p}.
> $$
>
> Logistic regression assumes that the **log-odds** are a linear function of the inputs:
> $$
> \log\left(\frac{p(x)}{1-p(x)}\right)
> =
> \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_p x_p.
> $$
>
> This is called the **logit** form of the model.
>
> ### Interpretation of coefficients
> - $\beta_j$ represents the change in the **log-odds** for a one-unit increase in $x_j$, holding other variables fixed.
> - $e^{\beta_j}$ represents the multiplicative change in the **odds** for a one-unit increase in $x_j$.
>
> For example, if
> $$
> \beta_j = 0.7,
> $$
> then
> $$
> e^{0.7} \approx 2.01,
> $$
> so the odds are multiplied by about $2$ when $x_j$ increases by one unit.
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. Prediction Rule
>
> Once the model estimates $p(x)$, a class label can be assigned by choosing a threshold, usually $0.5$:
>
> - predict class $1$ if
>   $$
>   p(x) \ge 0.5,
>   $$
> - predict class $0$ if
>   $$
>   p(x) < 0.5.
>   $$
>
> Since $p(x) \ge 0.5$ exactly when $z \ge 0$, the decision boundary is given by
> $$
> \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_p x_p = 0.
> $$
>
> Thus, logistic regression produces a **linear decision boundary** in the feature space.
>
> ---
>
> ## 5. How the Parameters Are Estimated
>
> The coefficients $\beta_0, \beta_1, \dots, \beta_p$ are usually estimated by **maximum likelihood**.
>
> For data
> $$
> (x_i, y_i), \quad i = 1,2,\dots,n,
> $$
> where $y_i \in \{0,1\}$, let
> $$
> p_i = P(Y=1 \mid x_i).
> $$
>
> Then each observation contributes
> $$
> p_i^{y_i}(1-p_i)^{1-y_i}
> $$
> to the likelihood. The full likelihood is
> $$
> L(\beta) = \prod_{i=1}^n p_i^{y_i}(1-p_i)^{1-y_i}.
> $$
>
> Taking logs gives the log-likelihood:
> $$
> \ell(\beta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \left[ y_i \log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i) \right].
> $$
>
> The parameter estimates are chosen to maximize this expression.
>
> Because there is no simple closed-form solution, iterative numerical methods such as **gradient descent** or **Newton's method** are used.
>
> ---
>
> ## 6. Loss Function
>
> In machine learning, logistic regression is often trained by minimizing the **cross-entropy loss** (also called log loss), which is equivalent to maximizing the likelihood.
>
> For one observation:
> $$
> \text{Loss} = -\left[y \log p + (1-y)\log(1-p)\right].
> $$
>
> For the full dataset:
> $$
> J(\beta) = -\sum_{i=1}^n \left[y_i \log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\right].
> $$
>
> A smaller value means a better fit.
>
> ---
>
> ## 7. Simple Example
>
> Suppose the fitted model is
> $$
> \log\left(\frac{p(x)}{1-p(x)}\right) = -3 + 0.8x.
> $$
>
> Then
> $$
> p(x) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-(-3+0.8x)}}.
> $$
>
> If $x=5$, then
> $$
> z = -3 + 0.8(5) = 1,
> $$
> so
> $$
> p(5) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-1}} \approx 0.731.
> $$
>
> Thus the predicted probability of class $1$ is about $73.1\%$, and with threshold $0.5$, the predicted class is $1$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 8. Assumptions
>
> Logistic regression does not require the response variable to be normally distributed, but it does rely on several important assumptions:
>
> 1. **Binary outcome**: $Y$ should be binary for standard logistic regression.
> 2. **Independent observations**: the data points are assumed independent.
> 3. **Linearity in the logit**: the predictors are linearly related to the log-odds, not necessarily to the probability itself.
> 4. **Little or no multicollinearity**: predictors should not be highly redundant.
> 5. **Large enough sample size**: estimation is more reliable with sufficient data.
>
> ---
>
> ## 9. Advantages
>
> - Simple and interpretable
> - Outputs probabilities
> - Efficient to train
> - Works well for linearly separable or approximately linear classification problems
> - Coefficients have clear statistical meaning
>
> ---
>
> ## 10. Limitations
>
> - Assumes a linear relationship in the log-odds
> - May perform poorly when the true boundary is highly nonlinear
> - Sensitive to outliers in some settings
> - Can struggle when classes are severely imbalanced unless adjusted
> - Requires feature engineering for complex patterns
>
> ---
>
> ## 11. Regularization
>
> To prevent overfitting, logistic regression is often combined with regularization.
>
> ### $L_2$ regularization
> Adds a penalty
> $$
> \lambda \sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j^2.
> $$
>
> This shrinks coefficients toward zero.
>
> ### $L_1$ regularization
> Adds a penalty
> $$
> \lambda \sum_{j=1}^p |\beta_j|.
> $$
>
> This can force some coefficients to become exactly zero, which helps with feature selection.
>
> The regularized objective becomes
> $$
> \text{Loss} + \text{Penalty}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 12. Multiclass Extension
>
> Standard logistic regression handles two classes. For more than two classes, common extensions include:
>
> - **One-vs-rest**
> - **Multinomial logistic regression**
>
> In multinomial logistic regression, probabilities for multiple classes are modeled using the **softmax** function.
>
> ---
>
> ## 13. Summary
>
> Logistic regression models the probability of a binary outcome using
> $$
> P(Y=1 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_p x_p)}}.
> $$
>
> Equivalently, it assumes
> $$
> \log\left(\frac{p(x)}{1-p(x)}\right)
> =
> \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_p x_p.
> $$
>
> Its key features are:
>
> - it predicts probabilities between $0$ and $1$,
> - it uses a linear model on the log-odds scale,
> - parameters are estimated by maximum likelihood,
> - it is widely used because it is simple, fast, and interpretable.
>
> Thus, logistic regression is one of the foundational methods for binary classification in statistics and machine learning.
