> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

What is the difference between ordinary least squares (OLS) regression and Ridge regression?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=838)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Difference Between OLS and Ridge Regression
>
> Both **Ordinary Least Squares (OLS)** and **Ridge regression** are linear regression methods used to model a response variable as a linear function of predictors. Their key difference is that Ridge adds a penalty term to control coefficient size.
>
> ### 1. OLS regression
>
> OLS estimates coefficients by minimizing the residual sum of squares:
>
> $$
> \min_{\beta} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(y_i - x_i^\top \beta\right)^2
> $$
>
> In matrix form:
>
> $$
> \min_{\beta} \|y - X\beta\|_2^2
> $$
>
> If $X^\top X$ is invertible, the solution is
>
> $$
> \hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y
> $$
>
> #### Properties of OLS
> - It is **unbiased** under the standard linear model assumptions.
> - It can have **high variance**, especially when predictors are highly correlated.
> - It may perform poorly when there is **multicollinearity** or when the number of predictors is large relative to the sample size.
> - If $p > n$, OLS cannot be uniquely computed because $X^\top X$ is singular.
>
> ---
>
> ### 2. Ridge regression
>
> Ridge regression modifies the OLS objective by adding an $L_2$ penalty on the coefficients:
>
> $$
> \min_{\beta} \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(y_i - x_i^\top \beta\right)^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_j^2 \right\}
> $$
>
> Equivalently,
>
> $$
> \min_{\beta} \left\{ \|y - X\beta\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\beta\|_2^2 \right\}
> $$
>
> where $\lambda \ge 0$ is a tuning parameter.
>
> Its solution is
>
> $$
> \hat{\beta}_{\text{Ridge}} = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y
> $$
>
> #### Properties of Ridge
> - It **shrinks** coefficients toward $0$.
> - It introduces **bias**, but often reduces **variance**.
> - It is especially useful when predictors are highly correlated.
> - It can still be computed even when $X^\top X$ is singular.
> - It usually improves **prediction accuracy** when overfitting is a concern.
>
> ---
>
> ### 3. Main conceptual difference
>
> OLS seeks the best fit to the training data only, whereas Ridge balances fit and coefficient size.
>
> - **OLS:** minimize training error.
> - **Ridge:** minimize training error **plus** a penalty for large coefficients.
>
> Thus, Ridge is a **regularized** version of OLS.
>
> ---
>
> ### 4. Bias–variance tradeoff
>
> A central distinction is the bias–variance tradeoff:
>
> - OLS: **low bias**, potentially **high variance**
> - Ridge: **higher bias**, often **lower variance**
>
> Ridge can outperform OLS in predictive settings because reducing variance may more than compensate for the added bias.
>
> ---
>
> ### 5. Effect on coefficients
>
> - OLS does **not** constrain coefficient magnitudes.
> - Ridge makes coefficients smaller as $\lambda$ increases.
> - Ridge typically **does not set coefficients exactly to zero**; it shrinks them continuously.
>
> Special cases:
> - If $\lambda = 0$, Ridge reduces to OLS.
> - As $\lambda \to \infty$, Ridge coefficients approach $0$.
>
> ---
>
> ### 6. When to use each
>
> #### Use OLS when:
> - the predictors are not strongly collinear,
> - the sample size is sufficiently large,
> - interpretability without shrinkage is important,
> - the classical linear model assumptions are appropriate.
>
> #### Use Ridge when:
> - predictors are highly correlated,
> - there are many predictors,
> - overfitting is a concern,
> - prediction is the main goal rather than unbiased estimation.
>
> ---
>
> ## Final answer
>
> The difference is that **OLS regression** minimizes only the residual sum of squares, while **Ridge regression** minimizes the residual sum of squares **plus** an $L_2$ penalty on the coefficients:
>
> $$
> \text{OLS: } \min_{\beta} \|y - X\beta\|_2^2
> $$
>
> $$
> \text{Ridge: } \min_{\beta} \left( \|y - X\beta\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\beta\|_2^2 \right)
> $$
>
> As a result, OLS gives unbiased estimates but can be unstable with multicollinearity, whereas Ridge introduces bias to reduce variance, stabilize estimates, and often improve prediction performance.
