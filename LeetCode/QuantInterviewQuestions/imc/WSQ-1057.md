> ## 1. Law of Large Numbers, explained simply
>
> The **Law of Large Numbers** says:
>
> If you repeat the same kind of random event many times, the average result will usually get closer and closer to the true long-term average.
>
> ### Everyday example: flipping a coin
>
> Suppose you flip a fair coin.
>
> - On one flip, you might get heads.
> - On two flips, you might get two heads.
> - On ten flips, maybe you get $7$ heads and $3$ tails.
> - But if you flip it a very large number of times, the fraction of heads will usually get closer to $50\%$.
>
> This does **not** mean the coin becomes less random.  
> It means that over many tries, the overall pattern becomes more stable.
>
> ### A simple real-life picture
>
> Imagine a café owner tracking how many coffees are sold each day.
>
> - On one day, maybe they sell $40$ coffees.
> - On another day, maybe $65$.
> - Some days are unusually busy or unusually quiet.
>
> If the owner looks at only a few days, the average can jump around a lot.  
> If the owner looks at hundreds of days, the average daily sales become much more reliable.
>
> That is the basic idea of the Law of Large Numbers.
>
> ### What it does *not* mean
>
> It does **not** say that short-term results must “balance out” immediately.
>
> For example, if a coin lands heads five times in a row, the next flip is still just a normal coin flip. The law does not force the next result to be tails.
>
> Instead, it says that **over a large number of flips**, the overall average tends to settle near the expected value.
>
> ### Why this matters
>
> The law helps explain why averages are useful in the real world:
>
> - insurance companies estimate risk from many customers,
> - pollsters survey many people,
> - businesses study customer behavior over time,
> - sports statistics become more meaningful over many games.
>
> A small sample can be misleading. A large sample is usually more trustworthy.
>
> ### One-sentence summary
>
> The Law of Large Numbers means that **when you collect more and more results, the average usually becomes more stable and closer to the true long-term value**.
>

> ## 2. One sorting algorithm explained simply: Bubble Sort
>
> A **sorting algorithm** is just a step-by-step method for putting things in order, such as numbers from smallest to largest.
>
> ### Bubble Sort idea
>
> Bubble Sort works by comparing two neighboring items at a time and swapping them if they are in the wrong order.
>
> It repeats this again and again, and the larger items gradually move to the end, like bubbles rising to the surface of water.
>
> ### Example with numbers
>
> Suppose we want to sort:
>
> $$
> 5,\ 2,\ 4,\ 1
> $$
>
> We compare neighbors:
>
> #### First pass
> - Compare $5$ and $2$  
>   Since $5 > 2$, swap them:
>
> $$
> 2,\ 5,\ 4,\ 1
> $$
>
> - Compare $5$ and $4$  
>   Since $5 > 4$, swap them:
>
> $$
> 2,\ 4,\ 5,\ 1
> $$
>
> - Compare $5$ and $1$  
>   Since $5 > 1$, swap them:
>
> $$
> 2,\ 4,\ 1,\ 5
> $$
>
> Now the largest number, $5$, has moved all the way to the end.
>
> #### Second pass
> - Compare $2$ and $4$  
>   They are already in order.
>
> $$
> 2,\ 4,\ 1,\ 5
> $$
>
> - Compare $4$ and $1$  
>   Since $4 > 1$, swap them:
>
> $$
> 2,\ 1,\ 4,\ 5
> $$
>
> Now $4$ is in the correct place.
>
> #### Third pass
> - Compare $2$ and $1$  
>   Since $2 > 1$, swap them:
>
> $$
> 1,\ 2,\ 4,\ 5
> $$
>
> Now the list is sorted.
>
> ### Everyday analogy
>
> Imagine people standing in a line holding number cards.
>
> A helper walks down the line and looks at each pair of neighbors:
>
> - If the smaller number is already on the left, do nothing.
> - If the bigger number is on the left, ask them to switch places.
>
> After walking through the line many times, everyone ends up in the right order.
>
> ### Why it is easy to understand
>
> Bubble Sort is popular for teaching because:
>
> - it is simple,
> - it follows a clear repeated rule,
> - you can watch the list become more ordered step by step.
>
> ### Its weakness
>
> Bubble Sort is easy to understand, but it is not very fast for large lists.  
> If you have many items, it may need lots of repeated comparisons.
>
> So in real software, faster methods are often preferred. But Bubble Sort is a good first example because the logic is very intuitive.
>
> ### One-sentence summary
>
> Bubble Sort sorts a list by **repeatedly comparing neighboring items and swapping them when they are in the wrong order**, until everything is in place.
