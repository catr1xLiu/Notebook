> [!example] Question 1100 · IMC
> ## Software Engineer Intern Interview

What is the minimum number of moves required for a knight to reach a target position on a chessboard, given that there is a bishop present which threatens certain squares? The knight cannot move to squares attacked by the bishop.

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=1101)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> Model the chessboard as an $8 \times 8$ grid. A knight starts on some square and must reach some target square in the minimum number of moves. In addition, there is a bishop fixed on one square, and the knight is not allowed to move onto any square attacked by that bishop (and, of course, not onto the bishop’s own square).
>
> The problem is therefore a shortest-path problem on a graph.
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. Squares forbidden by the bishop
>
> Let the bishop be on square $(r_b,c_b)$, where rows and columns are numbered from $1$ to $8$.
>
> A bishop attacks exactly the squares lying on the same diagonal, so a square $(r,c)$ is forbidden if either
>
> $$
> r = r_b \text{ and } c = c_b
> $$
>
> or
>
> $$
> |r-r_b| = |c-c_b|.
> $$
>
> Thus, all such squares are blocked for the knight.
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Knight moves
>
> From any square $(r,c)$, a knight can move to any of the following $8$ positions:
>
> $$
> (r+2,c+1),\quad (r+2,c-1),\quad (r-2,c+1),\quad (r-2,c-1),
> $$
>
> $$
> (r+1,c+2),\quad (r+1,c-2),\quad (r-1,c+2),\quad (r-1,c-2).
> $$
>
> A move is valid only if:
>
> 1. the destination remains on the board, i.e.
>    $$
>    1 \le r \le 8,\qquad 1 \le c \le 8,
>    $$
> 2. the destination square is not forbidden by the bishop.
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Why breadth-first search gives the minimum
>
> Each legal knight move has equal cost, namely $1$. Therefore, if we build a graph whose vertices are the allowed squares and whose edges are legal knight moves, then the least number of moves from the start square to the target square is exactly the graph distance between those two vertices.
>
> Breadth-first search (BFS) computes shortest path lengths in an unweighted graph. Hence BFS gives the minimum number of knight moves.
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. Algorithm
>
> Let the start square be $S$ and the target square be $T$.
>
> 1. Mark every square attacked by the bishop as blocked.
> 2. If $S$ or $T$ is blocked, then the answer is impossible.
> 3. Run BFS from $S$ over all legal knight moves.
> 4. The first time $T$ is reached, its BFS level is the minimum number of moves.
> 5. If BFS ends without reaching $T$, then the target is unreachable.
>
> ---
>
> ## 5. Pseudocode
>
> ```text
> blocked(r,c) = ((r,c) = bishop) or (|r-r_b| = |c-c_b|)
>
> BFS(start, target):
>     if blocked(start) or blocked(target):
>         return -1
>
>     create queue Q
>     create visited[8][8] initialized false
>     create dist[8][8]
>
>     visited[start] = true
>     dist[start] = 0
>     push start into Q
>
>     while Q not empty:
>         u = pop front(Q)
>
>         if u = target:
>             return dist[u]
>
>         for each knight move from u to v:
>             if v is on board and not blocked(v) and not visited[v]:
>                 visited[v] = true
>                 dist[v] = dist[u] + 1
>                 push v into Q
>
>     return -1
> ```
>
> Here, returning $-1$ means that no legal route exists.
>
> ---
>
> ## 6. Complexity
>
> The chessboard has only $64$ squares, and each square has at most $8$ knight moves. Hence BFS runs in
>
> $$
> O(64 + 8\cdot 64) = O(1)
> $$
>
> time on a standard chessboard, and uses
>
> $$
> O(64) = O(1)
> $$
>
> space.
>
> If generalized to an $n \times n$ board, the complexity is
>
> $$
> O(n^2)
> $$
>
> time and
>
> $$
> O(n^2)
> $$
>
> space.
>
> ---
>
> ## Final answer
>
> The least number of knight moves in the presence of one bishop threat is found by:
>
> 1. blocking every square on the bishop’s diagonals (including the bishop’s square),
> 2. running **breadth-first search** from the knight’s start square using only legal knight moves,
> 3. taking the BFS distance to the destination.
>
> Thus, the minimum number of moves is exactly the BFS shortest-path distance; if the destination is never reached, then it is impossible.
