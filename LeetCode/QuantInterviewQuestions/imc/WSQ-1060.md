> [!example] Question 1060 · IMC
> ## Graduate Quant Trader Interview

What is the expected number of times you need to roll a fair six-sided die to see an odd number?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=1061)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Expected number of rolls to see an odd number
>
> Assume a fair six-sided die. The odd outcomes are $1, 3, 5$, so the probability of rolling an odd number on any single roll is
>
> $$
> p=\frac{3}{6}=\frac{1}{2}.
> $$
>
> Let $X$ be the number of rolls needed until the first odd number appears. Then $X$ follows a geometric distribution with success probability $p=\frac{1}{2}$.
>
> For a geometric random variable, the expected value is
>
> $$
> E[X]=\frac{1}{p}.
> $$
>
> Thus,
>
> $$
> E[X]=\frac{1}{1/2}=2.
> $$
>
> Therefore, the expected number of rolls needed to see an odd number is
>
> $$
> \boxed{2}.
> $$
>
> ## Battleship-themed version
>
> If each turn in a Battleship-style game has probability $p$ of being a hit, and turns are independent, then the number of turns until the first hit is geometric. Its expected value is
>
> $$
> E[X]=\frac{1}{p}.
> $$
>
> For example, if the chance of a hit is $p=\frac{1}{4}$, then
>
> $$
> E[X]=\frac{1}{1/4}=4.
> $$
>
> ## Sampling-from-a-data-set version
>
> Suppose a data set has proportion $p$ of entries with some desired property, and items are sampled independently until the first such entry is found. Then the number of samples needed is geometric with expected value
>
> $$
> E[X]=\frac{1}{p}.
> $$
>
> For example, if $20\%$ of the data satisfy the property, then $p=0.2$, so
>
> $$
> E[X]=\frac{1}{0.2}=5.
> $$
>
> Thus, the die problem, the Battleship problem, and the data-sampling problem all use the same geometric-distribution idea: if each trial has success probability $p$, the expected number of trials until the first success is
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{1}{p}}.
> $$
