> [!example] Question 1053 · IMC
> ## Quant Trader Intern Interview

You and a taxman are playing a game with N cards, each labeled with a natural number from 1 to N. You can pick a card only if at least one of its factors is still on the table, and for every card you pick, the taxman takes all available factors of that card from the table. How do you maximize your score?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=1054)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Taxman game: maximizing the score
>
> We have cards labeled $1,2,\dots,N$ on the table.
>
> - When you pick a card $k$, the taxman immediately takes **every** proper divisor of $k$ that is still on the table, not just one.
> - You are allowed to pick $k$ only if at least one proper divisor of $k$ is still on the table.
> - If no remaining card has any proper divisor left on the table, the game ends; any cards still on the table at that point belong to neither player.
> - Your score is the sum of the cards you pick; the taxman's score is the sum of the cards he takes.
>
> The goal is to maximize your total.
>
> ## 1. An upper-bound heuristic: matching
>
> A useful relaxation imagines that each number $k$ you keep only ever has to sacrifice a single proper divisor, chosen in advance. Formally, build a bipartite graph with an edge from $k$ to each proper divisor $d$ of $k$, weighted by $k-d$, and look for a maximum-weight matching in which each kept number is paired with a distinct divisor.
>
> This matching value is only an **upper bound** on what is truly achievable. In the real game, picking $k$ sweeps away *all* of its currently-present proper divisors at once, not just the one you had "assigned" to it in the matching. If more than one of $k$'s proper divisors happens to still be on the table at the moment you pick it, you pay for all of them, which can only hurt you relative to the idealized single-sacrifice matching.
>
> The bound is only attained if there exists a legal play **order** under which, whenever a matched number is picked, every one of its other proper divisors has already been removed by some earlier pick.
>
> ## 2. Finding the true optimum requires search
>
> Whether a given matching can actually be realized by some order is itself a nontrivial question, and when it cannot, finding the best achievable score requires searching directly over play orders (for example, by dynamic programming over which cards remain), rather than reading the answer off the matching. The matching is a good starting heuristic and upper bound, but confirming optimality requires checking achievability.
>
> ## 3. Example: $N=10$
>
> The proper divisors are:
>
> - $2:\{1\}$, $3:\{1\}$, $4:\{1,2\}$, $5:\{1\}$, $6:\{1,2,3\}$, $7:\{1\}$, $8:\{1,2,4\}$, $9:\{1,3\}$, $10:\{1,2,5\}$
>
> A matching pairing $1\!\leftrightarrow\!7$, $2\!\leftrightarrow\!6$, $3\!\leftrightarrow\!9$, $4\!\leftrightarrow\!8$, $5\!\leftrightarrow\!10$ gives kept set $\{6,7,8,9,10\}$ with value $40$, so $40$ is an upper bound.
>
> Playing the matched numbers in **decreasing** order does not realize it: picking $10$ first sweeps away all of $1,2,5$ at once, since all three are still on the table. That strands $7$, whose only proper divisor is $1$, which is now gone — so $7$ can never legally be picked afterward.
>
> Instead, play in the order $7,9,6,8,10$:
>
> - Pick $7$: proper divisor $1$ present, legal. Taxman takes $1$.
> - Pick $9$: proper divisors $\{1,3\}$, only $3$ present, legal. Taxman takes $3$.
> - Pick $6$: proper divisors $\{1,2,3\}$, only $2$ present, legal. Taxman takes $2$.
> - Pick $8$: proper divisors $\{1,2,4\}$, only $4$ present, legal. Taxman takes $4$.
> - Pick $10$: proper divisors $\{1,2,5\}$, only $5$ present, legal. Taxman takes $5$.
>
> You end with $\{6,7,8,9,10\}$, for a score of
>
> $$
> 6+7+8+9+10 = 40.
> $$
>
> The taxman ends with $\{1,2,3,4,5\}$, for $15$, and $40+15=55$ accounts for every card, so there are no leftovers in this instance. Since this achieves the matching upper bound exactly, $40$ is the true optimum for $N=10$.
>
> ## 4. General conclusion
>
> There is no simple greedy rule that is always optimal, and no guarantee that the matching bound is achievable for every $N$. The practical method is:
>
> - build the maximum-weight matching on the divisor graph, with edge weight $k-d$, to get an upper bound on the score,
> - search for a play order realizing it, removing matched numbers only once all their other proper divisors have already been swept by earlier picks,
> - if no such order exists, refine the search to find the true optimum, which may fall short of the matching bound,
> - remember that any cards left on the table once no legal move remains belong to neither player.
