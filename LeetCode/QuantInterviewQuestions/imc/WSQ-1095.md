> ## Trader Intern Interview

Derive the Central Limit Theorem for a sequence of independent coin tosses.

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=1096)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Derivation of the Central Limit Theorem for Coin Tosses
>
> Consider independent coin tosses $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n$, where
>
> $$
> X_i =
> \begin{cases}
> 1, & \text{if the $i$th toss is heads},\\
> 0, & \text{if the $i$th toss is tails}.
> \end{cases}
> $$
>
> Assume the coin has probability $p$ of landing heads, so that
>
> $$
> P(X_i = 1) = p, \qquad P(X_i = 0) = 1-p.
> $$
>
> Thus each $X_i$ is a Bernoulli random variable with
>
> $$
> E[X_i] = p, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_i) = p(1-p).
> $$
>
> Let
>
> $$
> S_n = X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n
> $$
>
> be the total number of heads in $n$ tosses. Then $S_n \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(n,p)$, and
>
> $$
> E[S_n] = np, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(S_n) = np(1-p).
> $$
>
> The Central Limit Theorem states that the standardized sum converges in distribution to a standard normal random variable:
>
> $$
> \frac{S_n - np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}} \xrightarrow{d} N(0,1)
> \qquad \text{as } n \to \infty.
> $$
>
> To derive this, use moment generating functions.
>
> ### Step 1: Standardize the sum
>
> Define
>
> $$
> Z_n = \frac{S_n - np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}.
> $$
>
> We compute the moment generating function of $Z_n$:
>
> $$
> M_{Z_n}(t) = E\left[e^{tZ_n}\right]
> = E\left[\exp\left(\frac{t(S_n-np)}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\right)\right].
> $$
>
> Since $S_n = X_1+\cdots+X_n$ and the $X_i$ are independent,
>
> $$
> M_{Z_n}(t)
> = e^{-t np/\sqrt{np(1-p)}} \prod_{i=1}^n E\left[\exp\left(\frac{tX_i}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\right)\right].
> $$
>
> Because the $X_i$ are identically distributed,
>
> $$
> M_{Z_n}(t)
> = e^{-t np/\sqrt{np(1-p)}} \left(E\left[e^{uX_1}\right]\right)^n,
> $$
>
> where
>
> $$
> u = \frac{t}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}.
> $$
>
> For a Bernoulli random variable,
>
> $$
> E[e^{uX_1}] = (1-p)e^0 + pe^u = 1-p + pe^u.
> $$
>
> Hence
>
> $$
> M_{Z_n}(t)
> = e^{-t np/\sqrt{np(1-p)}} (1-p+pe^u)^n.
> $$
>
> Since
>
> $$
> \frac{np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}} = \frac{p\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}},
> $$
>
> it is more convenient to take logarithms:
>
> $$
> \log M_{Z_n}(t)
> = -\frac{tnp}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}} + n \log(1-p+pe^u).
> $$
>
> ### Step 2: Expand for small $u$
>
> As $n \to \infty$, we have $u \to 0$. Using the Taylor expansion
>
> $$
> e^u = 1 + u + \frac{u^2}{2} + o(u^2),
> $$
>
> we obtain
>
> $$
> 1-p+pe^u
> = 1-p + p\left(1 + u + \frac{u^2}{2} + o(u^2)\right)
> = 1 + pu + \frac{pu^2}{2} + o(u^2).
> $$
>
> Now use
>
> $$
> \log(1+x) = x - \frac{x^2}{2} + o(x^2)
> \qquad \text{as } x \to 0.
> $$
>
> Here,
>
> $$
> x = pu + \frac{pu^2}{2} + o(u^2),
> $$
>
> so
>
> $$
> \log(1-p+pe^u)
> = pu + \frac{pu^2}{2} - \frac{p^2u^2}{2} + o(u^2)
> = pu + \frac{p(1-p)}{2}u^2 + o(u^2).
> $$
>
> Therefore,
>
> $$
> \log M_{Z_n}(t)
> = -\frac{tnp}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}} + n\left(pu + \frac{p(1-p)}{2}u^2 + o(u^2)\right).
> $$
>
> Substitute $u = \dfrac{t}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}$:
>
> $$
> np\,u = np \cdot \frac{t}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}
> = \frac{tnp}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}},
> $$
>
> so the linear terms cancel. Also,
>
> $$
> n \cdot \frac{p(1-p)}{2}u^2
> = n \cdot \frac{p(1-p)}{2} \cdot \frac{t^2}{np(1-p)}
> = \frac{t^2}{2}.
> $$
>
> Finally, since $u^2 = O(1/n)$, we have
>
> $$
> n\,o(u^2) \to 0.
> $$
>
> Thus
>
> $$
> \log M_{Z_n}(t) \to \frac{t^2}{2}.
> $$
>
> Exponentiating,
>
> $$
> M_{Z_n}(t) \to e^{t^2/2},
> $$
>
> which is the moment generating function of $N(0,1)$.
>
> Therefore,
>
> $$
> Z_n = \frac{S_n - np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}} \xrightarrow{d} N(0,1).
> $$
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> For the number of heads $S_n$ in $n$ independent tosses of a coin with head probability $p$,
>
> $$
> \frac{S_n - np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}} \xrightarrow{d} N(0,1).
> $$
>
> Equivalently, for large $n$,
>
> $$
> S_n \approx N\bigl(np,\; np(1-p)\bigr).
> $$
>
> In the special case of a fair coin, where $p=\tfrac12$,
>
> $$
> E[S_n] = \frac{n}{2}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(S_n) = \frac{n}{4},
> $$
>
> and hence
>
> $$
> \frac{S_n - n/2}{\sqrt{n/4}}
> = \frac{S_n - n/2}{\sqrt{n}/2}
> \xrightarrow{d} N(0,1).
> $$
>
> So the number of heads in many fair coin tosses is approximately normal with mean $\dfrac{n}{2}$ and variance $\dfrac{n}{4}$.
