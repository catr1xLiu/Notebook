> [!example] Question 1013 · IMC
> ## Software Engineer Interview

What is the time complexity of various operations (such as insert, erase, and find) in std::set?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=1014)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Complexity of std::set operations
>
> `std::set` is typically implemented as a balanced binary search tree, usually a red-black tree. Therefore, most search, insertion, and deletion operations take logarithmic time.
>
> Let $n$ be the number of elements in the set.
>
> ### Core operations
>
> - `insert` : $O(\log n)$
> - `erase(key)` : $O(\log n)$
> - `erase(iterator)` : amortized $O(1)$ once the node is located; since finding that iterator generally costs $O(\log n)$, deletion is usually summarized as $O(\log n)$ overall
> - `find` : $O(\log n)$
> - `count` : $O(\log n)$, and since `std::set` contains unique keys the result is always $0$ or $1$
> - `contains` : $O(\log n)$
> - `lower_bound` : $O(\log n)$
> - `upper_bound` : $O(\log n)$
> - `equal_range` : $O(\log n)$
> - `clear` : $O(n)$
> - `size` : $O(1)$
> - `empty` : $O(1)$
> - `begin`, `end` : $O(1)$
>
> ### Range operations
>
> - `insert(first, last)` : $O(m \log(n+m))$ in general, where $m$ is the number of inserted elements
> - `erase(first, last)` : linear in the number of elements erased plus the rebalancing cost, commonly stated as $O(k\log n)$ where $k$ is the number of erased elements
>
> ### Iterator movement
>
> - Incrementing or decrementing an iterator, such as `++it` or `--it`, is amortized $O(1)$
>
> ### Comparison with std::unordered_set
>
> This is often asked together with `std::set`:
>
> - `std::set`: ordered, operations are typically $O(\log n)$
> - `std::unordered_set`: unordered, average-case operations are $O(1)$, worst-case $O(n)$
>
> ### Interview-ready summary
>
> `std::set` is usually implemented as a balanced BST, so `insert`, `erase`, `find`, `count`, `lower_bound`, and `upper_bound` are all $O(\log n)$. Access to `begin`, `end`, `size`, and `empty` is $O(1)$, and `clear` is $O(n)$. The key distinction from `std::unordered_set` is that `std::set` keeps elements sorted and guarantees logarithmic operations.
