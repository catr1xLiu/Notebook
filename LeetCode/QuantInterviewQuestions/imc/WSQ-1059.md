> ## 1. Explaining a sorting algorithm to someone without a STEM background
>
> A sorting algorithm is simply a clear set of steps for putting things in order, such as names in alphabetical order or numbers from smallest to largest.
>
> One easy example is **bubble sort**.
>
> ### Idea
>
> Imagine a line of books with price tags. You want the cheapest on the left and the most expensive on the right.
>
> You look at **two neighboring books at a time**:
>
> - if they are in the right order, leave them alone;
> - if they are in the wrong order, swap them.
>
> Then you move one step forward and do the same thing again.
>
> After one full pass through the line, the biggest item has “bubbled” to the end. That is why it is called **bubble sort**.
>
> ### Everyday example
>
> Suppose the numbers are:
>
> $$4,\ 2,\ 5,\ 1$$
>
> Compare neighbors:
>
> 1. Compare $4$ and $2$  
>    Since $4 > 2$, swap them:
>
>    $$2,\ 4,\ 5,\ 1$$
>
> 2. Compare $4$ and $5$  
>    They are already in the right order, so keep them:
>
>    $$2,\ 4,\ 5,\ 1$$
>
> 3. Compare $5$ and $1$  
>    Since $5 > 1$, swap them:
>
>    $$2,\ 4,\ 1,\ 5$$
>
> Now the largest number, $5$, is in the correct final position.
>
> Start again from the beginning:
>
> 1. Compare $2$ and $4$  
>    Correct order:
>
>    $$2,\ 4,\ 1,\ 5$$
>
> 2. Compare $4$ and $1$  
>    Swap:
>
>    $$2,\ 1,\ 4,\ 5$$
>
> Start again:
>
> 1. Compare $2$ and $1$  
>    Swap:
>
>    $$1,\ 2,\ 4,\ 5$$
>
> Now everything is sorted.
>
> ### Intuition
>
> This method is like tidying a messy line by repeatedly checking neighbors and fixing small mistakes. It is not the fastest method, but it is easy to understand because it uses a very human process:
>
> - look at two nearby items;
> - decide whether they are in the wrong order;
> - switch them if needed;
> - repeat until no mistakes remain.
>
> ### Main point
>
> A sorting algorithm is not magic. It is just a reliable recipe for arranging items step by step.
>

> ## 2. Explaining the law of large numbers to someone without a STEM background
>
> The **law of large numbers** says:
>
> when you repeat the same random process many times, the overall average tends to settle down near the true average.
>
> ### Coin-flip example
>
> Imagine flipping a fair coin.
>
> - Heads counts as $1$
> - Tails counts as $0$
>
> A fair coin should land heads about half the time, so the long-run average should be close to
>
> $$0.5$$
>
> But in a small number of flips, strange things can happen.
>
> For example:
>
> - after $2$ flips, you might get heads both times, giving an average of
>
>   $$1.0$$
>
> - after $10$ flips, you might get $7$ heads, giving an average of
>
>   $$0.7$$
>
> That may seem far from $0.5$. But if you keep flipping:
>
> - after $100$ flips, the average often gets closer to $0.5$;
> - after $1000$ flips, it usually gets closer still.
>
> The law does **not** say the results become perfectly balanced every short stretch. It says that over a **large total number** of tries, the average becomes more stable and usually gets closer to the expected value.
>
> ### Everyday example
>
> Suppose a cafe owner wants to know the average amount customers spend.
>
> - If only $3$ customers come in, the average could be unusual.
> - If $3000$ customers come in, the average spending becomes much more reliable.
>
> A few unusual customers matter a lot in a tiny sample, but much less in a very large one.
>
> ### Important intuition
>
> Small samples are jumpy.  
> Large samples are steadier.
>
> That is the heart of the law of large numbers.
>
> ### What it does **not** mean
>
> It does **not** mean that if a coin has landed tails many times, heads is now “due.” Each flip is still its own event.
>
> Instead, it means that as the number of flips grows, the **overall proportion** of heads tends to move toward
>
> $$\frac{1}{2}$$
>
> ### Main point
>
> The law of large numbers explains why averages based on lots of observations are usually more trustworthy than averages based on only a few.
