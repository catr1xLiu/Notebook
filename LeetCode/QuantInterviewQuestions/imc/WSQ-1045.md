> [!example] Question 1045 · IMC
> ## Graduate Trader Interview

Consider a game where you flip a fair coin: if it lands heads, you win \$20, and if it lands tails, you lose \$5. Would you like to play this game? In a second game, the payouts are \$20 million for heads and lose \$5 for tails. Would you prefer the second game? Lastly, if the payouts return to \$20 for heads and -\$5 for tails, but you can choose to play either once or 100 times, which option do you prefer and why? Please explain your reasoning.

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=1046)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> Interpret each game as a gamble with a fair coin, so that
>
> - with probability $\tfrac12$, the payoff is the stated amount for heads,
> - with probability $\tfrac12$, the payoff is the stated amount for tails.
>
> A natural first criterion is the **expected value**.
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. First game: heads $=\text{\textdollar}20$, tails $=-\text{\textdollar}5$
>
> Let $X$ be the payoff. Then
>
> $$
> P(X=20)=\frac12,\qquad P(X=-5)=\frac12.
> $$
>
> The expected payoff is
>
> $$
> E[X]=\frac12(20)+\frac12(-5)=10-2.5=7.5.
> $$
>
> Since
>
> $$
> E[X]=\$7.50>0,
> $$
>
> this is a favorable game in expectation.  
> So, under an expected-value criterion, one should like this game.
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Second game: heads $=\text{\textdollar}20{,}000{,}000$, tails $=-\text{\textdollar}5$
>
> Let $Y$ be the payoff. Then
>
> $$
> P(Y=20{,}000{,}000)=\frac12,\qquad P(Y=-5)=\frac12.
> $$
>
> Its expected payoff is
>
> $$
> E[Y]=\frac12(20{,}000{,}000)+\frac12(-5)
> =10{,}000{,}000-2.5
> =9{,}999{,}997.5.
> $$
>
> Thus
>
> $$
> E[Y]=\$9{,}999{,}997.50,
> $$
>
> which is vastly larger than the first game’s expected payoff of $\text{\textdollar}7.50$.
>
> So, by expected value alone, the second game is strictly preferable.
>
> ### But risk preferences matter
> If one is allowed to play only once, the probability of losing is still
>
> $$
> \frac12,
> $$
>
> and the loss is only $\text{\textdollar}5$. Since the downside is tiny while the upside is enormous, most people would strongly prefer the second game.
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. First game played once or $100$ times
>
> Now return to the original payoff structure:
>
> - heads: $\text{\textdollar}20$,
> - tails: $-\text{\textdollar}5$.
>
> Let $X_1,\dots,X_{100}$ be independent plays of this game, each with expected value
>
> $$
> E[X_i]=7.5.
> $$
>
> If the game is played once, expected total payoff is
>
> $$
> E[X_1]=7.5.
> $$
>
> If the game is played $100$ times, total payoff is
>
> $$
> S_{100}=X_1+\cdots+X_{100},
> $$
>
> and by linearity of expectation,
>
> $$
> E[S_{100}]=100\cdot 7.5=750.
> $$
>
> So the expected gain from $100$ plays is much larger:
>
> $$
> \$750 \quad \text{vs.} \quad \$7.50.
> $$
>
> Hence, under an expected-value criterion, one should prefer playing $100$ times.
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. Why $100$ times is also less risky per play average
>
> Although the total dollar fluctuation is larger over $100$ plays, the **average payoff per play** becomes more predictable.
>
> For one play, the payoff is either $\text{\textdollar}20$ or $-\text{\textdollar}5$.
>
> For many independent plays, the average payoff
>
> $$
> \overline{X}_{100}=\frac{S_{100}}{100}
> $$
>
> tends to be close to its expectation $7.5$ by the law of large numbers.
>
> In fact, if there are $H$ heads in $100$ flips, then
>
> $$
> S_{100}=20H-5(100-H)=25H-500.
> $$
>
> To lose money overall, one would need
>
> $$
> 25H-500<0
> \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
> H<20.
> $$
>
> So after $100$ plays, one loses money only if the number of heads is at most $19$.
>
> Since $H\sim \mathrm{Binomial}(100,\tfrac12)$, this probability is extremely small:
>
> $$
> P(S_{100}<0)=P(H\le 19),
> $$
>
> which is negligible.
>
> By contrast, if you play once, the probability of losing money is
>
> $$
> \frac12.
> $$
>
> Thus:
>
> - **one play:** expected gain $\text{\textdollar}7.50$, but $50\%$ chance of losing,
> - **$100$ plays:** expected gain $\text{\textdollar}750$, and the probability of an overall loss is extremely small.
>
> So if you can afford the temporary fluctuations and the plays are independent, $100$ plays is preferable.
>
> ---
>
> ## Final answer
>
> 1. For the game $\text{\textdollar}20$ on heads and $-\text{\textdollar}5$ on tails,
>
> $$
> E[X]=\frac12(20)+\frac12(-5)=\$7.50,
> $$
>
> so it is a good game in expectation.
>
> 2. For the game $\text{\textdollar}20{,}000{,}000$ on heads and $-\text{\textdollar}5$ on tails,
>
> $$
> E[Y]=\frac12(20{,}000{,}000)+\frac12(-5)=\$9{,}999{,}997.50,
> $$
>
> so it is overwhelmingly preferable to the first game, assuming you can play it once and the payoff is real.
>
> 3. For the original game, playing $100$ times is preferable to playing once, because
>
> $$
> E[S_{100}]=100\cdot 7.5=\$750,
> $$
>
> and the chance of an overall loss becomes very small, whereas one play has a $50\%$ chance of losing.
>
> Therefore, under standard expected-value reasoning, the preferences are:
>
> $$
> \text{second game} \;>\; \text{first game},
> \qquad
> 100 \text{ plays of the first game} \;>\; 1 \text{ play}.
> $$
>
> The only qualification is that a person with unusual risk aversion or severe wealth constraints might answer differently.
