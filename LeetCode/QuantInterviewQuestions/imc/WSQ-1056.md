> [!example] Question 1056 · IMC
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

A drunk passenger boards an airplane and sits in a random seat. Each subsequent passenger sits in their assigned seat if it is available; otherwise, they choose a random unoccupied seat. What is the probability that the last passenger will be able to sit in their assigned seat?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=1057)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let the passengers be numbered $1,2,\dots,n$, and let seat $i$ be assigned to passenger $i$. Passenger $1$ is the drunk and chooses a seat uniformly at random.
>
> We seek the probability that passenger $n$ gets seat $n$.
>
> ## Key observation
>
> After passenger $1$ sits, every later passenger behaves deterministically unless their own seat has already been taken. The only seats that matter are:
>
> - seat $1$, and
> - seat $n$.
>
> Why? Because whenever some passenger $k$ with $2 \le k \le n-1$ finds their own seat occupied, all earlier passengers $2,\dots,k-1$ must already be in their own seats, so the occupied “wrong” seat must have come from the chain started by passenger $1$. Passenger $k$ then chooses randomly among the remaining seats, and the process continues in the same form.
>
> Thus the process ends when one of two things happens first:
>
> 1. someone chooses seat $1$, or
> 2. someone chooses seat $n$.
>
> - If seat $n$ is chosen first, then passenger $n$ cannot sit in their own seat.
> - If seat $1$ is chosen first, then seat $n$ remains available until passenger $n$ arrives, so passenger $n$ gets their own seat.
>
> So the desired probability is exactly the probability that seat $1$ is chosen before seat $n$.
>
> ## Symmetry argument
>
> At every random choice stage, among the special seats still available, seat $1$ and seat $n$ play completely symmetric roles. There is no bias toward either one. Therefore the probability that seat $1$ is chosen first is the same as the probability that seat $n$ is chosen first.
>
> Since one of these two events must occur first, their probabilities sum to $1$. Hence each has probability
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{2}.
> $$
>
> Therefore, the probability that the last passenger sits in their assigned seat is
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{1}{2}}.
> $$
>
> ## Final answer
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{1}{2}}
> $$
