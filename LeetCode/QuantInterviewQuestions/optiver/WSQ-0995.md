> [!example] Question 995 · Optiver
> ## Trader Interview

You have 3 cards, each labeled with consecutive integers n, n+1, and n+2, where n is unknown. All cards are placed face down. On your turn, you flip one card. After seeing its value, you can either 'stay' and keep that value, or 'flip' another card. If you choose to flip another card, you can again choose to 'stay' and keep that value, or flip the final card and keep its value. Design the optimal strategy for maximizing your expected value.

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=996)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> Let the three card values be
> $$
> n,\quad n+1,\quad n+2,
> $$
> in unknown order, where $n$ is unknown.
>
> The key difficulty is that the absolute value seen on a card does not tell us whether it is the smallest, middle, or largest card, because $n$ is unknown. Thus the strategy must depend only on what can be inferred from the relative possibilities.
>
> We determine the optimal decision rule by backward induction.
>
> ---
>
> ## Step 1: Decision at the second card
>
> Suppose you did not stop after the first card, and now you have revealed two cards.
>
> Let the second revealed value be $x$. Since the three cards are consecutive integers, after seeing two distinct values there are only two possibilities for the third card:
>
> - If the two seen cards differ by $1$, then the unseen card is one step beyond them.
> - If the two seen cards differ by $2$, then the unseen card is the middle value.
>
> We compare the value of stopping at the second card with the expected value of continuing to the third.
>
> ### Case 1: The first two revealed cards differ by $2$
>
> Then they must be $n$ and $n+2$, so the third card is certainly
> $$
> n+1.
> $$
> If the second card is $n+2$, then stopping gives $n+2$, while continuing gives $n+1$, so stop.
>
> If the second card is $n$, then stopping gives $n$, while continuing gives $n+1$, so continue.
>
> Thus in this case, one should stop exactly when the second card is the larger of the two seen so far.
>
> ### Case 2: The first two revealed cards differ by $1$
>
> Then the unseen card is equally likely to be one below the smaller seen card or one above the larger seen card.
>
> If the two seen cards are $k$ and $k+1$, then the third card is equally likely to be
> $$
> k-1 \quad \text{or} \quad k+2.
> $$
> Hence its expected value is
> $$
> \frac{(k-1)+(k+2)}{2} = k+\frac12.
> $$
>
> Now compare with the second card:
>
> - If the second card is the smaller one, $x=k$, then continuing has expected value
>   $$
>   k+\frac12 > k,
>   $$
>   so continue.
>
> - If the second card is the larger one, $x=k+1$, then continuing has expected value
>   $$
>   k+\frac12 < k+1,
>   $$
>   so stop.
>
> Thus again, the optimal rule at the second card is to stop exactly when it is larger than the first.
>
> **Rule: stop at the second card if it is larger than the first; otherwise continue to the third card and keep whatever it shows.**
>
> ---
>
> ## Step 2: Decision at the first card
>
> Now suppose the first revealed card has value $x$.
>
> Because $n$ is unknown, $x$ is equally likely to be playing any of the three roles among the consecutive labels — smallest, middle, or largest — each with probability $\tfrac13$. To decide whether to keep $x$ or continue, compute the expected result of continuing (applying the Step 1 rule to the remaining two cards) under each role, then average.
>
> ### If $x$ is the smallest card ($n=x$)
>
> The remaining two cards are $x+1$ and $x+2$, in one of two equally likely orders. Since both remaining values exceed $x$, the Step 1 rule always stops at the second card, whichever of $x+1,x+2$ it happens to show. The expected result of continuing is
> $$
> \frac{(x+1)+(x+2)}{2}=x+\frac32.
> $$
>
> ### If $x$ is the middle card ($n=x-1$)
>
> The remaining two cards are $x-1$ and $x+1$, in one of two equally likely orders:
>
> - if the second card is $x-1$, which is smaller than $x$, continue to the third card, which must then be $x+1$;
> - if the second card is $x+1$, which is larger than $x$, stop at the second card, obtaining $x+1$.
>
> Either way the result is $x+1$, so the expected result of continuing is
> $$
> x+1.
> $$
>
> ### If $x$ is the largest card ($n=x-2$)
>
> The remaining two cards are $x-2$ and $x-1$, in one of two equally likely orders. Since both remaining values are below $x$, the Step 1 rule always continues to the third card, whichever of $x-2,x-1$ it happens to show. The expected result of continuing is
> $$
> \frac{(x-2)+(x-1)}{2}=x-\frac32.
> $$
>
> ### Averaging over the three roles
>
> Since the three roles are equally likely, the expected result of continuing, given only that the first card showed $x$, is
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[\text{continue}\mid x]=\frac13\left[\left(x+\frac32\right)+(x+1)+\left(x-\frac32\right)\right]=\frac13(3x+1)=x+\frac13.
> $$
>
> Since
> $$
> x+\frac13>x
> $$
> for every $x$, continuing is always strictly better than stopping at the first card, by the same margin $\tfrac13$ no matter what $x$ is. Hence you should **never stop after the first card**.
>
> ---
>
> ## Optimal strategy and expected value
>
> Combining the two steps, the optimal strategy is:
>
> 1. **Always reject the first card.**
> 2. After revealing the second card:
>    - **stop** and keep it if it is larger than the first;
>    - **otherwise continue** to the third card and keep whatever value it shows.
>
> To find the overall expected payoff, average the three role-conditional results from Step 2 over the true value of the first card, which is $n$, $n+1$, or $n+2$, each with probability $\tfrac13$:
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[\text{payoff}]=\frac13\left[\left(n+\frac32\right)+(n+2)+\left(n+\frac12\right)\right]=\frac13(3n+4)=n+\frac43.
> $$
>
> For comparison, always stopping at the first card would yield expected value
> $$
> \frac{n+(n+1)+(n+2)}{3}=n+1,
> $$
> so the optimal strategy improves the expectation by exactly
> $$
> \left(n+\frac43\right)-(n+1)=\frac13,
> $$
> matching the margin found in Step 2.
>
> ## Final answer
>
> $$
> \boxed{\text{Always flip a second card. Keep the second card iff it is larger than the first; otherwise flip the third card and keep it.}}
> $$
>
> The resulting maximal expected value is
>
> $$
> \boxed{E = n+\frac43.}
> $$
