> [!example] Question 965 · Optiver
> ## Quant Researcher Interview

You throw a standard die repeatedly, keeping a running total of the sums. What is the probability that when the running total exceeds 100 for the first time, the last die throw is a 2?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=966)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let $S_n$ denote the running total after $n$ rolls of a fair six-sided die, and let $X_n$ be the value shown on the $n$-th roll. Let
>
> $$
> T=\min\{n:S_n> 100\}
> $$
>
> be the first time the running total exceeds $100$. We want $\Pr(X_T=2)$, the probability that the roll which first pushes the total past $100$ shows a $2$.
>
> ## Step 1: A recursion in the deficit
>
> For a current total $n\le 100$, write $m=100-n$ for the deficit still needed to exceed $100$. From deficit $m$, a roll of $k$:
>
> - crosses the threshold immediately if $k>m$, succeeding if $k=2$ and failing otherwise,
> - otherwise leaves a new deficit $m-k$, and the game continues.
>
> So if $a_m$ denotes the probability of eventual success (last roll equal to $2$) starting from deficit $m$, then $a_m$ satisfies a fixed linear recursion in terms of $a_{m-1},\dots,a_{m-6}$, with the immediate-crossing case above supplying the boundary terms. In principle $a_m$ could be computed exactly for every $m$ from $0$ up to $100$ this way, but carrying out $100$ steps by hand is unnecessary. Because the largest possible roll is $6$, while the deficit at the very start of the process is $100$, the deficit process has long since forgotten how it started by the time it reaches the region near the threshold. The distribution of $X_T$ is therefore, to overwhelming precision, equal to its long-run distribution, which has a clean closed form.
>
> ## Step 2: The long-run distribution via length-biased sampling
>
> Picture the rolls as laying consecutive intervals of lengths $X_1,X_2,X_3,\dots$ end to end on the number line, starting at $0$. The threshold $100$ falls inside exactly one such interval, $(S_{T-1},S_T]$, and the length of that interval is exactly $X_T$, the quantity whose distribution we need.
>
> This is the classical inspection paradox: although each roll independently takes each value in $\{1,\dots,6\}$ with probability $\tfrac16$, a longer interval covers more of the line, so it is more likely to contain a fixed distant point, in direct proportion to its length. The probability that the interval covering such a point has length $r$ is
>
> $$
> \Pr(X_T=r) \approx \frac{r\cdot\Pr(X=r)}{E[X]},
> $$
>
> where $X$ is a single die roll and
>
> $$
> E[X]=\frac{1+2+3+4+5+6}{6}=3.5.
> $$
>
> Since $\Pr(X=r)=\tfrac16$ for each $r$, this gives
>
> $$
> \Pr(X_T=r)\approx\frac{r/6}{3.5}=\frac{r}{21},\qquad r=1,\dots,6.
> $$
>
> These six probabilities increase with $r$ and sum to $1$:
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{21}+\frac{2}{21}+\frac{3}{21}+\frac{4}{21}+\frac{5}{21}+\frac{6}{21}=1.
> $$
>
> ## Step 3: Evaluate at $r=2$
>
> Setting $r=2$,
>
> $$
> \Pr(X_T=2)\approx\frac{2}{21}.
> $$
>
> ## Final answer
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{2}{21}}
> $$
