> [!example] Question 997 · Optiver
> ## Trader Interview

Given \$1, \$5, \$20, and \$100 dollar bills, what is the fewest number of bills needed to form amounts ranging from \$1 to \$100? For example, \$4 would require four \$1 bills. Bills can be reused, so the number is not cumulative. What is the minimum number of bills you must have to always provide exact change for both a transaction of \$x and \$(100-x), where x \< 100?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=998)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> Let the available bill denominations be $1$, $5$, $20$, and $100$ dollars. We seek the smallest number of bills needed so that for every integer amount from $1$ to $100$, we can form that amount exactly.
>
> Because bills may be reused from one transaction to another, this is a **separate minimization for each amount**, not a cumulative inventory question.
>
> ### 1. Fewest bills needed to form each amount up to $100$
>
> To minimize the number of bills for a given amount, use the largest denominations possible.
>
> - For amounts $1$ to $4$, only $1$-dollar bills can be used, so the worst case is
>   $$
>   4 = 1+1+1+1,
>   $$
>   requiring $4$ bills.
>
> - For amounts $5$ to $19$, one $5$-dollar bill is useful, and the worst case is
>   $$
>   19 = 5+5+5+1+1+1+1,
>   $$
>   which uses $7$ bills.
>
> - For amounts $20$ to $99$, use as many $20$-dollar bills as possible, then $5$'s, then $1$'s. The largest bill count occurs when the remainder modulo $20$ is expensive to make.  
>   The most expensive remainder is $19$, since
>   $$
>   19 = 5+5+5+1+1+1+1
>   $$
>   uses $7$ bills.
>
>   Thus the worst amount below $100$ is
>   $$
>   99 = 20+20+20+20+5+5+5+1+1+1+1,
>   $$
>   which uses
>   $$
>   4+3+4=11
>   $$
>   bills.
>
> - For $100$, one $100$-dollar bill suffices.
>
> Hence the maximum, over all amounts from $1$ to $100$, is $11$ bills.
>
> So the fewest number of bills needed to be able to form **any single amount** from $1$ to $100$ is
>
> $$
> \boxed{11}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### 2. Fewest bills needed to make both $x$ and $100-x$
>
> Now we want the smallest number of bills sufficient so that for every $x<100$, we can make **both** amounts $x$ and $100-x$ exactly.
>
> Since $x$ and $100-x$ are formed separately, the required number of bills is
>
> $$
> \max_{1 \le x \le 99} \left( b(x) + b(100-x) \right),
> $$
>
> where $b(n)$ is the minimum number of bills needed to make $n$.
>
> We already know how to minimize $b(n)$ using the largest denominations.
>
> Consider a candidate worst case:
> $$
> x=19,\qquad 100-x=81.
> $$
>
> Then
> $$
> 19 = 5+5+5+1+1+1+1
> $$
> uses $7$ bills, and
> $$
> 81 = 20+20+20+20+1
> $$
> uses $5$ bills.
>
> So together this requires
> $$
> 7+5=12
> $$
> bills.
>
> Similarly,
> $$
> x=81,\qquad 100-x=19
> $$
> also requires $12$ bills.
>
> To see that no larger total is possible, note:
>
> - Any amount below $20$ requires at most $7$ bills.
> - If one of $x$ and $100-x$ is below $20$, the other is above $80$, and any amount from $80$ to $99$ requires at most $5$ bills.
> - If both are at least $20$, neither can require more than $6$ bills in a pair summing to $100$.
>
> Thus the maximum total is $12$.
>
> Therefore the fewest number of bills needed to always have exact change for both $x$ and $100-x$ is
>
> $$
> \boxed{12}.
> $$
>
> ## Final Answers
>
> - For any single amount from $1$ to $100$:  
>   $$
>   \boxed{11}
>   $$
>
> - To be able to make both $x$ and $100-x$ for every $x<100$:  
>   $$
>   \boxed{12}
>   $$
