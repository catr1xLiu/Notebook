> ## Software Engineer Interview

Given two sorted arrays, find the median of the combined arrays. Explain your algorithm and discuss how you would handle edge cases.

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=929)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Median of Two Sorted Arrays
>
> Let the two sorted arrays be $A$ and $B$, with lengths $m$ and $n$. The goal is to find the median of the multiset formed by combining them, in time better than simply merging them.
>
> The standard optimal solution runs in $O(\log(\min(m,n)))$ time using binary search on a partition.
>
> ---
>
> ## Core idea
>
> We want to partition the two arrays into a left half and a right half such that:
>
> - every element in the left half is $\le$ every element in the right half, and
> - the left half contains exactly half the total elements (or one extra when the total length is odd).
>
> Suppose we choose a partition in $A$ after index $i$, and a partition in $B$ after index $j$, so that
>
> $$
> i + j = \left\lfloor \frac{m+n+1}{2} \right\rfloor.
> $$
>
> Then:
>
> - left side consists of $A[0 \dots i-1]$ and $B[0 \dots j-1]$,
> - right side consists of $A[i \dots m-1]$ and $B[j \dots n-1]$.
>
> We need the partition to satisfy
>
> $$
> A[i-1] \le B[j]
> \quad \text{and} \quad
> B[j-1] \le A[i].
> $$
>
> If this holds, then the median is determined immediately.
>
> ---
>
> ## Why binary search works
>
> If $A[i-1] > B[j]$, then the partition in $A$ is too far right, so we must decrease $i$.
>
> If $B[j-1] > A[i]$, then the partition in $A$ is too far left, so we must increase $i$.
>
> Thus we can binary search on $i$.
>
> To keep the search range small and avoid invalid indices as much as possible, always binary search on the shorter array. So if $m > n$, swap the arrays first.
>
> ---
>
> ## Step-by-step algorithm
>
> Assume $m \le n$.
>
> Set
>
> $$
> \text{low} = 0, \qquad \text{high} = m.
> $$
>
> While $\text{low} \le \text{high}$:
>
> 1. Choose
>
>    $$
>    i = \left\lfloor \frac{\text{low}+\text{high}}{2} \right\rfloor.
>    $$
>
> 2. Compute
>
>    $$
>    j = \left\lfloor \frac{m+n+1}{2} \right\rfloor - i.
>    $$
>
> 3. Define boundary values:
>    - left of $A$:
>      $$
>      A_{\text{left}} =
>      \begin{cases}
>      -\infty, & i = 0,\\
>      A[i-1], & i > 0,
>      \end{cases}
>      $$
>    - right of $A$:
>      $$
>      A_{\text{right}} =
>      \begin{cases}
>      +\infty, & i = m,\\
>      A[i], & i < m,
>      \end{cases}
>      $$
>    - left of $B$:
>      $$
>      B_{\text{left}} =
>      \begin{cases}
>      -\infty, & j = 0,\\
>      B[j-1], & j > 0,
>      \end{cases}
>      $$
>    - right of $B$:
>      $$
>      B_{\text{right}} =
>      \begin{cases}
>      +\infty, & j = n,\\
>      B[j], & j < n.
>      \end{cases}
>      $$
>
> 4. Check whether the partition is valid:
>    $$
>    A_{\text{left}} \le B_{\text{right}}
>    \quad \text{and} \quad
>    B_{\text{left}} \le A_{\text{right}}.
>    $$
>
>    - If true, then:
>      - if $m+n$ is odd, the median is
>        $$
>        \max(A_{\text{left}}, B_{\text{left}});
>        $$
>      - if $m+n$ is even, the median is
>        $$
>        \frac{\max(A_{\text{left}}, B_{\text{left}}) + \min(A_{\text{right}}, B_{\text{right}})}{2}.
>        $$
>
>    - If
>      $$
>      A_{\text{left}} > B_{\text{right}},
>      $$
>      move left:
>      $$
>      \text{high} = i - 1.
>      $$
>
>    - Otherwise move right:
>      $$
>      \text{low} = i + 1.
>      $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Edge cases
>
> The interviewer’s request for edge cases is reasonable, because this problem is mostly about getting the partition logic exactly right.
>
> ### 1. One array is empty
>
> If $m = 0$, then the median is just the median of $B$.
>
> The partition method still works because:
>
> - $A_{\text{left}} = -\infty$,
> - $A_{\text{right}} = +\infty$,
>
> so the answer comes entirely from $B$.
>
> ### 2. Partition at the beginning of an array
>
> If $i = 0$, then there is nothing on the left side of $A$, so use
>
> $$
> A_{\text{left}} = -\infty.
> $$
>
> Similarly, if $j = 0$, use
>
> $$
> B_{\text{left}} = -\infty.
> $$
>
> This prevents out-of-bounds access and makes the comparison logic uniform.
>
> ### 3. Partition at the end of an array
>
> If $i = m$, then there is nothing on the right side of $A$, so use
>
> $$
> A_{\text{right}} = +\infty.
> $$
>
> Similarly, if $j = n$, use
>
> $$
> B_{\text{right}} = +\infty.
> $$
>
> Again this avoids special-case branching during comparison.
>
> ### 4. Total number of elements is odd
>
> If $m+n$ is odd, the left partition contains one more element than the right partition. Therefore the median is the maximum of the two left-side boundary values:
>
> $$
> \max(A_{\text{left}}, B_{\text{left}}).
> $$
>
> ### 5. Total number of elements is even
>
> If $m+n$ is even, the median is the average of the largest value on the left and the smallest value on the right:
>
> $$
> \frac{\max(A_{\text{left}}, B_{\text{left}}) + \min(A_{\text{right}}, B_{\text{right}})}{2}.
> $$
>
> ### 6. Duplicate values
>
> Duplicates cause no difficulty. The partition condition uses $\le$, not $<$, so equal values are handled naturally.
>
> ### 7. Very different array sizes
>
> This is exactly why we binary search only the shorter array. It keeps the running time
>
> $$
> O(\log(\min(m,n))).
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Example
>
> Let
>
> $$
> A = [1,3,8], \qquad B = [7,9,10,11].
> $$
>
> Then $m=3$, $n=4$, and
>
> $$
> \left\lfloor \frac{m+n+1}{2} \right\rfloor = 4.
> $$
>
> Try $i=1$, so $j=3$.
>
> Then:
>
> - $A_{\text{left}} = 1$,
> - $A_{\text{right}} = 3$,
> - $B_{\text{left}} = 10$,
> - $B_{\text{right}} = 11$.
>
> Since
>
> $$
> B_{\text{left}} = 10 > 3 = A_{\text{right}},
> $$
>
> the partition in $A$ is too far left. Increase $i$.
>
> Try $i=2$, so $j=2$.
>
> Then:
>
> - $A_{\text{left}} = 3$,
> - $A_{\text{right}} = 8$,
> - $B_{\text{left}} = 9$,
> - $B_{\text{right}} = 10$.
>
> Again
>
> $$
> 9 > 8,
> $$
>
> so increase $i$.
>
> Try $i=3$, so $j=1$.
>
> Then:
>
> - $A_{\text{left}} = 8$,
> - $A_{\text{right}} = +\infty$,
> - $B_{\text{left}} = 7$,
> - $B_{\text{right}} = 9$.
>
> Now
>
> $$
> 8 \le 9
> \quad \text{and} \quad
> 7 \le +\infty,
> $$
>
> so the partition is valid.
>
> Since $m+n=7$ is odd, the median is
>
> $$
> \max(8,7) = 8.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Pseudocode
>
> ```text
> function findMedianSortedArrays(A, B):
>     if length(A) > length(B):
>         swap A, B
>
>     m = length(A)
>     n = length(B)
>     low = 0
>     high = m
>
>     while low <= high:
>         i = floor((low + high) / 2)
>         j = floor((m + n + 1) / 2) - i
>
>         Aleft  = -infinity if i == 0 else A[i - 1]
>         Aright = +infinity if i == m else A[i]
>         Bleft  = -infinity if j == 0 else B[j - 1]
>         Bright = +infinity if j == n else B[j]
>
>         if Aleft <= Bright and Bleft <= Aright:
>             if (m + n) % 2 == 1:
>                 return max(Aleft, Bleft)
>             else:
>                 return (max(Aleft, Bleft) + min(Aright, Bright)) / 2
>
>         else if Aleft > Bright:
>             high = i - 1
>         else:
>             low = i + 1
> ```
>
> ---
>
> ## Complexity
>
> Because binary search is performed on the shorter array, the time complexity is
>
> $$
> O(\log(\min(m,n))),
> $$
>
> and the space complexity is
>
> $$
> O(1).
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Final answer
>
> The optimal way to find the median of two sorted arrays is to binary search a partition on the smaller array so that the combined left half and right half satisfy
>
> $$
> A_{\text{left}} \le B_{\text{right}}
> \quad \text{and} \quad
> B_{\text{left}} \le A_{\text{right}}.
> $$
>
> Once that partition is found:
>
> - if the total length is odd, return
>   $$
>   \max(A_{\text{left}}, B_{\text{left}});
>   $$
> - if the total length is even, return
>   $$
>   \frac{\max(A_{\text{left}}, B_{\text{left}}) + \min(A_{\text{right}}, B_{\text{right}})}{2}.
>   $$
>
> This handles all edge cases cleanly by treating missing boundary elements as $-\infty$ or $+\infty$.
