> [!example] Question 908 · Optiver
> ## Graduate Trader Interview

If I roll a die and add each result to a running total, which die face value am I most likely to roll on the final roll that brings the running total to at least 1000?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=909)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let $S_n$ be the running total after $n$ rolls of a fair six-sided die, and let $X_n$ denote the value shown on the $n$-th roll. Let
>
> $$
> T=\min\{n:S_n\ge 1000\}
> $$
>
> be the first time the total reaches or exceeds $1000$. We want the most likely value of the face shown on roll $T$ — that is, the most likely value of $X_T$, the die roll that causes the total to first reach at least $1000$.
>
> ## Step 1: Reduce to the interval containing the threshold
>
> Think of the rolls as laying consecutive intervals of lengths $X_1, X_2, X_3, \dots$ end to end along the number line, starting at $0$: the first interval occupies $(0,S_1]$, the second occupies $(S_1,S_2]$, and so on. The threshold $1000$ falls inside exactly one of these intervals, namely $(S_{T-1},S_T]$, whose length is exactly $X_T$, the face value whose distribution we want.
>
> Because every interval has length at most $6$, and $1000$ is far larger than $6$, the process has long since lost any memory of where it started; the interval covering $1000$ behaves just like the interval covering a uniformly chosen distant point on the line.
>
> ## Step 2: Length-biased sampling of the covering interval
>
> This is an instance of the classical inspection paradox. If intervals of lengths $1,2,\dots,6$ occur with equal frequency $\tfrac16$ each, a longer interval covers more of the number line, so a fixed distant point is more likely to fall inside a long interval than a short one, in direct proportion to the interval's length. Precisely, the probability that the interval covering a distant point has length $r$ is
>
> $$
> \Pr(X_T = r) \approx \frac{r\cdot \Pr(X=r)}{E[X]},
> $$
>
> where $X$ is one die roll and
>
> $$
> E[X]=\frac{1+2+3+4+5+6}{6}=3.5.
> $$
>
> Since $\Pr(X=r) = \tfrac16$ for each $r$, this gives
>
> $$
> \Pr(X_T=r) \approx \frac{r/6}{3.5}=\frac{r}{21}, \qquad r=1,\dots,6.
> $$
>
> ## Step 3: Identify the most likely face
>
> Substituting $r=1,\dots,6$ gives
>
> $$
> \Pr(X_T=1),\dots,\Pr(X_T=6) \approx \frac{1}{21},\frac{2}{21},\frac{3}{21},\frac{4}{21},\frac{5}{21},\frac{6}{21}.
> $$
>
> These probabilities sum to $1$ and are strictly increasing in $r$:
>
> $$
> \Pr(X_T=1)<\Pr(X_T=2)<\cdots<\Pr(X_T=6).
> $$
>
> So the most likely face on the final roll is the largest one, $r=6$, with probability
>
> $$
> \frac{6}{21}=\frac{2}{7}.
> $$
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> The die face most likely to appear on the roll that first brings the running total to at least $1000$ is
>
> $$
> \boxed{6},
> $$
>
> occurring with probability
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{2}{7}}.
> $$
>
> This matches intuition: a larger face value is more likely to be the one that finally pushes the total across a high threshold, in the same way that a longer interval between arrivals is more likely to be the one a random observer finds themselves inside.
