> [!example] Question 967 · Optiver
> ## Graduate Quantitative Researcher Interview

Provide a formal proof for the following: You have two groups of marbles—one group with 1 marble and the other with 2^n marbles. At each step, you can double the size of one group by taking marbles from the other group. Is it possible to reach a state where one group has 0 marbles and the other has 2^n + 1 marbles?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=968)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> Let the two groups contain $a$ and $b$ marbles, with initial state
>
> $$
> (a,b)=(1,2^n).
> $$
>
> At each step, one is allowed to **double one group by taking the same number of marbles from the other group**. Thus a move has one of the following forms:
>
> - if $a \ge b$, one may double $b$, giving
>   $$
>   (a,b)\mapsto (a-b,\,2b);
>   $$
> - if $b \ge a$, one may double $a$, giving
>   $$
>   (a,b)\mapsto (2a,\,b-a).
>   $$
>
> In either case, the total number of marbles is preserved:
>
> $$
> a+b=2^n+1.
> $$
>
> We must determine whether it is possible to reach
>
> $$
> (0,2^n+1)\quad\text{or}\quad (2^n+1,0).
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Key invariant: the odd part of the greatest common divisor
>
> For a positive integer $x$, write $x=2^e\cdot h$ with $h$ odd, and call $h=\mathrm{odd}(x)$ the **odd part** of $x$. We claim that
>
> $$
> \mathrm{odd}\big(\gcd(a,b)\big)
> $$
>
> is preserved by every allowed move. Note that the gcd itself is *not* generally preserved by this kind of move — for instance $(3,1)\mapsto(2,2)$ changes $\gcd(3,1)=1$ into $\gcd(2,2)=2$ — but its odd part is, because doubling one entry can only ever introduce extra factors of $2$, never remove or add an odd factor.
>
> To see this precisely, consider the move $(a,b)\mapsto(a-b,2b)$, valid when $a\ge b$ (the other move is symmetric, with the roles of $a$ and $b$ exchanged). Let
>
> $$
> g=\gcd(a,b).
> $$
>
> Since $\gcd(a-b,b)=\gcd(a,b)=g$ is a standard property of the gcd, we may write
>
> $$
> a-b=gp,\qquad b=gq,\qquad \gcd(p,q)=1.
> $$
>
> Then
>
> $$
> \gcd(a-b,2b)=\gcd(gp,2gq)=g\cdot\gcd(p,2q).
> $$
>
> Because $\gcd(p,q)=1$, the factor $q$ contributes nothing to $\gcd(p,2q)$ beyond what $2$ itself contributes, so
>
> $$
> \gcd(p,2q)=\gcd(p,2).
> $$
>
> This equals $1$ if $p$ is odd, or $2$ if $p$ is even. Hence
>
> $$
> \gcd(a-b,2b)=g \quad\text{if } p \text{ is odd},
> \qquad
> \gcd(a-b,2b)=2g \quad\text{if } p \text{ is even}.
> $$
>
> In either case, the odd part is unaffected: doubling $g$ to $2g$ only increases the power of $2$ it carries, without changing $\mathrm{odd}(g)$. So
>
> $$
> \mathrm{odd}\big(\gcd(a-b,2b)\big)=\mathrm{odd}(g)=\mathrm{odd}\big(\gcd(a,b)\big).
> $$
>
> The same argument, with $a$ and $b$ interchanged, applies to the move $(a,b)\mapsto(2a,b-a)$. Hence $\mathrm{odd}(\gcd(a,b))$ is invariant under every allowed move.
>
> Since initially
>
> $$
> \gcd(1,2^n)=1,
> $$
>
> which is already odd, every reachable state must satisfy
>
> $$
> \mathrm{odd}\big(\gcd(a,b)\big)=1.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Reachability of a terminal state
>
> Suppose we could reach a state with all marbles in one group, say
>
> $$
> (0,2^n+1).
> $$
>
> Then the gcd of the two groups would be
>
> $$
> \gcd(0,2^n+1)=2^n+1,
> $$
>
> and likewise $\gcd(2^n+1,0)=2^n+1$. Since $2^n+1$ is itself odd for every $n\ge 1$, its odd part is $2^n+1$ itself:
>
> $$
> \mathrm{odd}\big(\gcd(0,2^n+1)\big)=2^n+1.
> $$
>
> But the invariant requires this odd part to equal $1$, while $2^n+1>1$ for every $n\ge 1$. This is a contradiction.
>
> Therefore such a terminal state cannot be reached.
>
> ---
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> For every $n\ge 1$, it is **not possible** to reach a state with $0$ marbles in one group and $2^n+1$ marbles in the other.
>
> Thus the answer is:
>
> $$
> \boxed{\text{No, it is impossible.}}
> $$
