> [!example] Question 897 · Optiver
> ## Quantitative Trader Interview

Given a simulated order book, how would you determine the fair value of a derivative?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=898)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> To price a derivative at a fair value from a simulated order book, the standard approach is to infer a no-arbitrage or market-consistent value from the available bid and ask information, adjusted for execution and liquidity. The exact method depends on the derivative type and what the simulation provides, but the core framework is as follows.
>
> ## 1. Identify the relevant market inputs
>
> From the simulated order book, extract for the derivative or its underlier:
>
> - best bid price: $b_1$
> - best ask price: $a_1$
> - bid sizes and ask sizes at each level
> - full depth if large execution matters
> - timestamp and any dynamics if the simulation is multi-period
>
> If the derivative itself is quoted in the book, a natural first estimate of fair value is the midprice:
>
> $$
> V_{\text{mid}} = \frac{b_1 + a_1}{2}.
> $$
>
> This is the frictionless fair value implied by the top of book.
>
> ## 2. Adjust for liquidity if necessary
>
> If the position size is nontrivial, the top-of-book mid may be misleading. Suppose one must buy or sell quantity $Q$. Then compute the volume-weighted execution price from the order book.
>
> For a purchase of size $Q$, if the ask levels are $(a_1,q_1^{a}), (a_2,q_2^{a}), \dots$, define the execution cost
>
> $$
> P_{\text{buy}}(Q)
> =
> \frac{1}{Q}
> \sum_{i} a_i \, x_i,
> $$
>
> where $x_i$ is the amount filled at level $i$, satisfying
>
> $$
> 0 \le x_i \le q_i^{a},
> \qquad
> \sum_i x_i = Q.
> $$
>
> Similarly, for a sale of size $Q$, using bid levels $(b_1,q_1^{b}), (b_2,q_2^{b}), \dots$,
>
> $$
> P_{\text{sell}}(Q)
> =
> \frac{1}{Q}
> \sum_i b_i \, y_i,
> $$
>
> with
>
> $$
> 0 \le y_i \le q_i^{b},
> \qquad
> \sum_i y_i = Q.
> $$
>
> A liquidity-adjusted fair value for size $Q$ is then often taken as
>
> $$
> V_{\text{liq}}(Q) = \frac{P_{\text{buy}}(Q) + P_{\text{sell}}(Q)}{2}.
> $$
>
> This reflects executable value rather than merely quoted value.
>
> ## 3. If the derivative is not directly quoted, price it from the underlier
>
> If the order book is for the underlying asset and the derivative payoff is known, simulate or discount its expected payoff under a risk-neutral measure.
>
> If the derivative payoff at maturity $T$ is $H(S_T)$, then its fair value at time $0$ is
>
> $$
> V_0 = e^{-rT}\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[H(S_T)].
> $$
>
> In a simulation with $N$ risk-neutral scenarios $S_T^{(1)}, \dots, S_T^{(N)}$, estimate this by Monte Carlo:
>
> $$
> \hat V_0 = e^{-rT}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} H\bigl(S_T^{(j)}\bigr).
> $$
>
> Examples:
>
> - European call with strike $K$:
>
> $$
> H(S_T) = \max(S_T - K, 0),
> $$
>
> so
>
> $$
> \hat V_0 = e^{-rT}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} \max\bigl(S_T^{(j)} - K, 0\bigr).
> $$
>
> - European put with strike $K$:
>
> $$
> H(S_T) = \max(K - S_T, 0),
> $$
>
> so
>
> $$
> \hat V_0 = e^{-rT}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} \max\bigl(K - S_T^{(j)}, 0\bigr).
> $$
>
> ## 4. Use replication bounds from the order book
>
> If both the derivative and the underlying are quoted, the order book can be used to check arbitrage bounds.
>
> For example, for a European call on a non-dividend-paying stock,
>
> $$
> \max(S_0 - K e^{-rT}, 0) \le C_0 \le S_0.
> $$
>
> If the stock itself has bid and ask prices, then the fair derivative value should lie inside bounds implied by tradable prices. For instance, using executable prices:
>
> - buy stock at ask $a_S$
> - sell stock at bid $b_S$
>
> then the no-arbitrage bounds widen accordingly because replication uses actual execution prices, not frictionless spot.
>
> Thus, if the derivative’s quoted price lies outside those executable bounds, it is not fair.
>
> ## 5. Practical fair-value conventions
>
> In practice, a “fair value” from a simulated book is usually one of these:
>
> 1. **Mid fair value**
>    $$
>    V = \frac{b_1 + a_1}{2}.
>    $$
>
> 2. **Microprice**, which weights the best prices by opposite-side liquidity:
>    $$
>    V_{\text{micro}}
>    =
>    \frac{a_1 q_1^{b} + b_1 q_1^{a}}{q_1^{a} + q_1^{b}}.
>    $$
>    This is often preferred when the book is imbalanced.
>
> 3. **Liquidity-adjusted midpoint for size $Q$**
>    $$
>    V_{\text{liq}}(Q) = \frac{P_{\text{buy}}(Q) + P_{\text{sell}}(Q)}{2}.
>    $$
>
> 4. **Model-based fair value**
>    $$
>    V_0 = e^{-rT}\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[H(S_T)].
>    $$
>
> The appropriate choice depends on what “fair” means in the context:
> - quoted fair value $\rightarrow$ midpoint or microprice,
> - executable fair value $\rightarrow$ liquidity-adjusted value,
> - theoretical no-arbitrage fair value $\rightarrow$ discounted risk-neutral expectation.
>
> ## Final answer
>
> If the derivative itself is quoted in the simulated order book, the standard fair value is the midpoint
>
> $$
> V = \frac{b_1 + a_1}{2},
> $$
>
> or, if liquidity matters, the size-adjusted value
>
> $$
> V_{\text{liq}}(Q) = \frac{P_{\text{buy}}(Q) + P_{\text{sell}}(Q)}{2}.
> $$
>
> If instead the book is for the underlying and the derivative must be priced theoretically, use the discounted expected payoff under the risk-neutral simulation:
>
> $$
> V_0 = e^{-rT}\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[H(S_T)]
> \approx
> e^{-rT}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} H\bigl(S_T^{(j)}\bigr).
> $$
>
> Thus, the fair price is obtained either from the midpoint of tradable quotes, from a liquidity-adjusted executable midpoint, or from the discounted expected payoff, depending on the information provided by the simulation.
