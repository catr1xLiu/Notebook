> [!example] Question 938 · Optiver
> ## Trading Intern Interview

How would you calculate the final price of an asset in a trading simulation given historical trading data?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=939)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> To calculate the final price of an asset in a trading simulation using historical trading data, one typically defines a pricing rule based on the available data and then applies it to the last point of the simulation.
>
> ## 1. Identify the relevant historical price series
>
> Let the historical prices be
>
> $$
> P_1, P_2, \dots, P_T
> $$
>
> where $P_t$ is the asset price at time $t$.
>
> If the simulation ends at time $T$, the simplest final price is just the last observed market price:
>
> $$
> P_{\text{final}} = P_T
> $$
>
> ## 2. Use the last traded price
>
> In many trading simulations, the asset is marked to market using the most recent transaction price. If the historical dataset includes executed trades with prices
>
> $$
> p_1, p_2, \dots, p_n
> $$
>
> then the final asset price is
>
> $$
> P_{\text{final}} = p_n
> $$
>
> where $p_n$ is the last recorded trade price.
>
> ## 3. Use bid-ask midpoint if appropriate
>
> If the historical data includes the best bid and ask quotes at the end of the simulation, say
>
> $$
> B_T \quad \text{and} \quad A_T
> $$
>
> then a common fair-value estimate is the midpoint:
>
> $$
> P_{\text{final}} = \frac{B_T + A_T}{2}
> $$
>
> This is often preferred when no final trade occurs exactly at the simulation end.
>
> ## 4. Adjust for corporate actions if necessary
>
> If the historical data spans stock splits, dividends, or similar events, use adjusted prices. If $P_T^{\text{raw}}$ is the raw closing price and $c_T$ is the cumulative adjustment factor, then
>
> $$
> P_{\text{final}} = P_T^{\text{raw}} \cdot c_T
> $$
>
> ## 5. Compute portfolio value if needed
>
> If the simulation tracks holdings of $q$ units of the asset, then the final marked-to-market value is
>
> $$
> V_{\text{final}} = q \cdot P_{\text{final}}
> $$
>
> If there is also cash $C_T$, total final portfolio value is
>
> $$
> W_T = C_T + q \cdot P_{\text{final}}
> $$
>
> ## 6. If the simulation models returns instead of prices
>
> If the simulation evolves the asset through historical returns $r_1, r_2, \dots, r_T$, starting from initial price $P_0$, then the final price is
>
> $$
> P_T = P_0 \prod_{t=1}^{T} (1 + r_t)
> $$
>
> for simple returns, or
>
> $$
> P_T = P_0 \exp\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t\right)
> $$
>
> if $r_t$ are log-returns.
>
> ## Final answer
>
> The final price in a trading simulation is usually taken as the most recent available market valuation from the historical data, such as:
>
> - the last traded price:
>   $$
>   P_{\text{final}} = p_n
>   $$
> - or the final midpoint quote:
>   $$
>   P_{\text{final}} = \frac{B_T + A_T}{2}
>   $$
>
> If prices are reconstructed from returns, then
>
> $$
> P_T = P_0 \prod_{t=1}^{T} (1 + r_t)
> $$
>
> The exact method depends on whether the simulation uses trade prices, quotes, or return series.
