> [!example] Question 706 · Citadel
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

For the task of classifying news articles into their subject type (e.g., finance, science, politics), what is a current state-of-the-art approach? Describe this approach in detail and discuss its latency. Additionally, suggest simpler, classical models that can achieve faster throughput while maintaining high accuracy for this task.

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## State-of-the-art approach: Transformer-based text classification with a pretrained encoder
>
> A current strong approach for news-topic classification is to fine-tune a large pretrained Transformer encoder, typically a model in the BERT family such as **RoBERTa**, **DeBERTa**, or a compact modern variant such as **ModernBERT**. For many benchmark text-classification tasks, these models remain near state of the art because they combine:
>
> - strong contextual language understanding from large-scale pretraining,
> - efficient task adaptation through supervised fine-tuning,
> - high accuracy even when class boundaries depend on subtle semantics.
>
> For news classification, the input is the article text, often the title plus the first few paragraphs, or a truncated body.
>
> ### 1. Input representation
>
> Given an article with token sequence
> $$
> x = (w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n),
> $$
> the text is tokenized into subword units using a tokenizer such as byte-pair encoding or WordPiece:
> $$
> x \mapsto (t_1, t_2, \dots, t_m).
> $$
>
> A special classification token is added:
> $$
> [\mathrm{CLS}], t_1, t_2, \dots, t_m.
> $$
>
> Each token is mapped to an embedding, combined with positional information:
> $$
> h_i^{(0)} = e(t_i) + p_i.
> $$
>
> ### 2. Transformer encoder
>
> The sequence is passed through $L$ self-attention layers. At each layer, self-attention computes contextualized representations. For one attention head,
> $$
> Q = HW_Q,\quad K = HW_K,\quad V = HW_V,
> $$
> and attention is
> $$
> \mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \mathrm{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V.
> $$
>
> Multi-head attention and feed-forward blocks are stacked:
> $$
> H^{(\ell+1)} = \mathrm{TransformerBlock}\big(H^{(\ell)}\big), \quad \ell = 0,\dots,L-1.
> $$
>
> The final contextual embedding of the classification token,
> $$
> z = h_{\mathrm{CLS}}^{(L)},
> $$
> serves as the document representation.
>
> ### 3. Classification head
>
> A linear layer maps $z$ to class logits:
> $$
> s = Wz + b,
> $$
> followed by softmax probabilities:
> $$
> P(y=c \mid x) = \frac{e^{s_c}}{\sum_{j=1}^C e^{s_j}},
> $$
> where $C$ is the number of subject classes.
>
> Training minimizes cross-entropy:
> $$
> \mathcal{L} = -\sum_{i=1}^N \log P(y_i \mid x_i).
> $$
>
> ### 4. Why this works well for news classification
>
> News articles often contain topic signals that are not purely lexical. For example:
>
> - a politics article may contain economic vocabulary,
> - a science article may discuss policy,
> - a finance article may mention technology firms.
>
> Transformer encoders model long-range dependencies and word meaning in context, so they can disambiguate cases where bag-of-words methods struggle. They also handle variation in writing style and vocabulary across publishers.
>
> ### 5. Practical training recipe
>
> A strong modern setup is:
>
> - backbone: **DeBERTa-v3-base**, **RoBERTa-large**, or a similar encoder,
> - input: title + article body truncated to $256$ or $512$ tokens,
> - optimization: AdamW,
> - learning rate: typically in the range $10^{-5}$ to $5\times 10^{-5}$,
> - regularization: dropout, weight decay, early stopping,
> - class balancing if classes are skewed,
> - optional domain-adaptive pretraining on unlabeled news text before fine-tuning.
>
> For highest performance, one may also use:
>
> - **ensembles** of several fine-tuned Transformers,
> - **test-time augmentation**,
> - **knowledge distillation** into a smaller student model for deployment.
>

> ## Latency characteristics of this approach
>
> ### 1. Computational complexity
>
> For a Transformer encoder, self-attention over a sequence of length $n$ has quadratic cost:
> $$
> O(n^2 d),
> $$
> where $d$ is hidden size. This is the main latency bottleneck, especially for longer articles.
>
> If the article is truncated to $256$ or $512$ tokens, latency is manageable, but still significantly higher than classical sparse linear models.
>
> ### 2. Practical inference latency
>
> Latency depends on:
>
> - model size,
> - sequence length,
> - hardware,
> - batch size,
> - quantization and compilation,
> - whether inference runs on CPU or GPU.
>
> Typical deployment behavior:
>
> - **GPU, batched inference**: throughput can be high, but single-request latency may still be several milliseconds to tens of milliseconds for compact models and higher for larger ones.
> - **CPU, single-document inference**: latency is often tens of milliseconds to well over $100$ milliseconds for base/large models, especially at sequence lengths near $512$.
> - **Large models**: latency and memory usage may be too high for low-latency real-time systems.
>
> A useful qualitative summary is:
>
> - **excellent accuracy**,
> - **moderate to high latency**,
> - **substantially slower than linear sparse models**,
> - **latency grows noticeably with article length**.
>
> ### 3. End-to-end serving issues
>
> In production, total latency includes:
>
> - tokenization,
> - text truncation or preprocessing,
> - model inference,
> - postprocessing.
>
> For Transformer systems, tokenization is usually not the main bottleneck, but it is nontrivial. The inference stage dominates.
>
> ### 4. Distilled and compact variants
>
> If lower latency is needed while retaining much of the accuracy, teams commonly deploy:
>
> - **DistilBERT**,
> - **MiniLM**,
> - **TinyBERT**,
> - distilled versions of RoBERTa or DeBERTa.
>
> These reduce the number of layers or hidden dimensions, often preserving most of the classification accuracy while lowering latency substantially. They are often the best compromise when one still wants a neural contextual model.
>

> ## Faster classical models with high accuracy
>
> For topic classification of news, simpler models can be extremely competitive because topic labels correlate strongly with vocabulary. In such settings, classical methods can achieve very high accuracy with much lower latency.
>
> ## 1. TF-IDF + linear classifier
>
> This is the strongest classical baseline and often the best practical fast model.
>
> ### Representation
>
> Construct a sparse vector of term weights for each document:
> $$
> x_j = \mathrm{tfidf}(t_j, d),
> $$
> where each feature corresponds to a word or character $n$-gram.
>
> Using word unigrams and bigrams, and often character $n$-grams, yields strong topic signals.
>
> ### Classifier
>
> A linear classifier such as multinomial logistic regression or a linear SVM is trained:
> $$
> s = Wx + b.
> $$
>
> Prediction is
> $$
> \hat{y} = \arg\max_c s_c.
> $$
>
> ### Why it is fast
>
> The feature vector $x$ is sparse, so inference cost is proportional to the number of nonzero features:
> $$
> O(\mathrm{nnz}(x)),
> $$
> not to vocabulary size. This is extremely efficient on CPU.
>
> ### Accuracy
>
> For news-topic classification, TF-IDF with linear models is often surprisingly close to Transformer accuracy, especially when:
>
> - labels are broad topics,
> - training data is reasonably large,
> - titles and article leads contain strong topic-specific terms.
>
> ### Latency
>
> Latency is typically very low:
>
> - feature extraction is fast,
> - matrix scoring is sparse and cheap,
> - CPU throughput can be very high.
>
> For large-scale batch classification, this approach often outperforms neural models by a wide margin in documents per second.
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. FastText
>
> **FastText** is a lightweight neural text classifier that remains highly competitive for topic classification.
>
> ### Model
>
> A document is represented by averaging token or subword embeddings:
> $$
> z = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m e(t_i),
> $$
> then classified by a linear layer:
> $$
> s = Wz + b.
> $$
>
> FastText often includes hashed word and character $n$-gram features, making it robust to morphology and rare words.
>
> ### Advantages
>
> - much faster than Transformers,
> - usually more accurate than plain bag-of-words on many datasets,
> - simple training and deployment,
> - excellent CPU performance.
>
> ### Latency
>
> Inference is near-linear in document length:
> $$
> O(m),
> $$
> with very small constants. It is typically suitable for high-throughput real-time systems.
>
> ### Accuracy tradeoff
>
> For broad news categories, FastText often retains high accuracy and may be a better accuracy-speed tradeoff than heavier neural models.
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Hashing vectorizer + linear model
>
> Instead of storing a learned vocabulary, one can map tokens to a fixed-dimensional feature space using the hashing trick:
> $$
> \phi: \text{token} \mapsto \{1,\dots,D\}.
> $$
>
> Then train a linear classifier on the hashed sparse vectors.
>
> ### Benefits
>
> - no vocabulary storage,
> - streaming-friendly,
> - fast training and inference,
> - works well for very large corpora.
>
> This is especially useful when the news stream evolves continuously and the system must be updated frequently.
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. Naive Bayes and related linear baselines
>
> Multinomial Naive Bayes can be highly effective for topic classification because of strong class-conditional word distributions. Although often slightly less accurate than logistic regression or linear SVM, it is:
>
> - simple,
> - very fast,
> - robust for large sparse text data.
>
> A common strong variant is **Naive Bayes–SVM** style feature scaling, which often improves linear performance.
>
> ---
>
> ## Accuracy-speed tradeoff summary
>
> For this task, the rough ranking is typically:
>
> ### Highest accuracy
> - fine-tuned Transformer encoder such as RoBERTa or DeBERTa,
> - possibly with ensembling or domain adaptation.
>
> ### Best balanced deployment model
> - distilled Transformer such as MiniLM or DistilBERT.
>
> ### Best throughput with strong accuracy
> - TF-IDF + linear SVM or logistic regression,
> - or FastText.
>
> ### Maximum simplicity and speed
> - hashing vectorizer + linear classifier,
> - multinomial Naive Bayes.
>
> ---
>
> ## Recommended practical strategy
>
> A strong production workflow is to evaluate three tiers:
>
> ### Tier 1: Accuracy ceiling
> Train a fine-tuned Transformer:
> $$
> \text{Article} \rightarrow \text{Tokenizer} \rightarrow \text{Transformer} \rightarrow \text{Softmax}.
> $$
>
> This provides the best reference accuracy.
>
> ### Tier 2: Compressed neural deployment
> Distill the Transformer into a smaller model such as MiniLM or FastText-style student model.
>
> ### Tier 3: Classical high-throughput baseline
> Train:
> - TF-IDF with word unigrams + bigrams,
> - optionally character $3$-$5$ grams,
> - linear SVM or logistic regression.
>
> For news-topic classification, Tier 3 often delivers a large fraction of Tier 1 accuracy at a tiny fraction of the latency.
>
> ---
>
> ## Final answer
>
> A current state-of-the-art approach for classifying news articles by subject is to fine-tune a pretrained Transformer encoder such as **RoBERTa**, **DeBERTa**, or a similar modern BERT-style model, using the contextual representation of the full article (or truncated article) and a softmax classification head. This approach works well because it captures context, long-range dependencies, and subtle semantic distinctions between topics. Its main drawback is latency: self-attention has cost roughly quadratic in sequence length,
> $$
> O(n^2 d),
> $$
> so inference is much slower than classical sparse models, especially on CPU or for long articles.
>
> If lower latency and higher throughput are required, the best simpler alternatives are:
>
> - **TF-IDF + linear SVM/logistic regression**, often extremely strong for topic classification and very fast on CPU,
> - **FastText**, which is usually faster than Transformers and often maintains high accuracy,
> - **hashing vectorizer + linear classifier** for scalable streaming setups,
> - **Naive Bayes** for a very simple and fast baseline.
>
> For news-topic classification specifically, classical linear models can be surprisingly competitive because topical vocabulary is highly predictive, and they usually provide far better throughput than Transformer models while maintaining high accuracy.
