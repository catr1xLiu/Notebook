> ## Variance of a Momentum Trading Strategy
>
> Consider a simple momentum trading strategy with return
>
> $$
> R_t^{(m)} = w_t R_t,
> $$
>
> where $R_t$ is the asset return at time $t$, and $w_t$ is the position determined by past returns. A common specification is
>
> $$
> w_t = \operatorname{sign}(R_{t-1}),
> $$
>
> or, more generally, a linear signal
>
> $$
> w_t = \gamma R_{t-1},
> $$
>
> for some constant $\gamma > 0$. To analyze the variance clearly, it is convenient to use the linear form. Then the strategy return is
>
> $$
> R_t^{(m)} = \gamma R_{t-1}R_t.
> $$
>
> Assume the asset returns $\{R_t\}$ are weakly stationary with
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[R_t] = \mu, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(R_t) = \sigma^2,
> $$
>
> and autocovariance
>
> $$
> \operatorname{Cov}(R_t, R_{t-k}) = \gamma_k.
> $$
>
> In particular,
>
> $$
> \gamma_1 = \operatorname{Cov}(R_t, R_{t-1}).
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. Mean of the momentum strategy
>
> First compute the expected return:
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[R_t^{(m)}]
> = \gamma \mathbb{E}[R_{t-1}R_t].
> $$
>
> Using
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[R_{t-1}R_t] = \gamma_1 + \mu^2,
> $$
>
> we obtain
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[R_t^{(m)}] = \gamma(\gamma_1 + \mu^2).
> $$
>
> Thus momentum is profitable on average when returns exhibit positive serial dependence, that is, when $\gamma_1 > 0$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Variance of the momentum strategy
>
> By definition,
>
> $$
> \operatorname{Var}(R_t^{(m)})
> = \operatorname{Var}(\gamma R_{t-1}R_t)
> = \gamma^2 \operatorname{Var}(R_{t-1}R_t).
> $$
>
> Hence
>
> $$
> \operatorname{Var}(R_t^{(m)})
> = \gamma^2 \left( \mathbb{E}[R_{t-1}^2 R_t^2] - \big(\mathbb{E}[R_{t-1}R_t]\big)^2 \right).
> $$
>
> So the key object is the fourth cross-moment $\mathbb{E}[R_{t-1}^2 R_t^2]$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Special case: jointly Gaussian returns
>
> If $(R_{t-1}, R_t)$ are jointly normal, then Isserlis’ theorem gives
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[R_{t-1}^2 R_t^2]
> = \sigma^4 + 2\gamma_1^2
> $$
>
> when $\mu = 0$.
>
> Also,
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[R_{t-1}R_t] = \gamma_1.
> $$
>
> Therefore,
>
> $$
> \operatorname{Var}(R_t^{(m)})
> = \gamma^2 \left( \sigma^4 + 2\gamma_1^2 - \gamma_1^2 \right)
> = \gamma^2 \left( \sigma^4 + \gamma_1^2 \right).
> $$
>
> So under zero-mean jointly Gaussian returns,
>
> $$
> \boxed{\operatorname{Var}(R_t^{(m)}) = \gamma^2 \left( \sigma^4 + \gamma_1^2 \right).}
> $$
>
> This shows that the variance of the momentum strategy depends on both:
>
> - the unconditional volatility level $ \sigma^2 $, and
> - the first-order autocovariance $ \gamma_1 $.
>
> A stronger momentum effect, meaning larger $ \gamma_1 $, increases not only expected return but also variance.
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. Expression in terms of autocorrelation
>
> Let
>
> $$
> \rho_1 = \frac{\gamma_1}{\sigma^2}
> $$
>
> be the lag-$1$ autocorrelation. Then
>
> $$
> \operatorname{Var}(R_t^{(m)})
> = \gamma^2 \sigma^4 (1 + \rho_1^2).
> $$
>
> Thus the variance scales with $ \sigma^4 $ and rises with the square of the autocorrelation.
>
> ---
>
> ## 5. Nonzero mean case
>
> If returns have mean $\mu \neq 0$, then
>
> $$
> \operatorname{Var}(R_t^{(m)})
> = \gamma^2 \left( \mathbb{E}[R_{t-1}^2 R_t^2] - (\gamma_1 + \mu^2)^2 \right).
> $$
>
> For jointly Gaussian returns with equal mean $\mu$, one may expand the fourth moment to obtain
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[R_{t-1}^2 R_t^2]
> = \sigma^4 + 2\gamma_1^2 + 2\mu^2\sigma^2 + 4\mu^2\gamma_1 + \mu^4.
> $$
>
> Therefore,
>
> $$
> \operatorname{Var}(R_t^{(m)})
> = \gamma^2 \left[ \sigma^4 + \gamma_1^2 + 2\mu^2\sigma^2 + 2\mu^2\gamma_1 \right].
> $$
>
> When $\mu$ is small relative to $\sigma$, the zero-mean approximation is often adequate.
>
> ---
>
> ## 6. Interpretation
>
> The variance of a momentum strategy is larger than the variance of a simple linear exposure because the strategy return is the product of two random quantities: the signal $R_{t-1}$ and the realized return $R_t$. This creates:
>
> 1. a dependence on the fourth moments of returns,
> 2. sensitivity to volatility clustering and fat tails if returns are non-Gaussian,
> 3. an increase in variance when serial correlation becomes stronger.
>
> In particular, even if momentum has positive expected return, its risk can be substantial because high-volatility periods amplify both the signal magnitude and the realized payoff.
>
> ---
>
> ## Final result
>
> For the linear one-period momentum strategy
>
> $$
> R_t^{(m)} = \gamma R_{t-1}R_t,
> $$
>
> the variance is
>
> $$
> \boxed{
> \operatorname{Var}(R_t^{(m)})
> = \gamma^2 \left( \mathbb{E}[R_{t-1}^2 R_t^2] - \big(\mathbb{E}[R_{t-1}R_t]\big)^2 \right).
> }
> $$
>
> Under zero-mean jointly Gaussian returns,
>
> $$
> \boxed{
> \operatorname{Var}(R_t^{(m)}) = \gamma^2\left(\sigma^4 + \gamma_1^2\right)
> = \gamma^2 \sigma^4 (1+\rho_1^2).
> }
> $$
>
> This is the standard variance characterization of a simple momentum trading rule.
