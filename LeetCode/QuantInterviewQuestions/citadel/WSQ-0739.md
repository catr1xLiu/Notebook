> ### 1. Probability that the sum of two fair dice is prime
>
> The possible sums of two fair dice are $2$ through $12$. The prime sums in this range are
>
> $$
> 2,3,5,7,11.
> $$
>
> Count the number of outcomes for each:
>
> - Sum $2$: $1$ outcome
> - Sum $3$: $2$ outcomes
> - Sum $5$: $4$ outcomes
> - Sum $7$: $6$ outcomes
> - Sum $11$: $2$ outcomes
>
> Total favorable outcomes:
>
> $$
> 1+2+4+6+2=15.
> $$
>
> Since there are $36$ equally likely outcomes,
>
> $$
> P(\text{prime sum})=\frac{15}{36}=\frac{5}{12}.
> $$
>
> **Answer:** 
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{5}{12}}
> $$
>

> ### 2. Three people each flip a coin; the last person with a different result wins. Expected number of flips
>
> Interpret the game as follows: in each round, all three flip simultaneously. The game ends when exactly one person differs from the other two; that unique person is the winner.
>
> For three fair coins, the possible outcomes are:
>
> $$
> HHH,\ HHT,\ HTH,\ THH,\ TTH,\ THT,\ HTT,\ TTT.
> $$
>
> The game continues only if all three match, i.e. on
>
> $$
> HHH \text{ or } TTT,
> $$
>
> which occurs with probability
>
> $$
> \frac{2}{8}=\frac14.
> $$
>
> The game ends in a round with probability
>
> $$
> 1-\frac14=\frac34.
> $$
>
> Thus the number of rounds is geometric with success probability $\frac34$, so the expected number of rounds is
>
> $$
> E[N]=\frac{1}{3/4}=\frac43.
> $$
>
> If “expected number of flips” means total individual coin flips, then each round uses $3$ flips, so
>
> $$
> E[\text{total flips}]=3\cdot \frac43=4.
> $$
>
> **Answer:** 
>
> - Expected rounds: 
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac43}
> $$
>
> - Expected total coin flips: 
>
> $$
> \boxed{4}
> $$
>

> ### 6. Function to find arbitrage opportunities in a currency exchange matrix
>
> #### Approach
>
> Model currencies as a weighted directed graph. If the exchange rate from currency $i$ to currency $j$ is $r_{ij}$, then an arbitrage cycle exists iff there is a cycle whose product of rates exceeds $1$:
>
> $$
> r_{i_1 i_2}r_{i_2 i_3}\cdots r_{i_k i_1} > 1.
> $$
>
> Take logarithms and negate:
>
> $$
> w_{ij}=-\log r_{ij}.
> $$
>
> Then arbitrage exists iff there is a **negative cycle**, because
>
> $$
> \sum w_{ij} = -\log\left(\prod r_{ij}\right) < 0
> \iff
> \prod r_{ij}>1.
> $$
>
> Use Bellman–Ford to detect a negative cycle.
>
> #### Python implementation
>
> ```python
> import math
>
> def find_arbitrage(rates):
>     """
>     rates[i][j] = exchange rate from currency i to currency j
>     Returns a cycle of indices if arbitrage exists, else None.
>     """
>     n = len(rates)
>     edges = []
>
>     for i in range(n):
>         for j in range(n):
>             if rates[i][j] <= 0:
>                 continue
>             edges.append((i, j, -math.log(rates[i][j])))
>
>     dist = [0.0] * n
>     parent = [-1] * n
>     x = -1
>
>     for _ in range(n):
>         x = -1
>         for u, v, w in edges:
>             if dist[u] + w < dist[v]:
>                 dist[v] = dist[u] + w
>                 parent[v] = u
>                 x = v
>
>     if x == -1:
>         return None  # no arbitrage
>
>     # Recover a negative cycle
>     for _ in range(n):
>         x = parent[x]
>
>     cycle = []
>     cur = x
>     while True:
>         cycle.append(cur)
>         cur = parent[cur]
>         if cur == x and len(cycle) > 1:
>             break
>
>     cycle.reverse()
>     return cycle
> ```
>
> #### Complexity
>
> If there are $n$ currencies, there are $n^2$ edges. Bellman–Ford runs in
>
> $$
> O(n^3)
> $$
>
> time and
>
> $$
> O(n^2)
> $$
>
> space for the matrix.

> ### 7. Detect wash trading from real-time trade data
>
> #### Objective
>
> Wash trading is trading intended to create artificial volume or misleading price action, often involving the same beneficial owner trading with itself or colluding accounts.
>
> #### Signals to detect
>
> Given a stream of trades with fields such as timestamp, buyer, seller, symbol, price, size, venue, and beneficial owner:
>
> 1. **Self-trades**
>    - Buyer and seller are the same account or same beneficial owner.
>
> 2. **Near-simultaneous offsetting trades**
>    - Repeated buy and sell activity between the same pair of accounts within a short time window.
>
> 3. **No meaningful position change**
>    - Large gross volume but near-zero net position change.
>
> 4. **Abnormal repetition**
>    - Same size, same symbol, alternating direction, repeated many times.
>
> 5. **Price-insensitive behavior**
>    - Trading across the spread repeatedly with no economic rationale.
>
> 6. **Circular patterns**
>    - Account $A \to B \to C \to A$ type loops.
>
> #### Streaming design
>
> Maintain per-symbol state over sliding windows.
>
> #### Data structures
>
> - Hash map keyed by account / beneficial owner:
>   - net position
>   - gross bought and sold volume
> - Hash map keyed by ordered or unordered account pairs:
>   - recent trades count
>   - alternating pattern statistics
> - Queue or deque per symbol for recent trades in the last $T$ seconds
> - Graph of account interactions for cycle detection
>
> #### Detection logic
>
> For each incoming trade:
>
> 1. Normalize identity to beneficial owner if available.
> 2. Check whether buyer and seller match.
> 3. Update rolling position and volume statistics.
> 4. Update pairwise interaction counters.
> 5. Score suspiciousness using features such as:
>    - self-trade indicator
>    - high gross / low net ratio
>    - repeated opposite-direction trades within $\Delta t$
>    - recurring same size
>    - circular trade graph motifs
> 6. Emit alerts when score exceeds a threshold.
>
> #### Sketch of algorithm
>
> ```python
> class WashTradeDetector:
>     def __init__(self, window_seconds):
>         self.window = window_seconds
>         self.positions = {}      # account -> net position
>         self.gross = {}          # account -> [buy_vol, sell_vol]
>         self.pair_stats = {}     # (acct1, acct2, symbol) -> stats
>         self.recent = []         # recent trades for expiry
>
>     def process_trade(self, trade):
>         # trade: {ts, buyer, seller, symbol, side, qty, price, buyer_owner, seller_owner}
>         buyer = trade.get("buyer_owner", trade["buyer"])
>         seller = trade.get("seller_owner", trade["seller"])
>         symbol = trade["symbol"]
>         qty = trade["qty"]
>
>         alerts = []
>
>         if buyer == seller:
>             alerts.append(("self_trade", trade))
>
>         self.positions[buyer] = self.positions.get(buyer, 0) + qty
>         self.positions[seller] = self.positions.get(seller, 0) - qty
>
>         b_buy, b_sell = self.gross.get(buyer, (0, 0))
>         self.gross[buyer] = (b_buy + qty, b_sell)
>
>         s_buy, s_sell = self.gross.get(seller, (0, 0))
>         self.gross[seller] = (s_buy, s_sell + qty)
>
>         key = tuple(sorted((buyer, seller))) + (symbol,)
>         stats = self.pair_stats.get(key, {"count": 0, "volume": 0})
>         stats["count"] += 1
>         stats["volume"] += qty
>         self.pair_stats[key] = stats
>
>         # Example heuristic
>         if stats["count"] > 10 and stats["volume"] > 10000:
>             alerts.append(("repeated_pair_activity", key, stats))
>
>         return alerts
> ```
>
> #### Practical enhancement
>
> A production system would combine:
> - rules,
> - graph analytics,
> - anomaly detection,
> - and a supervised model trained on labeled surveillance cases.

> ### 8. Fast moving median for streaming data
>
> #### Approach
>
> To maintain a running median efficiently, use two heaps:
>
> - a max-heap for the lower half,
> - a min-heap for the upper half.
>
> For a **sliding window** median, also support deletion of expired elements. Standard technique: lazy deletion with hash maps.
>
> #### Key invariants
>
> - All elements in lower heap $\le$ all elements in upper heap.
> - Heap sizes differ by at most $1$.
> - Median is:
>   - top of one heap if odd window size,
>   - average of both tops if even.
>
> #### Python implementation
>
> ```python
> import heapq
> from collections import defaultdict
>
> class SlidingMedian:
>     def __init__(self, k):
>         self.k = k
>         self.small = []  # max-heap via negative values
>         self.large = []  # min-heap
>         self.delayed = defaultdict(int)
>         self.small_size = 0
>         self.large_size = 0
>
>     def prune(self, heap):
>         while heap:
>             num = -heap[0] if heap is self.small else heap[0]
>             if self.delayed[num]:
>                 self.delayed[num] -= 1
>                 heapq.heappop(heap)
>             else:
>                 break
>
>     def rebalance(self):
>         if self.small_size > self.large_size + 1:
>             heapq.heappush(self.large, -heapq.heappop(self.small))
>             self.small_size -= 1
>             self.large_size += 1
>             self.prune(self.small)
>         elif self.small_size < self.large_size:
>             heapq.heappush(self.small, -heapq.heappop(self.large))
>             self.small_size += 1
>             self.large_size -= 1
>             self.prune(self.large)
>
>     def add(self, num):
>         if not self.small or num <= -self.small[0]:
>             heapq.heappush(self.small, -num)
>             self.small_size += 1
>         else:
>             heapq.heappush(self.large, num)
>             self.large_size += 1
>         self.rebalance()
>
>     def remove(self, num):
>         self.delayed[num] += 1
>         if num <= -self.small[0]:
>             self.small_size -= 1
>             if num == -self.small[0]:
>                 self.prune(self.small)
>         else:
>             self.large_size -= 1
>             if self.large and num == self.large[0]:
>                 self.prune(self.large)
>         self.rebalance()
>
>     def median(self):
>         if self.k % 2 == 1:
>             return float(-self.small[0])
>         return (-self.small[0] + self.large[0]) / 2.0
> ```
>
> #### Complexity
>
> Each insertion and deletion is
>
> $$
> O(\log k),
> $$
>
> so each median update is
>
> $$
> O(\log k).
> $$
>

> ### 9. Arbitrage between S\&P $500$ futures and the underlying basket
>
> #### Principle
>
> Use the fair-value relation from cost of carry:
>
> $$
> F = S e^{(r-q)T},
> $$
>
> where:
> - $F$ = futures price,
> - $S$ = spot index level,
> - $r$ = financing rate,
> - $q$ = dividend yield,
> - $T$ = time to expiry.
>
> #### If futures are overpriced
>
> If market futures price satisfies
>
> $$
> F_{\text{mkt}} > S e^{(r-q)T},
> $$
>
> then:
>
> 1. **Buy the underlying basket** in index weights.
> 2. **Short the futures**.
> 3. Finance the stock purchase at rate $r$.
> 4. Receive dividends from the basket.
>
> At expiry, the spread converges and the arbitrage profit is the excess of quoted futures over fair value, net of transaction costs.
>
> #### If futures are underpriced
>
> If
>
> $$
> F_{\text{mkt}} < S e^{(r-q)T},
> $$
>
> then:
>
> 1. **Short the basket**,
> 2. **Buy the futures**,
> 3. Invest short-sale proceeds,
> 4. Pay dividends owed on borrowed stock.
>
> #### Practical issues
>
> True arbitrage is limited by:
> - transaction costs,
> - bid-ask spreads,
> - inability to trade all $500$ names instantly,
> - funding rates,
> - stock borrow constraints,
> - dividend uncertainty,
> - execution slippage.
>
> Hence in practice this is often an **index arbitrage program trade** rather than a frictionless arbitrage.

> ### 10. What happens to option prices during earnings announcements?
>
> Earnings announcements increase uncertainty about the stock’s near-term move. As a result:
>
> 1. **Implied volatility tends to rise before earnings**
>    - especially in options expiring just after the announcement.
>
> 2. **Options become more expensive**
>    - both calls and puts can rise in price because volatility enters both positively.
>
> 3. **After earnings, implied volatility usually collapses**
>    - this is the **vol crush**.
>
> Thus even if the stock moves, an option buyer can still lose money if the realized move is smaller than what the option market had already priced in.
>
> A rough way to infer the market-implied move from the at-the-money straddle is:
>
> $$
> \text{Implied move} \approx \text{ATM call price} + \text{ATM put price}.
> $$
>
> So the market often prices a jump, and after the jump uncertainty falls sharply.

> ### 11. Mean-reversion strategy for ETF pairs trading
>
> #### Idea
>
> Choose two related ETFs whose relative price is historically stable, for example sector ETFs or highly correlated broad-market products.
>
> #### Step 1: Select a pair
>
> Look for:
> - high correlation,
> - economic linkage,
> - stable hedge ratio.
>
> Example model:
>
> $$
> Y_t = \alpha + \beta X_t + \varepsilon_t.
> $$
>
> Estimate $\beta$ by regression, then define the spread:
>
> $$
> S_t = Y_t - \beta X_t.
> $$
>
> If the pair is cointegrated or the spread is stationary, mean reversion is plausible.
>
> #### Step 2: Standardize the spread
>
> Compute a rolling mean and standard deviation:
>
> $$
> Z_t = \frac{S_t - \mu_t}{\sigma_t}.
> $$
>
> #### Step 3: Trading rule
>
> - If $Z_t > z_{\text{entry}}$: short the spread
>   - short $Y$, long $\beta X$
> - If $Z_t < -z_{\text{entry}}$: long the spread
>   - long $Y$, short $\beta X$
> - Exit when $Z_t$ reverts toward $0$
>
> Typical thresholds might be:
>
> $$
> z_{\text{entry}} = 2,\qquad z_{\text{exit}} = 0.5.
> $$
>
> #### Step 4: Risk controls
>
> - stop-loss on spread divergence,
> - dollar neutrality or beta neutrality,
> - liquidity filters,
> - regime detection,
> - transaction cost adjustment,
> - borrow availability for shorts.
>
> #### Main risks
>
> - structural break in relationship,
> - crowding,
> - trend masquerading as temporary divergence,
> - execution cost overwhelming edge.

> ### 12. $100$ people with red/blue hats, simultaneous guesses, maximize group survival
>
> #### Strategy
>
> Encode colors as bits:
> - blue $=0$,
> - red $=1$.
>
> Beforehand, number the people $1,2,\dots,100$.
>
> Each person sees the other $99$ hats. Let person $i$ compute the parity of the red hats they see. They guess their own hat so that the **total parity** of all $100$ hats equals a predetermined value, say $0$ (even parity).
>
> Concretely:
> - if person $i$ sees an even number of red hats, they guess blue;
> - if they see an odd number of red hats, they guess red.
>
> #### Why this works
>
> Let the true total parity be either even or odd.
>
> - If the true total parity is even, then every person’s guess matches their actual hat.
> - If the true total parity is odd, then every person’s guess is wrong.
>
> Thus the group is either entirely correct or entirely incorrect.
>
> Since the two global parities are equally possible, this guarantees that in one of the two parity classes they all survive.
>
> If “group survival” means maximizing the number guaranteed correct for every configuration, this strategy does **not** maximize the worst-case guaranteed number.
>
> For the usual hat puzzle objective with **simultaneous guesses and no passing**, the best deterministic strategy guarantees
>
> $$
> 50
> $$
>
> correct guesses.
>
> #### Optimal strategy for maximum guaranteed survivors
>
> Pair the people into $50$ disjoint pairs. In each pair, the two players agree:
>
> - Person $A$ guesses the color they see on person $B$.
> - Person $B$ guesses the opposite of the color they see on person $A$.
>
> In each pair, exactly one guess is correct. Therefore across $50$ pairs, exactly
>
> $$
> 50
> $$
>
> people are correct, regardless of the hat assignment.
>
> #### Final answer
>
> The maximum guaranteed number of survivors is
>
> $$
> \boxed{50}.
> $$
>
> A simple optimal strategy is to partition into $50$ pairs and have each pair make opposite-mirroring guesses, guaranteeing exactly one correct guess per pair.
