> [!example] Question 699 · Citadel
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

What are the ways to construct a uniform distribution given only access to a fair coin?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=700)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Constructing a Uniform Distribution Using Only a Fair Coin
>
> A fair coin provides independent Bernoulli random bits:
> $$
> P(H)=P(T)=\tfrac12.
> $$
> From such bits, one can construct many uniform distributions. The methods depend on whether the target distribution is on a finite set, a countable set, or an interval such as $[0,1]$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. Uniform distribution on a set of size $2^k$
>
> Suppose the goal is to sample uniformly from
> $$
> \{0,1,\dots,2^k-1\}.
> $$
> Flip the fair coin $k$ times. Interpret heads as $1$ and tails as $0$, producing a binary string
> $$
> B_1B_2\cdots B_k.
> $$
> Then define
> $$
> X=\sum_{i=1}^k B_i 2^{k-i}.
> $$
> Since each binary string occurs with probability
> $$
> \left(\tfrac12\right)^k,
> $$
> every value of $X$ in $\{0,\dots,2^k-1\}$ is equally likely. Hence
> $$
> P(X=j)=\frac{1}{2^k}, \qquad j=0,\dots,2^k-1.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Uniform distribution on a finite set of arbitrary size $n$
>
> If $n$ is not a power of $2$, use **rejection sampling**.
>
> Choose $k$ such that
> $$
> 2^k \ge n.
> $$
> Generate a uniform integer
> $$
> Y \in \{0,1,\dots,2^k-1\}
> $$
> using $k$ fair coin flips as above.
>
> - If $Y<n$, output $Y$.
> - If $Y\ge n$, discard it and repeat.
>
> ### Why this works
> Each trial gives every number in $\{0,\dots,2^k-1\}$ equal probability. Conditioning on the event $\{Y<n\}$, each value in $\{0,\dots,n-1\}$ has probability
> $$
> P(Y=j\mid Y<n)=\frac{1/2^k}{n/2^k}=\frac{1}{n}.
> $$
> Thus the output is uniform on
> $$
> \{0,1,\dots,n-1\}.
> $$
>
> ### Example: uniform on $\{1,\dots,6\}$
> Take $k=3$, since
> $$
> 2^3=8\ge 6.
> $$
> Three coin flips give a number in $\{0,\dots,7\}$. Accept only $0,1,\dots,5$; reject $6,7$ and try again. The accepted output is uniform on six outcomes.
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Uniform distribution on dyadic rationals
>
> Using $k$ fair coin flips, one can generate a uniform random variable on
> $$
> \left\{0,\frac{1}{2^k},\frac{2}{2^k},\dots,\frac{2^k-1}{2^k}\right\}
> $$
> by setting
> $$
> U_k=\sum_{i=1}^k B_i 2^{-i}.
> $$
> Each dyadic rational with denominator $2^k$ occurs with probability
> $$
> \frac{1}{2^k}.
> $$
>
> This gives increasingly fine approximations to a continuous uniform random variable.
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. Uniform distribution on the interval $[0,1]$
>
> A standard construction uses the infinite binary expansion. Let $B_1,B_2,\dots$ be independent fair coin bits, with $B_i\in\{0,1\}$. Define
> $$
> U=\sum_{i=1}^\infty B_i 2^{-i}.
> $$
> Then $U$ is uniformly distributed on $[0,1]$.
>
> ### Justification
> For any binary interval of the form
> $$
> \left[\frac{m}{2^k},\frac{m+1}{2^k}\right),
> $$
> membership in that interval is determined by the first $k$ bits of the binary expansion. Since the first $k$ bits are equally likely among all $2^k$ possibilities,
> $$
> P\!\left(U\in \left[\frac{m}{2^k},\frac{m+1}{2^k}\right)\right)=\frac{1}{2^k}.
> $$
> These are exactly the probabilities of the uniform distribution on $[0,1]$.
>
> ### Remark on binary ambiguity
> Some numbers, such as
> $$
> \frac12=0.1000\cdots = 0.0111\cdots,
> $$
> have two binary expansions. This affects only a countable set of points, hence has probability $0$, so the distribution remains uniform.
>
> ---
>
> ## 5. Uniform distribution on an arbitrary interval $[a,b]$
>
> Once $U\sim \mathrm{Unif}[0,1]$ has been constructed, define
> $$
> X=a+(b-a)U.
> $$
> Then
> $$
> X\sim \mathrm{Unif}[a,b].
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 6. Uniform distribution on a countable or more general set
>
> If the set is finite, the methods above apply directly. If the set is countably infinite, there is **no uniform probability distribution** assigning equal positive probability to every element, because if each point had probability $p>0$, the total probability would be infinite, and if $p=0$, the total probability would be $0$. Thus a truly uniform distribution exists only for finite sets or continuous spaces such as intervals, with respect to length.
>
> ---
>
> ## Summary of constructions
>
> Using only a fair coin, one can construct:
>
> 1. **Uniform on $\{0,\dots,2^k-1\}$** by reading $k$ flips as a binary number.
> 2. **Uniform on $\{0,\dots,n-1\}$ for arbitrary $n$** by rejection sampling from a power of $2$.
> 3. **Uniform on dyadic rationals** in $[0,1]$ using finitely many binary digits.
> 4. **Uniform on $[0,1]$** using an infinite binary expansion
>    $$
>    U=\sum_{i=1}^\infty B_i2^{-i}.
>    $$
> 5. **Uniform on $[a,b]$** via the linear transformation
>    $$
>    a+(b-a)U.
>    $$
>
> These are the standard ways to build uniform distributions from a fair coin alone.
