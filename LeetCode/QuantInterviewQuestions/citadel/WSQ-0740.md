> [!example] Question 740 · Citadel
> ## Quantitative Analyst Interview

What are the assumptions of linear regression?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=741)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> In the classical linear regression model, the main assumptions are the following.
>
> ## 1. Linearity in parameters
> The relationship between the dependent variable and the regressors is linear in the coefficients:
> $$
> Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_{i1} + \cdots + \beta_k X_{ik} + u_i.
> $$
> This means the model is linear in $\beta_0, \beta_1, \ldots, \beta_k$.
>
> ## 2. Random sampling
> The data $\{(Y_i, X_{i1}, \ldots, X_{ik})\}_{i=1}^n$ are drawn as a random sample from the population.
>
> ## 3. No perfect multicollinearity
> No explanatory variable is an exact linear combination of the others. Equivalently, the design matrix has full column rank.
>
> ## 4. Zero conditional mean
> The error term has mean zero given the regressors:
> $$
> E[u_i \mid X_{i1}, \ldots, X_{ik}] = 0.
> $$
> This is the key exogeneity assumption ensuring unbiasedness of the ordinary least squares estimator.
>
> ## 5. Homoscedasticity
> The variance of the error term is constant across observations:
> $$
> \operatorname{Var}(u_i \mid X_{i1}, \ldots, X_{ik}) = \sigma^2.
> $$
>
> ## 6. No autocorrelation
> Errors are uncorrelated across observations:
> $$
> \operatorname{Cov}(u_i, u_j \mid X) = 0 \quad \text{for } i \ne j.
> $$
>
> ## 7. Normality of errors
> For exact small-sample inference, it is often assumed that
> $$
> u_i \mid X \sim N(0, \sigma^2).
> $$
> This assumption is not required for ordinary least squares to be unbiased, but it is used for exact $t$-tests and $F$-tests.
>
> ## Summary
> The most commonly cited core assumptions are:
> $$
> Y = X\beta + u,
> $$
> with
>
> - linearity,
> - random sampling,
> - no perfect multicollinearity,
> - $E[u \mid X] = 0$.
>
> For the classical model and standard inference, one usually also adds:
>
> - constant variance,
> - no correlation among errors,
> - normality of errors.
