> # Linear Regression
>
> Linear regression is a statistical method for modeling the relationship between a response variable and one or more explanatory variables. Its central idea is to represent the conditional mean of the response as a linear function of the predictors.
>
> ## 1. Basic idea
>
> Suppose the response variable is $Y$ and the predictor is $X$. In simple linear regression, the model is
>
> $$
> Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \varepsilon,
> $$
>
> where:
>
> - $\beta_0$ is the intercept,
> - $\beta_1$ is the slope,
> - $\varepsilon$ is the error term.
>
> The interpretation is that, on average, $Y$ changes by $\beta_1$ units for each one-unit increase in $X$, and when $X = 0$, the expected value of $Y$ is $\beta_0$.
>
> More generally, with multiple predictors $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_p$, the multiple linear regression model is
>
> $$
> Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \cdots + \beta_p X_p + \varepsilon.
> $$
>
> Equivalently,
>
> $$
> E[Y \mid X_1, \dots, X_p] = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \cdots + \beta_p X_p.
> $$
>
> Thus, the term “linear” refers to linearity in the coefficients $\beta_0, \beta_1, \dots, \beta_p$.
>
> ## 2. Purpose of linear regression
>
> Linear regression is commonly used for:
>
> - **Prediction**: estimating $Y$ from observed predictor values.
> - **Explanation**: understanding how predictors are associated with the response.
> - **Inference**: testing whether certain predictors have statistically significant relationships with the response.
> - **Adjustment**: controlling for confounding variables in observational studies.
>
> ## 3. Simple linear regression
>
> Given observations $(x_1, y_1), (x_2, y_2), \dots, (x_n, y_n)$, the fitted model is
>
> $$
> \hat{y}_i = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x_i.
> $$
>
> The residual for observation $i$ is
>
> $$
> e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i.
> $$
>
> The most common method for estimating $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ is **ordinary least squares** (OLS), which chooses the coefficients to minimize the residual sum of squares:
>
> $$
> \mathrm{RSS} = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i)^2.
> $$
>
> ### Least-squares estimates
>
> The OLS estimates are
>
> $$
> \hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2},
> $$
>
> and
>
> $$
> \hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x},
> $$
>
> where
>
> $$
> \bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i, \qquad \bar{y} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n y_i.
> $$
>
> Thus, the fitted line passes through the point $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$.
>
> ## 4. Multiple linear regression
>
> When there are several predictors, the model becomes
>
> $$
> Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \beta_2 x_{i2} + \cdots + \beta_p x_{ip} + \varepsilon_i,
> $$
>
> for $i = 1, \dots, n$.
>
> ### Matrix form
>
> Define
>
> $$
> \mathbf{y} =
> \begin{bmatrix}
> y_1 \\
> y_2 \\
> \vdots \\
> y_n
> \end{bmatrix},
> \qquad
> \mathbf{X} =
> \begin{bmatrix}
> 1 & x_{11} & x_{12} & \cdots & x_{1p} \\
> 1 & x_{21} & x_{22} & \cdots & x_{2p} \\
> \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
> 1 & x_{n1} & x_{n2} & \cdots & x_{np}
> \end{bmatrix},
> \qquad
> \boldsymbol{\beta} =
> \begin{bmatrix}
> \beta_0 \\
> \beta_1 \\
> \vdots \\
> \beta_p
> \end{bmatrix}.
> $$
>
> Then the model is
>
> $$
> \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}.
> $$
>
> The OLS estimator minimizes
>
> $$
> (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})^\top (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}).
> $$
>
> If $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}$ is invertible, the solution is
>
> $$
> \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}.
> $$
>
> The fitted values are
>
> $$
> \hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},
> $$
>
> and the residual vector is
>
> $$
> \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}.
> $$
>
> ## 5. Interpretation of coefficients
>
> In multiple regression, $\beta_j$ represents the change in the mean of $Y$ associated with a one-unit increase in $X_j$, **holding all other predictors fixed**.
>
> This “holding fixed” interpretation is essential. For example, in
>
> $$
> Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \varepsilon,
> $$
>
> $\beta_1$ measures the association between $X_1$ and $Y$ after adjusting for $X_2$.
>
> ## 6. Statistical assumptions
>
> Classical linear regression typically relies on the following assumptions:
>
> ### 6.1 Linearity
>
> The mean response is a linear combination of predictors:
>
> $$
> E[Y \mid X_1, \dots, X_p] = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \cdots + \beta_p X_p.
> $$
>
> ### 6.2 Independence
>
> The errors $\varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_n$ are independent.
>
> ### 6.3 Mean zero errors
>
> $$
> E[\varepsilon_i \mid \mathbf{X}] = 0.
> $$
>
> This implies that the predictors are not systematically related to omitted factors in the error term.
>
> ### 6.4 Constant variance
>
> The variance of the errors is constant:
>
> $$
> \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i \mid \mathbf{X}) = \sigma^2.
> $$
>
> This is called **homoscedasticity**. If the variance changes with predictor level, the errors are **heteroscedastic**.
>
> ### 6.5 No perfect multicollinearity
>
> No predictor can be expressed as an exact linear combination of the others. Otherwise, $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}$ is singular and OLS coefficients are not uniquely determined.
>
> ### 6.6 Normality of errors
>
> For exact small-sample hypothesis tests and confidence intervals, it is often assumed that
>
> $$
> \varepsilon_i \sim N(0, \sigma^2).
> $$
>
> This assumption is not required for OLS estimation itself, but it simplifies inference.
>
> ## 7. Why least squares works
>
> Under the Gauss–Markov assumptions, the OLS estimator is the **best linear unbiased estimator** (BLUE). This means that among all linear unbiased estimators, it has the smallest variance.
>
> If, in addition, the errors are normally distributed, then OLS is also closely connected to maximum likelihood estimation.
>
> ## 8. Goodness of fit
>
> A common measure of fit is the coefficient of determination, $R^2$:
>
> $$
> R^2 = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{\mathrm{TSS}},
> $$
>
> where
>
> $$
> \mathrm{RSS} = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2,
> \qquad
> \mathrm{TSS} = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2.
> $$
>
> $R^2$ measures the proportion of variation in $Y$ explained by the regression model.
>
> In multiple regression, adding predictors never decreases $R^2$, so one often uses the **adjusted $R^2$**:
>
> $$
> \bar{R}^2 = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{RSS}/(n-p-1)}{\mathrm{TSS}/(n-1)}.
> $$
>
> Adjusted $R^2$ penalizes unnecessary predictors.
>
> ## 9. Inference for coefficients
>
> To assess whether a predictor is related to the response, one often tests
>
> $$
> H_0: \beta_j = 0
> \qquad \text{versus} \qquad
> H_1: \beta_j \ne 0.
> $$
>
> The test statistic is typically
>
> $$
> t = \frac{\hat{\beta}_j}{\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_j)},
> $$
>
> which, under the classical assumptions and normal errors, follows a $t$-distribution with $n-p-1$ degrees of freedom.
>
> A confidence interval for $\beta_j$ is
>
> $$
> \hat{\beta}_j \pm t_{\alpha/2,\, n-p-1}\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_j).
> $$
>
> For testing several coefficients jointly, one uses an $F$-test.
>
> ## 10. Prediction
>
> Given a new predictor vector
>
> $$
> \mathbf{x}_0 =
> \begin{bmatrix}
> 1 \\
> x_{01} \\
> \vdots \\
> x_{0p}
> \end{bmatrix},
> $$
>
> the predicted mean response is
>
> $$
> \hat{y}_0 = \mathbf{x}_0^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}.
> $$
>
> There is a distinction between:
>
> - a **confidence interval** for the mean response $E[Y \mid \mathbf{x}_0]$,
> - a **prediction interval** for a new observation at $\mathbf{x}_0$.
>
> The prediction interval is wider because it includes both uncertainty in the estimated mean and the variability of individual outcomes around that mean.
>
> ## 11. Residual analysis and diagnostics
>
> After fitting a regression model, one should examine whether the assumptions are reasonable.
>
> ### 11.1 Residual plots
>
> Plotting residuals against fitted values or predictors can reveal:
>
> - nonlinearity,
> - heteroscedasticity,
> - outliers,
> - model misspecification.
>
> A random scatter around zero is desirable.
>
> ### 11.2 Normal Q–Q plot
>
> This assesses whether residuals are approximately normally distributed.
>
> ### 11.3 Leverage and influence
>
> Some points have unusual predictor values and can exert strong influence on the fitted model.
>
> - **Leverage** measures how far a point is from the center of predictor space.
> - **Influence** measures how much the fitted model changes if the point is removed.
>
> Cook’s distance is a common influence diagnostic.
>
> ## 12. Common issues
>
> ### 12.1 Nonlinearity
>
> If the true relationship is not linear, a straight-line model may fit poorly. Remedies include:
>
> - transforming variables,
> - adding polynomial terms such as $X^2$,
> - using splines or nonparametric methods.
>
> ### 12.2 Multicollinearity
>
> When predictors are highly correlated, coefficient estimates can become unstable and standard errors can be large. The model may still predict well, but interpretation becomes difficult.
>
> ### 12.3 Heteroscedasticity
>
> If error variance is not constant, OLS coefficients remain unbiased under suitable conditions, but standard errors may be incorrect. One may use:
>
> - robust standard errors,
> - weighted least squares,
> - transformations.
>
> ### 12.4 Outliers
>
> Outliers may distort the fit substantially. They should be investigated, not automatically removed.
>
> ### 12.5 Omitted variable bias
>
> If an important predictor is omitted and it is correlated with included predictors, coefficient estimates can be biased.
>
> ## 13. Categorical predictors
>
> Linear regression can incorporate categorical variables using indicator variables. For example, if a variable has two categories, one may define
>
> $$
> D =
> \begin{cases}
> 1, & \text{if in category A}, \\
> 0, & \text{if in category B}.
> \end{cases}
> $$
>
> Then the model
>
> $$
> Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 D + \varepsilon
> $$
>
> gives:
>
> - mean response $\beta_0$ for category $B$,
> - mean response $\beta_0 + \beta_1$ for category $A$.
>
> With more than two categories, one uses several dummy variables.
>
> ## 14. Interaction terms
>
> An interaction allows the effect of one predictor to depend on another. For predictors $X_1$ and $X_2$, the model
>
> $$
> Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \beta_3 X_1X_2 + \varepsilon
> $$
>
> means that the effect of $X_1$ on $Y$ is
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial E[Y \mid X_1, X_2]}{\partial X_1} = \beta_1 + \beta_3 X_2.
> $$
>
> Thus, the slope with respect to $X_1$ changes with $X_2$.
>
> ## 15. Geometric view
>
> Linear regression can be understood geometrically. The observed response vector $\mathbf{y}$ is projected onto the column space of $\mathbf{X}$. The fitted vector $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ is the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{y}$ onto that subspace.
>
> Consequently, the residual vector is orthogonal to every column of $\mathbf{X}$:
>
> $$
> \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}.
> $$
>
> This yields the normal equations
>
> $$
> \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}.
> $$
>
> ## 16. Connection to maximum likelihood
>
> If the errors satisfy
>
> $$
> \varepsilon_i \sim N(0, \sigma^2)
> $$
>
> independently, then
>
> $$
> Y_i \mid \mathbf{X} \sim N(\mathbf{x}_i^\top \boldsymbol{\beta}, \sigma^2).
> $$
>
> In this case, maximizing the likelihood with respect to $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ is equivalent to minimizing the sum of squared residuals. Thus, OLS and maximum likelihood coincide for the coefficients.
>
> ## 17. Advantages and limitations
>
> ### Advantages
>
> - simple and interpretable,
> - computationally efficient,
> - widely applicable,
> - strong theoretical foundation,
> - useful for both prediction and inference.
>
> ### Limitations
>
> - assumes linear structure in the mean,
> - sensitive to outliers,
> - can be misleading under multicollinearity,
> - inference depends on assumptions,
> - may perform poorly when relationships are strongly nonlinear.
>
> ## 18. Example interpretation
>
> Suppose the fitted model is
>
> $$
> \hat{Y} = 12 + 3X.
> $$
>
> Then:
>
> - when $X = 0$, the predicted response is $12$;
> - for each one-unit increase in $X$, the predicted response increases by $3$ units.
>
> If instead the model is
>
> $$
> \hat{Y} = 5 + 2X_1 - 4X_2,
> $$
>
> then:
>
> - holding $X_2$ fixed, increasing $X_1$ by $1$ raises the predicted response by $2$;
> - holding $X_1$ fixed, increasing $X_2$ by $1$ lowers the predicted response by $4$.
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> Linear regression models the conditional mean of a response variable as a linear function of one or more predictors. Its parameters are usually estimated by ordinary least squares, which minimizes the sum of squared residuals. The method supports prediction, interpretation, and statistical inference, provided its assumptions are reasonably satisfied. In both simple and multiple settings, it remains one of the most important and widely used tools in statistics and data analysis.
