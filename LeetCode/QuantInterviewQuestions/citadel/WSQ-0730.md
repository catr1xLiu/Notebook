> [!example] Question 730 · Citadel
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

How will the probability of an option ending up in the money change if the volatility of the underlying stock increases?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=731)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> # Solution
>
> For a European option, the probability of finishing in the money is not simply increasing in volatility — the relationship is more subtle than the common intuition suggests. In most configurations, the probability that a call finishes in the money actually *decreases* as volatility grows, tending to zero as volatility becomes very large.
>
> ## Setup
>
> Under Black-Scholes, a call finishes in the money when $S_T>K$, with risk-neutral probability
>
> $$
> \mathbb{P}(S_T>K) = N(d_2), \qquad d_2=\frac{\ln(S_0/K)+\left(r-\tfrac12\sigma^2\right)T}{\sigma\sqrt T}.
> $$
>
> A put finishes in the money when $S_T<K$, with probability $N(-d_2)$.
>
> Holding $S_0,K,r,T$ fixed, write
>
> $$
> d_2(\sigma) = \frac{A}{\sigma\sqrt T}-\frac{\sigma\sqrt T}{2}, \qquad A:=\ln(S_0/K)+rT,
> $$
>
> where $A>0$ means the forward price exceeds the strike, and $A<0$ means a deep out-of-the-money call.
>
> ## How the probability moves with volatility
>
> Differentiating,
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{d\sigma}d_2(\sigma) = -\frac{A}{\sigma^2\sqrt T}-\frac{\sqrt T}{2}.
> $$
>
> If $A\ge 0$, at-the-money or forward in-the-money calls, both terms are $\le 0$, so $d_2(\sigma)$ is **strictly decreasing** for every $\sigma>0$: it falls from $+\infty$ at $\sigma\to0^+$ to $-\infty$ as $\sigma\to\infty$, so $N(d_2)$ falls monotonically from $1$ toward $0$.
>
> If $A<0$, a deep out-of-the-money call, the derivative starts at $+\infty$ as $\sigma\to0^+$ and decreases to $-\sqrt T/2<0$ as $\sigma\to\infty$, crossing zero exactly once at $\sigma^\ast=\sqrt{-2A/T}$. So $N(d_2)$ **rises** from $0$ up to a single interior maximum at $\sigma^\ast$, then **falls** back toward $0$ as $\sigma\to\infty$: the relationship is genuinely non-monotone.
>
> In every case, as $\sigma\to\infty$ the first term of $d_2(\sigma)$ vanishes while the second diverges to $-\infty$, so
>
> $$
> N(d_2)\to 0.
> $$
>
> Extremely high volatility makes a call *less* likely to finish in the money, not more.
>
> ## Puts
>
> Since $N(-d_2)=1-N(d_2)$, its behavior mirrors the call's: it rises monotonically from $0$ to $1$ when $A\ge0$, and dips to an interior minimum before rising back to $1$ when $A<0$. In every case,
>
> $$
> N(-d_2)\to 1 \quad \text{as } \sigma\to\infty,
> $$
>
> so a put becomes almost certain to finish in the money once volatility is large enough: extreme dispersion, combined with the lognormal floor at $0$, drags most of the distribution's mass below any fixed strike.
>
> ## Contrast with option price
>
> This is distinct from how the option's *price* behaves. Vega,
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial C}{\partial \sigma} = S_0\sqrt{T}\,\varphi(d_1) > 0,
> $$
>
> is always strictly positive, so both call and put prices rise monotonically with volatility regardless of strike or spot. That fact concerns the magnitude of the payoff in favorable outcomes, which grows with dispersion; it is not the same as the probability of finishing in the money, governed by $N(d_2)$ or $N(-d_2)$, which need not increase and for a typical call actually decreases as volatility rises.
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> Increasing volatility does not generally raise the probability that an option finishes in the money. For an at-the-money or forward in-the-money call, that probability strictly decreases toward $0$; for a deep out-of-the-money call, it first rises, then falls, again toward $0$. For puts, the in-the-money probability tends to $1$ as volatility grows without bound. This should not be confused with the separate, and always true, fact that option prices increase with volatility.
