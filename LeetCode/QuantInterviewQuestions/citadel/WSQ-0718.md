> [!example] Question 718 · Citadel
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

Why is an L2 penalty added to linear regression models? Explain the effect of the L2 penalty on the solution.

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=719)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> In ordinary linear regression, the coefficients are chosen to minimize the residual sum of squares:
>
> $$
> \min_{\beta} \sum_{i=1}^n \left(y_i - x_i^\top \beta\right)^2.
> $$
>
> Adding an $L_2$ penalty produces **ridge regression**:
>
> $$
> \min_{\beta} \sum_{i=1}^n \left(y_i - x_i^\top \beta\right)^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j^2,
> $$
>
> where $\lambda \ge 0$ controls the strength of the penalty.
>
> ## Why add the $L_2$ penalty?
>
> ### 1. To reduce overfitting
> Large coefficients can make the model fit noise in the training data. The $L_2$ term penalizes large values of $\beta_j$, encouraging smaller coefficients and a smoother, less variable model. This often improves performance on new data.
>
> ### 2. To handle multicollinearity
> When predictors are highly correlated, ordinary least squares coefficients can become unstable: small changes in the data may cause large changes in the estimates. The $L_2$ penalty stabilizes the solution by shrinking coefficients toward $0$.
>
> ### 3. To make the problem well-conditioned
> The ordinary least squares estimator is
>
> $$
> \hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y,
> $$
>
> when $X^\top X$ is invertible. If $X^\top X$ is nearly singular or singular, this is unstable or impossible. Ridge regression replaces this with
>
> $$
> \hat{\beta}_{\text{ridge}} = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y,
> $$
>
> and $X^\top X + \lambda I$ is invertible for $\lambda > 0$. Thus the penalty improves numerical stability.
>
> ### 4. To trade a little bias for a large reduction in variance
> Ridge regression introduces bias because coefficients are shrunk toward $0$, but this often substantially reduces variance. By the bias-variance tradeoff, a modest increase in bias can produce lower overall prediction error.
>
> ### 5. To discourage extreme coefficient values
> If very large coefficients are not plausible or desirable, the $L_2$ penalty encodes a preference for smaller parameter magnitudes.
>
> ## Interpretation
> The penalty can be viewed geometrically as constraining the coefficient vector to remain small:
>
> $$
> \sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j^2 \le c.
> $$
>
> It can also be viewed probabilistically: ridge regression corresponds to placing a Gaussian prior on the coefficients,
>
> $$
> \beta_j \sim N(0, \tau^2).
> $$
>
> ## Important note
> Unlike $L_1$ regularization, the $L_2$ penalty usually does **not** force coefficients to be exactly zero. It shrinks them continuously.
>
> ## Conclusion
> The $L_2$ penalty is added to linear regression to shrink coefficients, reduce overfitting, improve stability under multicollinearity, and often achieve better predictive performance through a favorable bias-variance tradeoff.
