> [!example] Question 705 · Citadel
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

What happens to the optimal parameters of a linear regression if you feed in the same data twice? How about the R^2 and z-scores?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=706)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Consider ordinary least squares with design matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$ and response vector $y \in \mathbb{R}^n$. “Feeding in the same data twice” means replacing the dataset by
>
> $$
> X^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}X\\ X\end{bmatrix}, 
> \qquad
> y^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}y\\ y\end{bmatrix},
> $$
>
> so the sample size becomes $2n$.
>
> ## 1. Effect on the optimal regression coefficients
>
> The ordinary least squares estimator is
>
> $$
> \hat{\beta}=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y
> $$
>
> when $X^\top X$ is invertible. For the duplicated dataset,
>
> $$
> (X^{*})^\top X^{*}
> =
> X^\top X + X^\top X
> =
> 2X^\top X,
> $$
>
> and
>
> $$
> (X^{*})^\top y^{*}
> =
> X^\top y + X^\top y
> =
> 2X^\top y.
> $$
>
> Hence
>
> $$
> \hat{\beta}^{*}
> =
> \bigl((X^{*})^\top X^{*}\bigr)^{-1}(X^{*})^\top y^{*}
> =
> (2X^\top X)^{-1}(2X^\top y)
> =
> (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y
> =
> \hat{\beta}.
> $$
>
> So **the optimal parameters do not change**.
>
> This remains true more generally for weighted least squares if all observations are duplicated with the same weights: duplicating every point multiplies the objective by a constant factor, which does not change its minimizer.
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Effect on $R^2$
>
> Recall
>
> $$
> R^2 = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{SSE}}{\mathrm{SST}},
> $$
>
> where
>
> $$
> \mathrm{SSE}=\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2,
> \qquad
> \mathrm{SST}=\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i-\bar y)^2.
> $$
>
> If the data are duplicated, then each residual appears twice, so
>
> $$
> \mathrm{SSE}^{*}=2\,\mathrm{SSE}.
> $$
>
> Also, the sample mean $\bar y$ is unchanged by duplication, so
>
> $$
> \mathrm{SST}^{*}=2\,\mathrm{SST}.
> $$
>
> Therefore,
>
> $$
> R^{2*}
> =
> 1-\frac{\mathrm{SSE}^{*}}{\mathrm{SST}^{*}}
> =
> 1-\frac{2\,\mathrm{SSE}}{2\,\mathrm{SST}}
> =
> 1-\frac{\mathrm{SSE}}{\mathrm{SST}}
> =
> R^2.
> $$
>
> So **$R^2$ stays the same**.
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Effect on $z$-scores (or $t$-statistics) of coefficients
>
> For a regression coefficient $\hat{\beta}_j$, the usual test statistic has the form
>
> $$
> z_j = \frac{\hat{\beta}_j}{\mathrm{SE}(\hat{\beta}_j)}
> \qquad \text{or} \qquad
> t_j = \frac{\hat{\beta}_j}{\mathrm{SE}(\hat{\beta}_j)}.
> $$
>
> The coefficient itself is unchanged, so the key is the standard error.
>
> For homoskedastic OLS,
>
> $$
> \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\beta}) = \sigma^2 (X^\top X)^{-1}.
> $$
>
> After duplication,
>
> $$
> (X^{*})^\top X^{*}=2X^\top X,
> $$
>
> so
>
> $$
> \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\beta}^{*})
> =
> \sigma^2 \bigl(2X^\top X\bigr)^{-1}
> =
> \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 (X^\top X)^{-1}.
> $$
>
> Thus the standard errors scale by
>
> $$
> \mathrm{SE}^{*}(\hat{\beta}_j)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,\mathrm{SE}(\hat{\beta}_j).
> $$
>
> Hence the corresponding $z$- or $t$-statistic becomes
>
> $$
> z_j^{*}
> =
> \frac{\hat{\beta}_j}{\mathrm{SE}^{*}(\hat{\beta}_j)}
> =
> \frac{\hat{\beta}_j}{\mathrm{SE}(\hat{\beta}_j)/\sqrt{2}}
> =
> \sqrt{2}\, z_j,
> $$
>
> and similarly
>
> $$
> t_j^{*}=\sqrt{2}\, t_j.
> $$
>
> So **the coefficient significance statistics increase by a factor of $\sqrt{2}$**, and the associated $p$-values become smaller.
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. Interpretation
>
> This increase in significance is **not genuine new information**. Exact duplication does not add independent observations; it only causes the standard formulas to behave as if the sample size had doubled. Thus:
>
> - **Coefficients:** unchanged
> - **Predictions:** unchanged
> - **$R^2$:** unchanged
> - **Standard errors:** shrink by a factor of $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$
> - **$z$-scores / $t$-statistics:** increase by a factor of $\sqrt{2}$
>
> If the duplication is recognized as repeated copies rather than new independent data, statistical inference should not treat them as doubling the effective sample size.
