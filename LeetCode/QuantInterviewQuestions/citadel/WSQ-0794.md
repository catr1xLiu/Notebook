> ## 1. Past Research Experience: Reinforcement Learning and Model Explainability
>
> A strong answer should clearly describe the problem, the method, the technical contribution, and the outcome.
>
> ### Reinforcement Learning
>
> A structured way to present reinforcement learning research is:
>
> - **Problem setting:** identify the environment, state space, action space, and reward.
> - **Goal:** maximize expected cumulative reward.
> - **Method used:** for example, $Q$-learning, Deep $Q$-Networks, policy gradients, actor-critic, or model-based RL.
> - **Challenges addressed:** sparse rewards, exploration, instability, sample inefficiency.
> - **Results:** improvement in reward, convergence speed, or robustness.
>
> A concise technical framing is:
>
> An RL problem is often modeled as a Markov Decision Process $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \gamma)$, where:
>
> - $\mathcal{S}$ is the state space,
> - $\mathcal{A}$ is the action space,
> - $P(s' \mid s,a)$ is the transition distribution,
> - $R(s,a)$ is the reward,
> - $\gamma \in [0,1)$ is the discount factor.
>
> The objective is to learn a policy $\pi(a \mid s)$ maximizing
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t r_t\right].
> $$
>
> If the work involved value-based methods, one may discuss the Bellman equation:
>
> $$
> Q^\pi(s,a) = \mathbb{E}\left[r + \gamma \mathbb{E}_{a' \sim \pi(\cdot \mid s')} Q^\pi(s',a')\right].
> $$
>
> For optimal control,
>
> $$
> Q^*(s,a) = \mathbb{E}\left[r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q^*(s',a')\right].
> $$
>
> If deep RL was used, mention stabilization mechanisms such as:
>
> - replay buffer,
> - target networks,
> - reward shaping,
> - entropy regularization,
> - curriculum learning.
>
> Typical follow-up questions include:
>
> - Why was RL appropriate instead of supervised learning?
> - How did you define the reward?
> - How did you balance exploration and exploitation?
> - What metrics did you use?
> - What failure modes did you observe?
> - How did you ensure reproducibility and stability?
>
> ### Model Explainability
>
> A structured description of explainability research should include:
>
> - the model being explained,
> - whether explanations were global or local,
> - whether the method was intrinsic or post hoc,
> - how explanation quality was evaluated.
>
> Common methods include:
>
> - feature importance,
> - partial dependence plots,
> - SHAP,
> - LIME,
> - saliency maps,
> - counterfactual explanations.
>
> For example, SHAP values are based on Shapley values from cooperative game theory. For feature $i$, the contribution is
>
> $$
> \phi_i = \sum_{S \subseteq N \setminus \{i\}} \frac{|S|!(|N|-|S|-1)!}{|N|!}\left[f_{S \cup \{i\}}(x) - f_S(x)\right].
> $$
>
> These quantify the average marginal contribution of feature $i$ across all coalitions.
>
> Typical follow-up questions include:
>
> - How do you define a “good” explanation?
> - How do you evaluate faithfulness versus interpretability?
> - What are the limitations of post hoc explanation methods?
> - How do explanations behave under correlated features?
> - Can explanations be manipulated?
> - What is the difference between local and global interpretability?
>
> A good conceptual distinction is:
>
> - **Interpretability**: how understandable the model or explanation is to humans.
> - **Faithfulness**: how accurately the explanation reflects the true model behavior.
>
> Post hoc methods may be intuitive without always being fully faithful, especially under strong feature dependence or model nonlinearity.

> ## 2. Bias–Variance Trade-off in Random Forest
>
> Random forests reduce variance while typically causing only a small increase in bias relative to a single deep decision tree.
>
> ### General Bias–Variance Decomposition
>
> For a prediction problem, the expected test error at a point $x$ can be decomposed as
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}\big[(Y - \hat{f}(x))^2\big]
> = \sigma^2 + \operatorname{Bias}[\hat{f}(x)]^2 + \operatorname{Var}[\hat{f}(x)],
> $$
>
> where:
>
> - $\sigma^2$ is irreducible noise,
> - $\operatorname{Bias}[\hat{f}(x)]$ measures systematic error,
> - $\operatorname{Var}[\hat{f}(x)]$ measures sensitivity to the training sample.
>
> ### Single Decision Trees
>
> A fully grown decision tree usually has:
>
> - **low bias**: it can fit complex nonlinear patterns,
> - **high variance**: small changes in training data can lead to very different trees.
>
> ### Bagging
>
> Bagging averages predictions from many bootstrap-sampled trees. If the trees have variance $\sigma^2$ and pairwise correlation $\rho$, then the variance of the average of $B$ trees is approximately
>
> $$
> \rho \sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2.
> $$
>
> As $B \to \infty$, this approaches
>
> $$
> \rho \sigma^2.
> $$
>
> Thus, averaging reduces variance, but the benefit is limited when trees are highly correlated.
>
> ### Why Random Forest Improves Over Bagging
>
> Random forests add feature subsampling at each split. This decorrelates trees by preventing the same dominant predictors from being chosen repeatedly. Lower correlation means lower ensemble variance.
>
> Thus random forests:
>
> - **substantially reduce variance** compared with a single tree,
> - **slightly increase bias** because each split is chosen from only a subset of features,
> - often **improve overall test error** because the variance reduction dominates.
>
> ### Effect of Hyperparameters
>
> - **Number of trees**:
>   - Increasing the number of trees decreases variance of the ensemble estimate and stabilizes performance.
>   - It does not significantly affect bias.
> - **Maximum depth**:
>   - Deeper trees $\Rightarrow$ lower bias, higher variance.
>   - Shallower trees $\Rightarrow$ higher bias, lower variance.
> - **Number of features per split**:
>   - Smaller subset $\Rightarrow$ more decorrelation, lower variance, possibly higher bias.
>   - Larger subset $\Rightarrow$ lower bias per tree, but higher correlation and higher ensemble variance.
> - **Minimum samples per leaf / split constraints**:
>   - Stronger regularization increases bias and reduces variance.
>
> ### Summary
>
> Random forests achieve a favorable bias–variance trade-off by combining:
>
> 1. deep, flexible trees with low bias,
> 2. averaging across many trees to reduce variance,
> 3. random feature selection to reduce tree correlation further.
>
> Hence, compared with a single decision tree, a random forest usually has **similar or slightly higher bias but much lower variance**, leading to better generalization.

> ## 3. Linear Regression Closed-Form Solution
>
> Consider a dataset with design matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ and target vector $y \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$.
>
> We seek a parameter vector $w \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$.
>
> ---
>
> ### 3.1 Ordinary Least Squares: No Regularization
>
> The objective is
>
> $$
> J(w) = \|Xw - y\|_2^2.
> $$
>
> Expanding,
>
> $$
> J(w) = (Xw - y)^\top (Xw - y).
> $$
>
> Differentiate with respect to $w$:
>
> $$
> \nabla_w J(w) = 2X^\top(Xw - y).
> $$
>
> Set the gradient to zero:
>
> $$
> X^\top(Xw - y) = 0.
> $$
>
> Therefore,
>
> $$
> X^\top X w = X^\top y.
> $$
>
> These are the normal equations. If $X^\top X$ is invertible, the closed-form solution is
>
> $$
> \hat{w} = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y.
> $$
>
> ### If $X^\top X$ is Not Invertible
>
> If $X^\top X$ is singular, the inverse does not exist. In that case one uses the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse:
>
> $$
> \hat{w} = X^+ y.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### 3.2 Linear Regression with $L_2$ Regularization
>
> This is ridge regression. The objective is
>
> $$
> J(w) = \|Xw - y\|_2^2 + \lambda \|w\|_2^2,
> $$
>
> where $\lambda \ge 0$ is the regularization parameter.
>
> Expanding the second term:
>
> $$
> J(w) = (Xw - y)^\top(Xw - y) + \lambda w^\top w.
> $$
>
> Differentiate:
>
> $$
> \nabla_w J(w) = 2X^\top(Xw - y) + 2\lambda w.
> $$
>
> Set the gradient to zero:
>
> $$
> X^\top(Xw - y) + \lambda w = 0.
> $$
>
> So,
>
> $$
> X^\top X w + \lambda w = X^\top y.
> $$
>
> Equivalently,
>
> $$
> (X^\top X + \lambda I)w = X^\top y.
> $$
>
> Hence the ridge solution is
>
> $$
> \hat{w}_{\text{ridge}} = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### 3.3 Intercept Term
>
> If the model includes an intercept $b$, one may write
>
> $$
> y = Xw + b\mathbf{1} + \varepsilon.
> $$
>
> A common implementation strategy is to augment $X$ with a column of ones. If regularization is used, typically the intercept is **not** regularized. In that case the regularization matrix is modified so that the intercept coefficient is excluded from the penalty.
>
> ---
>
> ### 3.4 Interpretation of $L_2$ Regularization
>
> Ridge regression shrinks coefficients toward zero. Compared with ordinary least squares:
>
> - it increases bias,
> - reduces variance,
> - improves stability under multicollinearity,
> - ensures invertibility of $X^\top X + \lambda I$ for $\lambda > 0$.
>
> This is why ridge regression often generalizes better when features are highly correlated or when $d$ is large relative to $n$.
>

> ## Final Answers
>
> ### Bias–Variance in Random Forest
> Random forests reduce variance by averaging many decorrelated decision trees. Relative to a single deep tree, they usually have much lower variance and only slightly higher bias, which often lowers overall test error.
>
> ### Linear Regression Closed-Form
> - **Without regularization**:
>
> $$
> \hat{w} = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y
> $$
>
> when $X^\top X$ is invertible.
>
> - **With $L_2$ regularization (ridge)**:
>
> $$
> \hat{w} = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y.
> $$
>
