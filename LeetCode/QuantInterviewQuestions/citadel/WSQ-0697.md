> [!example] Question 697 · Citadel
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

Suppose you want to predict y and you have predictor vectors, each with known accuracy, variance, and sample length. How do you combine these predictors to minimize the residual standard error (RSE)?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=698)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Combining Multiple Predictors to Minimize Residual Standard Error
>
> Suppose there are $k$ candidate predictors of the same target variable $y$, denoted
>
> $$
> \hat y_1,\hat y_2,\dots,\hat y_k.
> $$
>
> The goal is to combine them into a single predictor
>
> $$
> \hat y = \sum_{j=1}^k w_j \hat y_j
> $$
>
> so that the residual error is as small as possible. A standard way to formalize this is to choose weights $w_1,\dots,w_k$ to minimize the variance of the prediction error, or equivalently the mean squared residual.
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. Define the prediction errors
>
> For each predictor, define its error as
>
> $$
> e_j = y - \hat y_j.
> $$
>
> Then the combined predictor has error
>
> $$
> e = y - \hat y
> = y - \sum_{j=1}^k w_j \hat y_j.
> $$
>
> If the weights satisfy
>
> $$
> \sum_{j=1}^k w_j = 1,
> $$
>
> then
>
> $$
> e = \sum_{j=1}^k w_j e_j.
> $$
>
> Thus minimizing residual standard error amounts to minimizing the variance of the combined error.
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. General optimal solution
>
> Let the error vector be
>
> $$
> e = (e_1,\dots,e_k)^\top,
> $$
>
> and let its covariance matrix be
>
> $$
> \Sigma = \operatorname{Cov}(e).
> $$
>
> Then the variance of the combined error is
>
> $$
> \operatorname{Var}\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^k w_j e_j\right)
> = w^\top \Sigma w,
> $$
>
> where
>
> $$
> w = (w_1,\dots,w_k)^\top.
> $$
>
> So the optimization problem is
>
> $$
> \min_w \; w^\top \Sigma w
> \quad \text{subject to} \quad
> \mathbf{1}^\top w = 1,
> $$
>
> where $\mathbf{1}$ is the vector of all ones.
>
> Using Lagrange multipliers, the solution is
>
> $$
> w^* = \frac{\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{1}^\top \Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}}.
> $$
>
> This is the minimum-variance linear combination of the predictors.
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Interpretation
>
> This formula shows that:
>
> - predictors with lower error variance get larger weight;
> - predictors whose errors are highly correlated get less additional value from being combined;
> - the full covariance structure matters, not just each predictor’s individual variance.
>
> Thus, to combine predictors optimally, one needs the covariance matrix of their prediction errors.
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. If predictors are unbiased and independent
>
> A common simplified case is that each predictor is unbiased and the prediction errors are independent. Then
>
> $$
> \Sigma =
> \begin{pmatrix}
> \sigma_1^2 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
> 0 & \sigma_2^2 & \cdots & 0 \\
> \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
> 0 & 0 & \cdots & \sigma_k^2
> \end{pmatrix},
> $$
>
> where $\sigma_j^2 = \operatorname{Var}(e_j)$.
>
> Then the optimal weights reduce to inverse-variance weights:
>
> $$
> w_j^* = \frac{1/\sigma_j^2}{\sum_{m=1}^k 1/\sigma_m^2}.
> $$
>
> So the combined predictor is
>
> $$
> \hat y = \sum_{j=1}^k
> \frac{1/\sigma_j^2}{\sum_{m=1}^k 1/\sigma_m^2}
> \hat y_j.
> $$
>
> The variance of the combined error becomes
>
> $$
> \operatorname{Var}(e)
> = \frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^k 1/\sigma_j^2}.
> $$
>
> Hence the residual standard error is
>
> $$
> \operatorname{RSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^k 1/\sigma_j^2}}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 5. Where sample length enters
>
> If each predictor’s variance estimate comes from a sample of size $n_j$, then larger $n_j$ generally means the estimated error variance is more reliable. There are two distinct issues:
>
> ### (a) Minimizing true prediction error
>
> If the true error variances $\sigma_j^2$ are known, then only those variances and covariances matter. Sample size $n_j$ does not enter the optimal formula directly.
>
> ### (b) Estimating the weights from data
>
> In practice, $\sigma_j^2$ and $\Sigma$ are unknown and must be estimated. Then sample length matters because:
>
> - larger $n_j$ gives more stable estimates of accuracy and variance;
> - if one predictor is evaluated on much less data, its estimated performance is less trustworthy;
> - regularization or shrinkage may be needed if $k$ is large relative to the available data.
>
> Thus, sample length affects the reliability of the estimated optimal weights, even if it is not part of the population formula itself.
>
> ---
>
> ## 6. If you know “accuracy” rather than error variance
>
> If “accuracy” means bias and variance information are available, then proceed as follows.
>
> For predictor $j$, let
>
> $$
> b_j = \mathbb{E}[\hat y_j - y]
> $$
>
> be its bias, and let
>
> $$
> \sigma_j^2 = \operatorname{Var}(\hat y_j - y)
> $$
>
> be its error variance.
>
> Then the mean squared error of the combined predictor is
>
> $$
> \operatorname{MSE}(\hat y)
> = \operatorname{Bias}(\hat y)^2 + \operatorname{Var}(e),
> $$
>
> with
>
> $$
> \operatorname{Bias}(\hat y) = \sum_{j=1}^k w_j b_j.
> $$
>
> Therefore the objective becomes
>
> $$
> \min_w \left( \left(\sum_{j=1}^k w_j b_j\right)^2 + w^\top \Sigma w \right)
> \quad \text{subject to} \quad
> \mathbf{1}^\top w = 1.
> $$
>
> If all predictors are unbiased, this reduces to the variance-minimization problem above. If some are biased, the optimal weights trade off bias against variance.
>
> ---
>
> ## 7. Practical answer
>
> To combine the predictors so as to minimize residual standard error:
>
> 1. Compute each predictor’s residuals on a common validation sample:
>    $$
>    e_j^{(i)} = y^{(i)} - \hat y_j^{(i)}.
>    $$
>
> 2. Estimate the error covariance matrix:
>    $$
>    \hat\Sigma = \operatorname{Cov}(e_1,\dots,e_k).
>    $$
>
> 3. Set the weights to
>    $$
>    \hat w = \frac{\hat\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{1}^\top \hat\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}}.
>    $$
>
> 4. Form the ensemble predictor
>    $$
>    \hat y = \sum_{j=1}^k \hat w_j \hat y_j.
>    $$
>
> If error correlations are negligible, use inverse-error-variance weights:
>
> $$
> \hat w_j = \frac{1/\hat\sigma_j^2}{\sum_{m=1}^k 1/\hat\sigma_m^2}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Final answer
>
> The linear combination of predictors that minimizes residual standard error is obtained by weighting them according to the covariance matrix of their prediction errors. If $e_j = y - \hat y_j$ and $\Sigma = \operatorname{Cov}(e)$, then the minimum-variance weights satisfying $\sum_j w_j = 1$ are
>
> $$
> w^* = \frac{\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{1}^\top \Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}}.
> $$
>
> If the predictors’ errors are independent, this simplifies to inverse-variance weighting:
>
> $$
> w_j^* = \frac{1/\sigma_j^2}{\sum_{m=1}^k 1/\sigma_m^2}.
> $$
>
> If predictors are biased, minimize mean squared error instead:
>
> $$
> \min_w \left( \left(\sum_{j=1}^k w_j b_j\right)^2 + w^\top \Sigma w \right),
> \quad \text{subject to} \quad \mathbf{1}^\top w = 1.
> $$
>
> Sample length matters because it determines how well the variances, biases, and covariances are estimated, though the population-optimal weights themselves depend on the error covariance structure, not directly on sample size.
