> [!example] Question 713 · Citadel
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

Using stock data from the last five years, how can we build a model to predict the next day's price?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=714)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Building a Next-Day Stock Price Prediction Model from 5 Years of Data
>
> To predict the next day’s stock price using the last 5 years of data, one should treat the task as a **supervised time-series forecasting problem**. The overall procedure is:
>
> 1. collect and clean historical data,
> 2. define the prediction target,
> 3. construct predictive features,
> 4. choose appropriate models,
> 5. evaluate with time-aware validation,
> 6. deploy and monitor.
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. Define the Prediction Objective
>
> A precise target must be chosen before modeling. Common choices are:
>
> - **Next-day closing price**: predict $P_{t+1}^{\text{close}}$,
> - **Next-day return**: predict
>   $$
>   r_{t+1}=\frac{P_{t+1}^{\text{close}}-P_t^{\text{close}}}{P_t^{\text{close}}},
>   $$
> - **Direction only**: predict whether the next day goes up or down.
>
> In practice, predicting **returns** is often preferable to predicting raw prices, because prices are typically nonstationary while returns are more stable statistically.
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Gather Historical Data
>
> Using 5 years of daily stock data, collect variables such as:
>
> - open, high, low, close, adjusted close,
> - trading volume,
> - dividends and stock splits,
> - market index data such as the S\&P 500,
> - optionally macroeconomic or sentiment indicators.
>
> Let the daily data at time $t$ be denoted by
>
> $$
> X_t = \left(P_t^{\text{open}}, P_t^{\text{high}}, P_t^{\text{low}}, P_t^{\text{close}}, V_t\right).
> $$
>
> Adjusted prices should generally be used so that splits and dividends do not distort the series.
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Clean and Prepare the Data
>
> The data must be aligned and cleaned before modeling.
>
> ### (a) Handle missing values
> Missing values may occur due to holidays or incomplete records. Typical treatments include:
>
> - forward filling for some exogenous variables,
> - dropping rows with insufficient history,
> - ensuring all features at time $t$ are available before predicting time $t+1$.
>
> ### (b) Adjust for corporate actions
> Use **adjusted close** or equivalently adjusted OHLC fields where possible.
>
> ### (c) Sort chronologically
> Time-series models must preserve order. One must never randomly shuffle observations.
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. Create the Target Variable
>
> If the goal is to predict next-day return, define
>
> $$
> y_t = r_{t+1} = \ln\left(\frac{P_{t+1}}{P_t}\right)
> $$
>
> or alternatively
>
> $$
> y_t = \frac{P_{t+1}-P_t}{P_t}.
> $$
>
> If the goal is classification, define
>
> $$
> y_t =
> \begin{cases}
> 1, & \text{if } P_{t+1}>P_t,\\
> 0, & \text{otherwise.}
> \end{cases}
> $$
>
> Using log returns is common because they are additive over time and often easier to model.
>
> ---
>
> ## 5. Engineer Predictive Features
>
> Raw prices alone are usually insufficient. Good performance depends heavily on feature construction.
>
> ### (a) Lagged prices and returns
> Include recent history such as
>
> $$
> r_t,\; r_{t-1},\; r_{t-2},\; \dots,\; r_{t-k}.
> $$
>
> For example, features may include 1-day, 3-day, 5-day, and 10-day lagged returns.
>
> ### (b) Rolling statistics
> Useful summary features include:
>
> - moving averages:
>   $$
>   \text{MA}_n(t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} P_{t-i},
>   $$
> - rolling volatility:
>   $$
>   \sigma_n(t)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(r_{t-i}-\bar r)^2},
>   $$
> - rolling min/max,
> - rolling volume averages.
>
> ### (c) Technical indicators
> Common indicators include:
>
> - Relative Strength Index (RSI),
> - Moving Average Convergence Divergence (MACD),
> - Bollinger Bands,
> - stochastic oscillator,
> - momentum.
>
> These are transformations of historical prices and volume intended to capture short-term patterns.
>
> ### (d) Calendar features
> Include variables such as:
>
> - day of week,
> - month,
> - whether the date is near earnings announcements,
> - holiday effects.
>
> ### (e) Market and sector context
> A stock often moves with the broader market. Add:
>
> - index returns, such as the S\&P 500 return,
> - sector ETF returns,
> - volatility index such as $VIX$,
> - interest rates if relevant.
>
> ---
>
> ## 6. Split the Data Correctly
>
> For time series, the train-test split must respect chronology.
>
> A typical split for 5 years of daily data is:
>
> - first 3.5 to 4 years: training,
> - next several months: validation,
> - final several months: test.
>
> If the data are indexed by time, then
>
> $$
> \text{train} = \{1,\dots,T_1\}, \quad
> \text{validation} = \{T_1+1,\dots,T_2\}, \quad
> \text{test} = \{T_2+1,\dots,T\}.
> $$
>
> Random splitting should be avoided because it causes leakage from the future into the past.
>
> A stronger method is **walk-forward validation**:
>
> 1. train on an initial window,
> 2. predict the next period,
> 3. expand or roll the window,
> 4. repeat.
>
> This mimics real forecasting.
>
> ---
>
> ## 7. Choose a Modeling Approach
>
> Several model families are appropriate.
>
> ### (a) Baseline models
>
> A baseline is essential for comparison. Examples:
>
> - **naive price model**:
>   $$
>   \hat P_{t+1}=P_t,
>   $$
> - **zero-return model**:
>   $$
>   \hat r_{t+1}=0.
>   $$
>
> If a sophisticated model cannot beat these, it is not useful.
>
> ### (b) Linear regression
>
> A simple regression on engineered features is often a strong starting point:
>
> $$
> y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{t,1} + \cdots + \beta_p x_{t,p} + \varepsilon_t.
> $$
>
> Advantages:
>
> - interpretable,
> - fast,
> - effective when relationships are approximately linear.
>
> Regularized versions such as **Ridge** or **Lasso** are often better when there are many correlated predictors.
>
> ### (c) Tree-based machine learning models
>
> Useful choices include:
>
> - Random Forest,
> - Gradient Boosting,
> - XGBoost,
> - LightGBM.
>
> These models capture nonlinear relationships and feature interactions without requiring strict distributional assumptions.
>
> ### (d) Time-series models
>
> Classical time-series methods include:
>
> - ARIMA,
> - SARIMA,
> - GARCH for volatility.
>
> For example, an autoregressive model for returns is
>
> $$
> r_t = \phi_1 r_{t-1} + \cdots + \phi_p r_{t-p} + \varepsilon_t.
> $$
>
> These are effective when temporal dependence is the main signal.
>
> ### (e) Deep learning models
>
> For larger datasets or richer feature sets, one may use:
>
> - recurrent neural networks,
> - LSTM,
> - GRU,
> - Temporal Convolutional Networks,
> - Transformers for time series.
>
> These can model complex sequential dependencies, but with only 5 years of daily data, the sample size may be limited. Deep learning is therefore not always superior.
>
> ---
>
> ## 8. Standardize and Train
>
> Some models require scaling. For example, if using linear regression with regularization or neural networks, standardize each feature:
>
> $$
> x_{t,j}^{\text{scaled}} = \frac{x_{t,j}-\mu_j}{s_j},
> $$
>
> where $\mu_j$ and $s_j$ are computed using the **training set only**.
>
> Then fit the model on the training data and tune hyperparameters using the validation set or walk-forward cross-validation.
>
> ---
>
> ## 9. Evaluate the Model
>
> Evaluation depends on whether the task is regression or classification.
>
> ### (a) Regression metrics
> If predicting next-day price or return, use:
>
> - Mean Absolute Error:
>   $$
>   \text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n |y_i-\hat y_i|,
>   $$
> - Root Mean Squared Error:
>   $$
>   \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i-\hat y_i)^2},
>   $$
> - Mean Absolute Percentage Error, when appropriate.
>
> ### (b) Classification metrics
> If predicting direction, use:
>
> - accuracy,
> - precision/recall,
> - F1-score,
> - AUC.
>
> ### (c) Financial evaluation
> A statistically accurate model is not necessarily profitable. It should also be evaluated by a simple backtest. For example:
>
> - go long if $\hat r_{t+1}>0$,
> - stay out or short if $\hat r_{t+1}<0$.
>
> Then compute:
>
> - cumulative return,
> - Sharpe ratio,
> - maximum drawdown,
> - turnover,
> - transaction costs.
>
> This step is essential because small prediction errors can still lead to poor trading performance after fees.
>
> ---
>
> ## 10. Avoid Common Pitfalls
>
> ### (a) Data leakage
> Do not use future information in feature construction. Every feature at time $t$ must be computable using data up to time $t$ only.
>
> ### (b) Overfitting
> With only about 5 years of daily data, there may be roughly $1250$ observations, which is not large. Very complex models may fit noise rather than signal.
>
> ### (c) Nonstationarity
> Financial markets change over time. A model trained on older periods may degrade in new regimes.
>
> ### (d) Ignoring transaction costs
> Even if directional accuracy exceeds $50\%$, the strategy may still lose money after commissions and slippage.
>
> ---
>
> ## 11. A Practical Modeling Pipeline
>
> A sensible end-to-end procedure is:
>
> ### Step 1
> Collect 5 years of daily adjusted stock data and market index data.
>
> ### Step 2
> Compute daily log returns:
>
> $$
> r_t = \ln\left(\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}}\right).
> $$
>
> ### Step 3
> Build features such as:
>
> - lagged returns: $r_t, r_{t-1}, \dots, r_{t-5}$,
> - moving averages over $5$, $10$, and $20$ days,
> - rolling volatility over $5$ and $20$ days,
> - volume change,
> - RSI and MACD,
> - market index return.
>
> ### Step 4
> Set the target:
>
> $$
> y_t = r_{t+1}.
> $$
>
> ### Step 5
> Split chronologically into training, validation, and test sets.
>
> ### Step 6
> Train several models:
>
> - naive baseline,
> - linear regression or Ridge regression,
> - XGBoost or Random Forest,
> - optionally LSTM if enough data are available.
>
> ### Step 7
> Select the best model based on validation performance and backtest results.
>
> ### Step 8
> Test on the final holdout set.
>
> ### Step 9
> Retrain periodically as new data arrive.
>
> ---
>
> ## 12. Recommended Starting Point
>
> For an open-ended problem like this, a strong practical recommendation is:
>
> 1. predict **next-day return** rather than raw price,
> 2. use **lagged returns, rolling statistics, technical indicators, and market features**,
> 3. start with **Ridge regression** and **XGBoost**,
> 4. validate using **walk-forward testing**,
> 5. compare against the naive baseline,
> 6. evaluate both prediction error and trading performance after transaction costs.
>
> This is often more reliable than beginning with highly complex deep learning models.
>
> ---
>
> ## Final Answer
>
> Using 5 years of stock data, build a next-day prediction model by framing the task as a supervised time-series problem. Use adjusted historical data, define the target as next-day return
>
> $$
> y_t = \ln\left(\frac{P_{t+1}}{P_t}\right),
> $$
>
> create features from lagged returns, moving averages, volatility, volume, technical indicators, and market index behavior, and split the data chronologically. Train baseline, linear, and nonlinear models such as Ridge regression and XGBoost, evaluate them with walk-forward validation using metrics like RMSE or directional accuracy, and finally backtest the resulting trading rule with transaction costs. A practical first model is a return-prediction model with engineered features and time-aware validation, rather than a raw-price model with random train-test splitting.
