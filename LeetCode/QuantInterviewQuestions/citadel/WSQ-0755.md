> [!example] Question 755 · Citadel
> ## Software Developer Intern Interview

Design an algorithm to randomly select people in a city such that the probability of selecting someone from a given district is proportional to the district's population size. Additionally, discuss how you would test your implementation and optimize its runtime, considering repeated calls to the selection function with an acceptable error margin.

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=756)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Selecting a random person proportional to district population
>
> Let the city be partitioned into districts with populations
>
> $$
> p_1, p_2, \dots, p_n,
> $$
>
> where $p_i \ge 0$ and
>
> $$
> P = \sum_{i=1}^n p_i
> $$
>
> is the total population. The goal is to return district $i$ with probability
>
> $$
> \Pr(i) = \frac{p_i}{P}.
> $$
>
> If one then chooses a uniformly random resident within that district, every person in the city is selected with equal probability.
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. Basic solution
>
> ### Idea
>
> Construct the cumulative population array
>
> $$
> c_i = \sum_{j=1}^i p_j.
> $$
>
> Then draw a random integer
>
> $$
> r \in \{1,2,\dots,P\}
> $$
>
> uniformly, and return the first district $i$ such that
>
> $$
> c_i \ge r.
> $$
>
> This works because district $i$ occupies exactly the interval
>
> $$
> \{c_{i-1}+1, \dots, c_i\},
> $$
>
> whose size is $p_i$.
>
> ### Correctness
>
> Since $r$ is uniform over $P$ values, the probability of landing in district $i$ is
>
> $$
> \frac{c_i - c_{i-1}}{P} = \frac{p_i}{P}.
> $$
>
> Thus the selection is proportional to district population.
>
> ### Complexity
>
> - Preprocessing cumulative sums: $O(n)$
> - Each query by linear scan: $O(n)$
> - Extra space: $O(n)$
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Improved repeated-query solution
>
> If the function is called many times, the linear scan becomes wasteful. Since the cumulative array is sorted, use binary search.
>
> ### Algorithm
>
> 1. Precompute cumulative sums $c_1, \dots, c_n$.
> 2. For each call, sample $r \in \{1,\dots,P\}$ uniformly.
> 3. Use binary search to find the smallest $i$ with $c_i \ge r$.
>
> ### Complexity
>
> - Preprocessing: $O(n)$
> - Each query: $O(\log n)$
> - Extra space: $O(n)$
>
> This is usually the best exact solution when populations are static and many samples are needed.
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Further optimization: alias method
>
> If the function will be called extremely often, one can preprocess into an alias table.
>
> ### Result
>
> - Preprocessing: $O(n)$
> - Each query: $O(1)$
> - Extra space: $O(n)$
>
> ### When appropriate
>
> This is useful when:
>
> - the distribution is fixed,
> - many repeated samples are required,
> - one wants exact sampling with constant-time queries.
>
> The tradeoff is more complex implementation and preprocessing.
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. Approximate solution with acceptable error
>
> If some sampling error is acceptable, one may use approximation to reduce costs in special settings.
>
> ### Example approach: grouping or rounding weights
>
> Replace populations $p_i$ by approximations $\tilde p_i$ and sample from
>
> $$
> \tilde P = \sum_{i=1}^n \tilde p_i.
> $$
>
> Then district $i$ is selected with approximate probability
>
> $$
> \tilde q_i = \frac{\tilde p_i}{\tilde P}
> $$
>
> instead of the exact
>
> $$
> q_i = \frac{p_i}{P}.
> $$
>
> The approximation is acceptable if
>
> $$
> | \tilde q_i - q_i |
> $$
>
> is below the required tolerance.
>
> This can reduce memory or speed up updates, but only makes sense if the problem explicitly permits bias.
>
> ### Important distinction
>
> Repeated random sampling already has statistical fluctuation, but that is not algorithmic error. Approximation introduces systematic bias. These should not be confused.
>
> ---
>
> ## 5. Testing
>
> Testing should verify both correctness and statistical behavior.
>
> ### A. Deterministic unit tests
>
> Use a fixed random seed and test simple cases.
>
> #### Case 1: one district
>
> If
>
> $$
> p = [100],
> $$
>
> the algorithm must always return district $1$.
>
> #### Case 2: equal populations
>
> If
>
> $$
> p = [10,10,10],
> $$
>
> each district should have probability
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{3}.
> $$
>
> #### Case 3: zero-population district
>
> If
>
> $$
> p = [5,0,5],
> $$
>
> district $2$ should never be returned.
>
> #### Case 4: cumulative-boundary cases
>
> Check that values exactly at cumulative boundaries map correctly.
>
> For example, if
>
> $$
> p = [2,3,4],
> $$
>
> then cumulative sums are
>
> $$
> [2,5,9].
> $$
>
> Thus:
>
> - $r = 1,2 \mapsto 1$
> - $r = 3,4,5 \mapsto 2$
> - $r = 6,7,8,9 \mapsto 3$
>
> This catches off-by-one errors.
>
> ---
>
> ### B. Statistical tests
>
> Run the sampler many times, say $N$ trials, and compare observed counts with expected counts.
>
> If district $i$ has true probability
>
> $$
> q_i = \frac{p_i}{P},
> $$
>
> then the expected count is
>
> $$
> N q_i.
> $$
>
> One may use:
>
> - a chi-square goodness-of-fit test,
> - confidence intervals,
> - or simply check that empirical frequencies are close to expected values.
>
> For large $N$, the empirical proportion $\hat q_i$ should be near $q_i$, typically within about
>
> $$
> O\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{q_i(1-q_i)}{N}}\right).
> $$
>
> This tests whether the implementation is statistically plausible.
>
> ---
>
> ### C. Property-based tests
>
> Generate random population arrays and verify invariants:
>
> - returned district index is valid,
> - zero-population districts are never chosen,
> - scaling all populations by the same constant does not change probabilities,
> - merging two districts gives combined probability equal to the sum of their original probabilities.
>
> These tests are excellent for catching subtle logic errors.
>
> ---
>
> ## 6. Edge cases and engineering concerns
>
> ### Large populations
>
> If populations are large, the total
>
> $$
> P = \sum_i p_i
> $$
>
> may overflow standard integer types. Use sufficiently wide integers, such as $64$-bit integers, or arbitrary-precision arithmetic if needed.
>
> ### Invalid input
>
> Handle cases such as:
>
> - empty district list,
> - negative populations,
> - all populations equal to zero.
>
> These should generally raise an error.
>
> ### Updates
>
> If district populations change frequently:
>
> - rebuilding the cumulative array each time costs $O(n)$,
> - a Fenwick tree or segment tree supports:
>   - updates in $O(\log n)$,
>   - weighted sampling in $O(\log n)$.
>
> This is preferable when there are many interleaved updates and queries.
>
> ---
>
> ## 7. Summary of approaches
>
> ### Single or few queries
>
> Use cumulative sums plus linear scan.
>
> - Preprocessing: $O(n)$
> - Query: $O(n)$
>
> ### Many repeated queries, static populations
>
> Use cumulative sums plus binary search.
>
> - Preprocessing: $O(n)$
> - Query: $O(\log n)$
>
> ### Extremely many repeated queries, static populations
>
> Use the alias method.
>
> - Preprocessing: $O(n)$
> - Query: $O(1)$
>
> ### Many updates and queries
>
> Use a Fenwick tree or segment tree.
>
> - Update: $O(\log n)$
> - Query: $O(\log n)$
>
> ---
>
> ## Final answer
>
> A correct way to select a person proportionally to district population is to treat district populations as weights. Precompute cumulative populations
>
> $$
> c_i = \sum_{j=1}^i p_j,
> $$
>
> draw a uniform random integer
>
> $$
> r \in \{1,\dots,P\}, \qquad P=\sum_{i=1}^n p_i,
> $$
>
> and return the first district $i$ such that
>
> $$
> c_i \ge r.
> $$
>
> This gives
>
> $$
> \Pr(i)=\frac{p_i}{P}.
> $$
>
> For repeated calls, optimize the lookup with binary search for $O(\log n)$$ $per query, or use the alias method for $O(1)$$ $per query after $O(n)$ preprocessing. Test with boundary cases, zero-population districts, and statistical goodness-of-fit over many trials. If populations change frequently, use a Fenwick tree or segment tree to support both updates and weighted random selection in $O(\log n)$.
