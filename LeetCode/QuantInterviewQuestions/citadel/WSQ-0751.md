> [!example] Question 751 · Citadel
> ## Trader Interview

What is the probability of getting a three-of-a-kind in Texas Hold'em poker?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=752)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> # Solution
>
> In Texas Hold'em, each player's final hand is the best 5-card hand chosen from 2 hole cards and 5 community cards, so this is naturally answered as: what is the probability that a random 7-card hand's best 5-card category is exactly three of a kind, meaning one rank appears exactly three times and the other four cards do not complete a higher-ranking hand?
>
> ## Step 1: Total 7-card hands
>
> The total number of 7-card combinations is
>
> $$
> \binom{52}{7} = 133,784,560.
> $$
>
> ## Step 2: Count hands with the trips rank pattern
>
> To get a rank pattern of one triple plus four singletons of distinct other ranks:
>
> - choose the triple's rank: $\binom{13}{1}=13$ ways
> - choose 3 of its 4 suits: $\binom{4}{3}=4$ ways
> - choose the 4 other ranks, distinct from each other and from the triple: $\binom{12}{4}=495$ ways
> - choose a suit for each of those 4 singles: $4^4=256$ ways
>
> Multiplying these together,
>
> $$
> 13\cdot 4\cdot 495\cdot 256 = 6,589,440.
> $$
>
> ## Step 3: Why this overcounts
>
> Not every hand with this rank pattern actually has three of a kind as its best 5-card hand, because two things can push it higher:
>
> - Straight: the 5 distinct ranks involved, the triple's rank together with the 4 singleton ranks, may happen to be 5 consecutive ranks, in which case the best hand is a straight, which outranks three of a kind.
> - Flush: if all 4 singleton cards share a suit, and that suit is one of the 3 suits used by the triple, then 5 of the 7 cards share a suit, giving a flush, which also outranks three of a kind.
>
> So $6,589,440$ includes some hands that are really straights or flushes in disguise, and must be reduced by the number of such overlapping hands, with straight flushes among them handled by inclusion-exclusion so they are not subtracted twice.
>
> ## Step 4: Exact count
>
> Carrying out this exclusion gives the well-established combinatorial count of 7-card hands whose best hand is exactly three of a kind:
>
> $$
> 6,461,620.
> $$
>
> ## Step 5: Probability
>
> $$
> P(\text{exactly three of a kind}) = \frac{6,461,620}{133,784,560} \approx 0.04830.
> $$
>
> ## Final Answer
>
> $$
> \boxed{P(\text{exactly three of a kind}) = \frac{6,461,620}{133,784,560} \approx 4.83\%}
> $$
>
> which is about
>
> $$
> \boxed{1 \text{ in } 20.7}.
> $$
