> [!example] Question 708 · Citadel
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

Suppose there are 10 lions and a piece of meat. If any one of the lions eats the meat, that lion falls asleep. While asleep, any other lion can eat her and will also fall asleep. This process continues in the same way. What will happen at the beginning? Will any lion eat the meat?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=709)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ### Solution
>
> Use backward reasoning on the number of lions.
>
> The key observation is:
>
> - A lion will eat the meat **only if** doing so does **not** lead to her own death.
> - If a lion eats the meat, she falls asleep immediately.
> - Then the remaining lions decide whether one of them should eat the sleeping lion.
>
> So the problem reduces to understanding what happens with smaller numbers of lions.
>
> ---
>
> ### Step 1: Small cases
>
> #### Case $n=1$
> There is only one lion and the meat.
>
> - If she eats the meat, no other lion is present to eat her.
> - So she survives.
>
> Hence, with $1$ lion, the lion **eats the meat**.
>
> #### Case $n=2$
> Suppose one lion considers eating the meat.
>
> - She falls asleep.
> - The other lion can then eat her.
> - Since after doing so there are no lions left to threaten him, he survives.
>
> So with $2$ lions, the first lion will **not** eat the meat, because eating it guarantees death.
>
> #### Case $n=3$
> Suppose one lion eats the meat.
>
> - She falls asleep.
> - Now there are $2$ lions facing a sleeping lion.
> - From the $n=2$ case, when there are $2$ lions competing for a piece of meat, neither wants to be the one to eat if that causes vulnerability.
> - Thus neither of the two remaining lions will eat the sleeping lion.
>
> So with $3$ lions, a lion **will** eat the meat.
>
> ---
>
> ### Step 2: Inductive pattern
>
> This creates an alternating pattern:
>
> - If with $n-1$ lions, no lion would eat available meat, then with $n$ lions, one lion can safely eat the meat.
> - If with $n-1$ lions, some lion would eat available meat, then with $n$ lions, no lion wants to be the first to eat.
>
> Thus:
>
> - For **odd** $n$, a lion **eats** the meat.
> - For **even** $n$, **no** lion eats the meat.
>
> ---
>
> ### Step 3: Apply to $n=10$
>
> Since $10$ is even, the lions will reason that if any one of them eats the meat, she will be eaten by another lion.
>
> Therefore, **no lion will eat the meat at the beginning**.
>
> ### Final Answer
>
> With $10$ lions, **none of the lions will eat the meat**.
