> [!example] Question 721 · Citadel
> ## Quantitative Research Interview

Why is regularization important, for example using ridge or lasso regression compared to ordinary least squares (OLS), even when the number of samples is greater than the number of parameters?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=722)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Ordinary least squares minimizes training error, but low training error does not guarantee good prediction on new data. Regularization is important even when the number of samples satisfies $n > p$ because the OLS estimator can still have high variance, be unstable, and overfit noise.
>
> ## 1. OLS is unbiased, but not necessarily good for prediction
>
> In the linear model
> $$
> y = X\beta + \varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim (0,\sigma^2 I),
> $$
> the OLS estimator is
> $$
> \hat\beta_{\mathrm{OLS}} = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y,
> $$
> assuming $X^\top X$ is invertible.
>
> Its covariance is
> $$
> \mathrm{Var}(\hat\beta_{\mathrm{OLS}}) = \sigma^2 (X^\top X)^{-1}.
> $$
> Thus, even when $n > p$, if $X^\top X$ is close to singular, the entries of $(X^\top X)^{-1}$ can be large, so the estimator has high variance. This makes predictions sensitive to small changes in the data.
>
> Regularization reduces this variance, often improving test error, even though it introduces some bias. This is the bias–variance tradeoff.
>
> ## 2. Multicollinearity makes OLS unstable
>
> If predictors are highly correlated, then $X^\top X$ is ill-conditioned. In that case:
>
> - estimated coefficients can become very large,
> - small perturbations in the data can cause large changes in $\hat\beta$,
> - predictions on new data may be poor.
>
> Ridge regression replaces OLS with
> $$
> \hat\beta_{\mathrm{ridge}}
> = \arg\min_\beta \left\{ \|y - X\beta\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\beta\|_2^2 \right\},
> $$
> giving
> $$
> \hat\beta_{\mathrm{ridge}} = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y.
> $$
> The matrix $X^\top X + \lambda I$ is better conditioned than $X^\top X$, so the estimator is more stable.
>
> ## 3. OLS can overfit noise even when $n > p$
>
> Having more samples than parameters only guarantees that the OLS problem is identifiable under suitable rank conditions. It does **not** imply that the fitted coefficients reflect the true signal rather than noise.
>
> If some predictors have weak or no true relationship with the response, OLS will still fit them to reduce training error. This can hurt generalization. Regularization discourages unnecessary complexity:
>
> - ridge shrinks all coefficients toward $0$,
> - lasso can set some coefficients exactly to $0$.
>
> Lasso solves
> $$
> \hat\beta_{\mathrm{lasso}}
> = \arg\min_\beta \left\{ \|y - X\beta\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\beta\|_1 \right\}.
> $$
> This is useful when the true signal is sparse or approximately sparse.
>
> ## 4. Lower prediction error can come from a biased estimator
>
> For prediction, one cares about mean squared prediction error, not only unbiasedness of coefficients. A biased estimator can outperform an unbiased one if it has much lower variance.
>
> This is the key reason ridge often beats OLS in practice: although ridge is biased,
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[\hat\beta_{\mathrm{ridge}}] \neq \beta,
> $$
> its variance is typically smaller than that of OLS, and the total error
> $$
> \mathrm{MSE} = \mathrm{Bias}^2 + \mathrm{Variance} + \text{irreducible noise}
> $$
> can be smaller.
>
> ## 5. Regularization incorporates a preference for simpler models
>
> Regularization imposes structure:
>
> - ridge prefers small coefficients,
> - lasso prefers sparse coefficients.
>
> This is valuable when many different coefficient vectors fit the observed data similarly well. Among them, regularization chooses one with controlled complexity, which is often more plausible and more robust.
>
> From a Bayesian viewpoint:
>
> - ridge corresponds to a Gaussian prior on coefficients,
> - lasso corresponds to a Laplace prior.
>
> Thus regularization encodes prior belief that extremely large or numerous nonzero coefficients are unlikely.
>
> ## 6. Better numerical behavior
>
> Even when prediction is not the main concern, regularization can improve numerical stability. If the design matrix is ill-conditioned, OLS can be computationally unstable. Ridge in particular alleviates this by shifting the eigenvalues of $X^\top X$ away from $0$.
>
> ## 7. When OLS may be sufficient
>
> If:
>
> - $n$ is much larger than $p$,
> - predictors are not strongly correlated,
> - signal-to-noise ratio is high,
> - the model is correctly specified,
>
> then OLS may perform well, and regularization may offer little benefit. But these conditions are often not met in real data.
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> Regularization is important even when $n > p$ because the main issue is not just existence of the OLS solution. The real concerns are:
>
> - high variance,
> - multicollinearity,
> - overfitting noise,
> - unstable coefficients,
> - poor out-of-sample prediction.
>
> Ridge and lasso improve these by shrinking coefficients, trading a small amount of bias for often substantial reductions in variance and better generalization.
