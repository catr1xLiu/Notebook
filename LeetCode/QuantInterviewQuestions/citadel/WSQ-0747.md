> [!example] Question 747 · Citadel
> ## Trader Interview

What is the probability of finding two consecutive ones in a randomly generated binary string of length n, where each bit is equally likely to be 0 or 1?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=748)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let a binary string of length $n$ be chosen uniformly at random from the $2^n$ strings of $0$’s and $1$’s. We seek the probability that the string contains at least one occurrence of two consecutive $1$’s.
>
> ## Step 1: Count the complementary event
>
> It is easier to count the number of binary strings of length $n$ with **no** two consecutive $1$’s.
>
> Let $a_n$ denote this number.
>
> A valid string of length $n$ must begin in one of two ways:
>
> - If it begins with $0$, the remaining $n-1$ bits can be any valid string of length $n-1$, giving $a_{n-1}$ possibilities.
> - If it begins with $1$, then the next bit must be $0$, and the remaining $n-2$ bits can be any valid string of length $n-2$, giving $a_{n-2}$ possibilities.
>
> Hence,
> $$
> a_n = a_{n-1} + a_{n-2}.
> $$
>
> The initial values are
> $$
> a_1 = 2 \quad (\text{strings } 0,1),
> $$
> $$
> a_2 = 3 \quad (\text{strings } 00,01,10).
> $$
>
> Thus $a_n$ follows the Fibonacci recurrence. With the convention $F_1=1$, $F_2=1$, this gives
> $$
> a_n = F_{n+2}.
> $$
>
> ## Step 2: Convert to probability
>
> There are $2^n$ total binary strings of length $n$.
>
> Therefore, the probability that a random binary string has **no** two consecutive $1$’s is
> $$
> \frac{F_{n+2}}{2^n}.
> $$
>
> So the probability that it has **at least one** pair of consecutive $1$’s is
> $$
> 1 - \frac{F_{n+2}}{2^n}.
> $$
>
> ## Final answer
>
> $$
> \boxed{\Pr(\text{at least one occurrence of }11)=1-\frac{F_{n+2}}{2^n}}
> $$
>
> where $F_k$ is the $k$th Fibonacci number, defined by
> $$
> F_1=1,\quad F_2=1,\quad F_k=F_{k-1}+F_{k-2}\quad (k\ge 3).
> $$
