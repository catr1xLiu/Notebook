> [!example] Question 793 · Citadel
> ## Quant Research Intern Interview

How do you derive the Black-Scholes formula for pricing European options?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=794)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> # Derivation of the Black–Scholes Formula
>
> ## 1. Market model and assumptions
>
> Assume a frictionless market with:
>
> - a risk-free asset $B_t$ satisfying
>   $$
>   dB_t = r B_t\,dt, \qquad B_t = e^{rt},
>   $$
>   where $r$ is constant;
>
> - a stock price $S_t$ following geometric Brownian motion
>   $$
>   dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t,
>   $$
>   where $\mu$ is the stock’s expected return, $\sigma > 0$ is constant volatility, and $W_t$ is a Brownian motion.
>
> Let $V(S,t)$ denote the price at time $t$ of a European derivative with terminal payoff
> $$
> V(S,T) = \Phi(S).
> $$
> For a European call with strike $K$,
> $$
> \Phi(S) = (S-K)^+.
> $$
>
> The goal is to find $V(S,t)$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Apply Itô’s lemma to the option price
>
> Since $V = V(S,t)$ and $S_t$ is stochastic, Itô’s lemma gives
> $$
> dV = \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}\,dt + \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\,dS + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}(dS)^2.
> $$
> Using
> $$
> dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW_t,
> \qquad
> (dS)^2 = \sigma^2 S^2\,dt,
> $$
> we obtain
> $$
> dV
> =
> \left(
> \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}
> +
> \mu S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}
> +
> \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}
> \right)dt
> +
> \sigma S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\,dW_t.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Construct a riskless portfolio
>
> Form a portfolio $\Pi$ consisting of:
>
> - one option, long;
> - $\Delta$ shares of stock, short.
>
> Thus
> $$
> \Pi = V - \Delta S.
> $$
> Its differential is
> $$
> d\Pi = dV - \Delta\, dS.
> $$
> Substitute the expressions for $dV$ and $dS$:
> $$
> d\Pi
> =
> \left(
> \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}
> +
> \mu S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}
> +
> \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}
> \right)dt
> +
> \sigma S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\,dW_t
> -
> \Delta(\mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW_t).
> $$
> Choose
> $$
> \Delta = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}.
> $$
> Then the stochastic term disappears:
> $$
> d\Pi
> =
> \left(
> \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}
> +
> \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}
> \right)dt.
> $$
> This portfolio is locally riskless, so by no-arbitrage it must earn the risk-free rate:
> $$
> d\Pi = r\Pi\,dt = r\left(V - S\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\right)dt.
> $$
> Hence
> $$
> \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}
> +
> \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}
> =
> r\left(V - S\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\right).
> $$
> Rearranging,
> $$
> \boxed{
> \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}
> +
> \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}
> +
> rS\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}
> -
> rV
> =
> 0
> }
> $$
> This is the **Black–Scholes partial differential equation**.
>
> For a European call, the terminal condition is
> $$
> V(S,T) = (S-K)^+.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. Transform the PDE into the heat equation
>
> Let
> $$
> \tau = T-t.
> $$
> Then
> $$
> \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial \tau}.
> $$
> The PDE becomes
> $$
> \frac{\partial V}{\partial \tau}
> =
> \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}
> +
> rS\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}
> -
> rV.
> $$
>
> Now set
> $$
> x = \ln\!\left(\frac{S}{K}\right),
> \qquad
> V(S,t) = K\,v(x,\tau).
> $$
> Using
> $$
> S\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} = K\frac{\partial v}{\partial x},
> \qquad
> S^2\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} = K\left(\frac{\partial^2 v}{\partial x^2} - \frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\right),
> $$
> the PDE becomes
> $$
> \frac{\partial v}{\partial \tau}
> =
> \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \frac{\partial^2 v}{\partial x^2}
> +
> \left(r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}
> -
> rv.
> $$
>
> To remove the first-order and zeroth-order terms, let
> $$
> v(x,\tau) = e^{\alpha x + \beta \tau}u(x,\tau).
> $$
> Choose $\alpha,\beta$ so that the PDE for $u$ becomes
> $$
> \frac{\partial u}{\partial \tau}
> =
> \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}.
> $$
> A standard calculation gives
> $$
> \alpha = -\frac{r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2}{\sigma^2}
> = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{r}{\sigma^2},
> $$
> and
> $$
> \beta = -\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \alpha^2 - \left(r-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)\alpha + r.
> $$
> Thus the problem is reduced to the heat equation with transformed terminal data.
>
> ---
>
> ## 5. Solve for the European call
>
> Although one may continue with the heat kernel directly, the cleanest final form comes from the equivalent risk-neutral valuation implied by the PDE. Under the risk-neutral measure,
> $$
> dS_t = rS_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t^{\mathbb{Q}},
> $$
> so
> $$
> S_T = S_t \exp\!\left[\left(r-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)(T-t) + \sigma\sqrt{T-t}\,Z\right],
> $$
> where $Z \sim N(0,1)$.
>
> Hence the call price is
> $$
> C(S,t) = e^{-r(T-t)}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[(S_T-K)^+ \mid S_t=S\right].
> $$
> Write $\tau = T-t$. Then
> $$
> C = e^{-r\tau}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[(S e^{(r-\frac12\sigma^2)\tau + \sigma\sqrt{\tau}Z} - K)^+\right].
> $$
> The event $S_T > K$ is equivalent to
> $$
> Z > \frac{\ln(K/S) - \left(r-\frac12\sigma^2\right)\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}
> = -d_2,
> $$
> where
> $$
> d_2 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + \left(r-\frac12\sigma^2\right)\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}.
> $$
> Define
> $$
> d_1 = d_2 + \sigma\sqrt{\tau}
> = \frac{\ln(S/K) + \left(r+\frac12\sigma^2\right)\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}.
> $$
> Then
> $$
> C
> =
> e^{-r\tau}
> \left(
> \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[S_T \mathbf{1}_{\{S_T>K\}}]
> -
> K\,\mathbb{Q}(S_T>K)
> \right).
> $$
> Now
> $$
> \mathbb{Q}(S_T>K) = N(d_2),
> $$
> where $N(\cdot)$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function.
>
> Also,
> $$
> \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[S_T \mathbf{1}_{\{S_T>K\}}]
> =
> S e^{r\tau} N(d_1).
> $$
> Therefore
> $$
> C = e^{-r\tau}\left(S e^{r\tau}N(d_1) - K N(d_2)\right),
> $$
> so
> $$
> \boxed{
> C(S,t) = S\,N(d_1) - K e^{-r(T-t)} N(d_2)
> }
> $$
> with
> $$
> \boxed{
> d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + \left(r+\frac12\sigma^2\right)(T-t)}{\sigma\sqrt{T-t}},
> \qquad
> d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T-t}.
> }
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 6. Put formula
>
> By put–call parity,
> $$
> C - P = S - K e^{-r(T-t)},
> $$
> hence
> $$
> \boxed{
> P(S,t) = K e^{-r(T-t)}N(-d_2) - S N(-d_1).
> }
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 7. Final result
>
> For a European call option on a non-dividend-paying stock, the Black–Scholes formula is
> $$
> \boxed{
> C = S\,N(d_1) - K e^{-r(T-t)} N(d_2)
> }
> $$
> where
> $$
> \boxed{
> d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + \left(r+\frac12\sigma^2\right)(T-t)}{\sigma\sqrt{T-t}},
> \qquad
> d_2 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + \left(r-\frac12\sigma^2\right)(T-t)}{\sigma\sqrt{T-t}}.
> }
> $$
>
> For a European put,
> $$
> \boxed{
> P = K e^{-r(T-t)}N(-d_2) - S N(-d_1).
> }
> $$
>
> The essential idea is:
>
> 1. model the stock by geometric Brownian motion;
> 2. apply Itô’s lemma to the derivative price;
> 3. hedge away randomness with $\Delta = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}$;
> 4. impose no-arbitrage to obtain the Black–Scholes PDE;
> 5. solve the PDE, equivalently evaluate the discounted risk-neutral expected payoff.
