> [!example] Question 746 · Citadel
> ## Trader Interview

How would you construct an options position that is long vega and short gamma?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=747)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> To construct a position that is **long vega** and **short gamma**, use an option structure that benefits from increases in implied volatility but has negative convexity with respect to the underlying price.
>
> ## Key sign relationships
>
> For standard options:
>
> - **Long options** have:
>   - $\Gamma > 0$
>   - $\text{Vega} > 0$
>
> - **Short options** have:
>   - $\Gamma < 0$
>   - $\text{Vega} < 0$
>
> Thus, to obtain **short gamma**, the position must contain a net **short option convexity** component. But to remain **long vega**, the position must also have stronger exposure to volatility from some other options.
>
> The usual way to achieve this is with a **calendar spread**.
>
> ## Construction: long calendar spread
>
> A standard example is:
>
> - **Buy** a longer-dated option
> - **Sell** a shorter-dated option
> - Same strike and same option type
>
> For example:
>
> - Long $1$ six-month at-the-money call
> - Short $1$ one-month at-the-money call
>
> ## Why this works
>
> ### Vega
>
> Longer-dated options typically have much larger vega than shorter-dated options. Therefore,
>
> $$
> \text{Vega}_{\text{position}}
> =
> \text{Vega}_{\text{long dated}}
> -
> \text{Vega}_{\text{short dated}}
> 0
> $$
>
> so the position is **long vega**.
>
> ### Gamma
>
> Shorter-dated at-the-money options usually have much higher gamma than longer-dated options. Since you are short the near-dated option,
>
> $$
> \Gamma_{\text{position}}
> =
> \Gamma_{\text{long dated}}
> -
> \Gamma_{\text{short dated}}
> < 0
> $$
>
> provided the short near-term gamma dominates. Hence the position is **short gamma**.
>
> ## Intuition
>
> - **Vega** increases with maturity, so the long back-month option contributes a lot of positive vega.
> - **Gamma** is concentrated in short-dated at-the-money options, so shorting the front-month option gives a large negative gamma.
>
> This is exactly the combination needed.
>
> ## Final answer
>
> A typical **long vega, short gamma** position is a **long calendar spread**:
>
> $$
> \text{Buy long-maturity option, sell short-maturity option at the same strike.}
> $$
>
> Example:
>
> $$
> +1 \text{ six-month ATM call} \;-\; 1 \text{ one-month ATM call}
> $$
>
> This is generally:
>
> $$
> \text{Vega} > 0, \qquad \Gamma < 0.
> $$
