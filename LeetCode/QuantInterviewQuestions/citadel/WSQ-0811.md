> [!example] Question 811 · Citadel
> ## Software Engineer Intern Interview

Find the number of pairs of indices (i, j) in an array v such that v\[i\] = v\[j\] = v\[i+1\] + v\[i+2\] + ... + v\[j-1\]. Design an algorithm with time complexity O(n log n).

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=812)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> We must count pairs of indices $(i,j)$ such that
>
> $$
> v[i] = v[j] = v[i+1] + v[i+2] + \cdots + v[j-1].
> $$
>
> Assume $i < j$. Then the condition is equivalent to saying:
>
> - the two endpoints are equal, and
> - that common value equals the sum of the elements strictly between them.
>
> We derive a form suitable for counting in $O(n \log n)$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. Prefix sums
>
> Define prefix sums
>
> $$
> P[0] = 0, \qquad P[k] = v[1] + v[2] + \cdots + v[k] \quad \text{for } k \ge 1.
> $$
>
> Then the sum of the interior segment is
>
> $$
> v[i+1] + \cdots + v[j-1] = P[j-1] - P[i].
> $$
>
> So the condition becomes
>
> $$
> v[i] = v[j]
> $$
>
> and
>
> $$
> v[i] = P[j-1] - P[i].
> $$
>
> Using
>
> $$
> P[i] = P[i-1] + v[i],
> $$
>
> the second equation becomes
>
> $$
> P[i] - P[i-1] = P[j-1] - P[i].
> $$
>
> Rearranging,
>
> $$
> 2P[i] = P[i-1] + P[j-1].
> $$
>
> Thus a pair $(i,j)$ is valid iff
>
> $$
> v[i] = v[j]
> \quad \text{and} \quad
> 2P[i] = P[i-1] + P[j-1].
> $$
>
> Now use $v[i] = v[j] = P[j] - P[j-1]$. Since $v[i] = P[i]-P[i-1]$, the equality $v[i]=v[j]$ can be written as
>
> $$
> P[i]-P[i-1] = P[j]-P[j-1].
> $$
>
> But there is a simpler way.
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. A cleaner transformation
>
> Starting from
>
> $$
> v[i] = v[i+1] + \cdots + v[j-1],
> $$
>
> add $v[i]$ to both sides:
>
> $$
> 2v[i] = v[i] + v[i+1] + \cdots + v[j-1].
> $$
>
> Hence
>
> $$
> 2v[i] = P[j-1] - P[i-1].
> $$
>
> Since also $v[i] = v[j]$, we get
>
> $$
> 2v[j] = P[j-1] - P[i-1].
> $$
>
> Using $v[j] = P[j] - P[j-1]$,
>
> $$
> 2(P[j]-P[j-1]) = P[j-1] - P[i-1].
> $$
>
> So
>
> $$
> P[i-1] = 3P[j-1] - 2P[j].
> $$
>
> Similarly, from $2v[i] = P[j-1]-P[i-1]$ and $v[i]=P[i]-P[i-1]$,
>
> $$
> 2(P[i]-P[i-1]) = P[j-1]-P[i-1],
> $$
>
> thus
>
> $$
> 2P[i] - P[i-1] = P[j-1].
> $$
>
> This last equation is especially useful:
>
> $$
> P[j-1] = 2P[i] - P[i-1].
> $$
>
> And we must also have
>
> $$
> v[j] = v[i].
> $$
>
> But once $P[j-1]$ is fixed, this means
>
> $$
> P[j] = P[j-1] + v[i].
> $$
>
> Therefore for a fixed $i$, the index $j$ is valid exactly when
>
> $$
> P[j-1] = 2P[i] - P[i-1]
> $$
>
> and
>
> $$
> P[j] = P[j-1] + v[i].
> $$
>
> That suggests storing information about adjacent prefix sums.
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Reformulation as counting equal pairs of values
>
> For each index $j$, define the pair
>
> $$
> A_j = \bigl(P[j-1],\, P[j]\bigr).
> $$
>
> For a fixed $i$, define the target pair
>
> $$
> T_i = \bigl(2P[i]-P[i-1],\, 3P[i]-2P[i-1]\bigr),
> $$
>
> because
>
> $$
> P[j-1] = 2P[i]-P[i-1]
> $$
>
> and then
>
> $$
> P[j] = P[j-1] + v[i]
> = (2P[i]-P[i-1]) + (P[i]-P[i-1])
> = 3P[i]-2P[i-1].
> $$
>
> So $(i,j)$ is valid iff
>
> $$
> A_j = T_i.
> $$
>
> Thus the problem becomes:
>
> - for each $i$, count indices $j>i$ such that
> $$
> (P[j-1],P[j]) = (2P[i]-P[i-1],\, 3P[i]-2P[i-1]).
> $$
>
> This can be done by processing indices from right to left and maintaining counts of pairs $(P[j-1],P[j])$ for all already-seen $j$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. Data structure and algorithm
>
> Process $i$ from $n$ down to $1$.
>
> Maintain a map $\text{cnt}$ where
>
> $$
> \text{cnt}\bigl[(x,y)\bigr]
> $$
>
> is the number of indices $j>i$ with
>
> $$
> (P[j-1],P[j]) = (x,y).
> $$
>
> At step $i$:
>
> 1. Compute
>    $$
>    x = 2P[i]-P[i-1], \qquad y = 3P[i]-2P[i-1].
>    $$
> 2. Add
>    $$
>    \text{cnt}[(x,y)]
>    $$
>    to the answer.
> 3. Insert index $i$ for future use:
>    $$
>    \text{cnt}[(P[i-1],P[i])] \mathrel{+}= 1.
>    $$
>
> Since each map operation costs $O(\log n)$ with an ordered map, total complexity is $O(n \log n)$. With a hash map, expected complexity is $O(n)$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 5. Correctness proof
>
> We prove that the algorithm counts exactly all valid pairs.
>
> ### Lemma 1
> For indices $i<j$, the pair $(i,j)$ is valid iff
>
> $$
> (P[j-1],P[j]) = (2P[i]-P[i-1],\, 3P[i]-2P[i-1]).
> $$
>
> #### Proof
> The pair $(i,j)$ is valid iff
>
> $$
> v[i] = v[j] = v[i+1]+\cdots+v[j-1].
> $$
>
> From
>
> $$
> v[i] = v[i+1]+\cdots+v[j-1],
> $$
>
> we get
>
> $$
> 2v[i] = v[i]+\cdots+v[j-1] = P[j-1]-P[i-1].
> $$
>
> Since
>
> $$
> v[i] = P[i]-P[i-1],
> $$
>
> this yields
>
> $$
> 2(P[i]-P[i-1]) = P[j-1]-P[i-1],
> $$
>
> so
>
> $$
> P[j-1] = 2P[i]-P[i-1].
> $$
>
> Also, because $v[j]=v[i]$,
>
> $$
> P[j] = P[j-1] + v[j] = P[j-1] + v[i].
> $$
>
> Substituting the formulas above,
>
> $$
> P[j] = (2P[i]-P[i-1]) + (P[i]-P[i-1]) = 3P[i]-2P[i-1].
> $$
>
> Thus validity implies the stated pair equality.
>
> Conversely, if
>
> $$
> P[j-1] = 2P[i]-P[i-1], \qquad P[j]=3P[i]-2P[i-1],
> $$
>
> then
>
> $$
> v[j] = P[j]-P[j-1] = P[i]-P[i-1] = v[i],
> $$
>
> and
>
> $$
> P[j-1]-P[i] = (2P[i]-P[i-1]) - P[i] = P[i]-P[i-1] = v[i].
> $$
>
> Hence
>
> $$
> v[i+1]+\cdots+v[j-1] = P[j-1]-P[i] = v[i] = v[j],
> $$
>
> so $(i,j)$ is valid. ∎
>
> ---
>
> ### Lemma 2
> When processing index $i$ from right to left, the map contains exactly the counts of pairs $(P[j-1],P[j])$ for all $j>i$.
>
> #### Proof
> Initially, before processing any index, the map is empty, which is correct. After processing index $i$, we insert $(P[i-1],P[i])$, so before the next iteration the map contains exactly the pairs for all indices to the right. By induction, the claim holds throughout. ∎
>
> ---
>
> ### Theorem
> The algorithm returns the number of valid pairs $(i,j)$.
>
> #### Proof
> By Lemma 1, for a fixed $i$, valid indices $j>i$ are exactly those with
>
> $$
> (P[j-1],P[j]) = (2P[i]-P[i-1],\,3P[i]-2P[i-1]).
> $$
>
> By Lemma 2, the map count for this target pair equals the number of such $j$. Summing this over all $i$ counts every valid pair exactly once. Therefore the algorithm is correct. ∎
>
> ---
>
> ## 6. Complexity
>
> - Prefix sums: $O(n)$.
> - For each index, one query and one insertion into a map:
>   - $O(\log n)$ each with an ordered map,
>   - expected $O(1)$ each with a hash map.
>
> Therefore:
>
> $$
> O(n \log n)
> $$
>
> with an ordered map, and
>
> $$
> O(n)
> $$
>
> expected with hashing.
>
> ---
>
> ## 7. Reference implementation idea
>
> ```cpp
> long long countPairs(const vector<long long>& v) {
>     int n = (int)v.size() - 1; // assume 1-based
>     vector<long long> P(n + 1, 0);
>     for (int i = 1; i <= n; ++i) P[i] = P[i - 1] + v[i];
>
>     map<pair<long long,long long>, long long> cnt;
>     long long ans = 0;
>
>     for (int i = n; i >= 1; --i) {
>         long long x = 2 * P[i] - P[i - 1];
>         long long y = 3 * P[i] - 2 * P[i - 1];
>         ans += cnt[{x, y}];
>         cnt[{P[i - 1], P[i]}]++;
>     }
>     return ans;
> }
> ```
>
> ---
>
> ## Final answer
>
> Count pairs by prefix sums. A pair $(i,j)$ is valid iff
>
> $$
> (P[j-1],P[j]) = (2P[i]-P[i-1],\, 3P[i]-2P[i-1]).
> $$
>
> Process indices from right to left, maintaining counts of all pairs $(P[j-1],P[j])$ for indices $j$ already seen. For each $i$, add the count of
>
> $$
> (2P[i]-P[i-1],\, 3P[i]-2P[i-1])
> $$
>
> to the answer, then insert
>
> $$
> (P[i-1],P[i]).
> $$
>
> This yields an $O(n \log n)$ algorithm.
