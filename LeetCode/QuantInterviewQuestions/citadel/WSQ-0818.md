> [!example] Question 818 · Citadel
> ## Trading Interview

Explain why the volatility smile exists.

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=819)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> The volatility smile exists because the market prices of options are not fully consistent with the assumptions of the Black–Scholes model.
>
> ## 1. What Black–Scholes predicts
>
> In the Black–Scholes framework, the underlying asset price is assumed to follow a lognormal diffusion with constant volatility $\sigma$. Under these assumptions, every option written on the same underlying with the same maturity should be priced using the same volatility. Therefore, if one computes the implied volatility from market prices across strikes, the graph of implied volatility against strike should be flat.
>
> In reality, it is not flat. Instead, implied volatility varies with strike, often producing a curved pattern called a volatility smile or, in equity markets, more commonly a skew or smirk.
>
> ## 2. Why market implied volatilities differ by strike
>
> The smile reflects the fact that the true distribution of future asset prices differs from the lognormal distribution assumed by Black–Scholes.
>
> ### (a) Fat tails
>
> Market participants believe extreme moves occur more often than the lognormal model predicts. This means out-of-the-money options, especially those paying off in rare events, are worth more than Black–Scholes with constant volatility would imply. To match these higher prices, one must use a higher implied volatility for those strikes.
>
> ### (b) Skewness of returns
>
> Asset returns are often not symmetric.
>
> - In equity markets, large downward moves are more feared than large upward moves.
> - As a result, out-of-the-money puts tend to be especially expensive.
> - This produces higher implied volatilities for low strikes than for high strikes, giving a downward-sloping skew rather than a symmetric smile.
>
> In markets such as foreign exchange, where upside and downside tail risks may be more balanced, the curve can look more like a symmetric smile.
>
> ### (c) Stochastic volatility
>
> Actual volatility changes over time. If volatility is random rather than constant, then options with different strikes respond differently to possible future volatility paths. A single constant $\sigma$ cannot price all strikes consistently, so implied volatility must vary across strikes.
>
> ### (d) Jumps in asset prices
>
> Asset prices sometimes move discontinuously because of earnings announcements, macroeconomic news, defaults, or crises. Jump risk especially affects deep out-of-the-money options, increasing their value relative to the Black–Scholes benchmark and contributing to the smile.
>
> ### (e) Supply and demand for tail protection
>
> Option prices are not determined solely by statistical expectations; they also reflect risk premia and hedging demand. For example:
>
> - institutional investors often demand downside protection via puts,
> - dealers who sell these options require compensation for bearing crash risk.
>
> This extra demand raises put prices and thus their implied volatilities.
>
> ## 3. Economic interpretation
>
> Implied volatility is not just a forecast of future realized volatility. It is the parameter that makes the Black–Scholes formula reproduce the market price. Since market prices incorporate non-normal risks, stochastic volatility, jumps, and risk premia, the implied volatility must vary by strike and maturity. The smile is therefore the market’s way of encoding a more realistic risk-neutral distribution.
>
> ## 4. Summary
>
> The volatility smile exists because:
>
> 1. the true return distribution has fatter tails and often skewness,
> 2. volatility is not constant,
> 3. asset prices may jump,
> 4. investors pay a premium for protection against extreme outcomes.
>
> Hence, options at different strikes embed different risks, and a single Black–Scholes volatility cannot price them all. The variation of implied volatility across strikes is observed as the volatility smile.
