> [!example] Question 696 · Citadel
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

What is an O(n log n) sorting algorithm? Give an example and briefly explain how it works.

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=697)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> An $n \log n$ sorting algorithm is a sorting algorithm whose running time grows on the order of
>
> $$
> O(n \log n)
> $$
>
> for an input of size $n$.
>
> ## Meaning of $n \log n$
>
> - $n$ is the number of items to sort.
> - $\log n$ typically comes from repeatedly dividing the problem into smaller parts.
>
> Thus, an $O(n \log n)$ algorithm is much faster than an $O(n^2)$ algorithm for large $n$.
>
> ## Common examples
>
> Typical $n \log n$ sorting algorithms include:
>
> - **Merge sort**: always runs in
>   $$
>   O(n \log n)
>   $$
> - **Heap sort**: runs in
>   $$
>   O(n \log n)
>   $$
> - **Quicksort**: average-case running time is
>   $$
>   O(n \log n),
>   $$
>   though its worst case is
>   $$
>   O(n^2)
>   $$
>
> ## Why $n \log n$ appears
>
> In divide-and-conquer sorting methods, the array is split into parts repeatedly. If there are about
>
> $$
> \log n
> $$
>
> levels of splitting, and each level does about
>
> $$
> O(n)
> $$
>
> work, then the total is
>
> $$
> O(n \log n).
> $$
>
> For example, merge sort satisfies:
>
> $$
> T(n) = 2T\left(\frac{n}{2}\right) + O(n),
> $$
>
> which solves to
>
> $$
> T(n) = O(n \log n).
> $$
>
> ## Importance
>
> For comparison-based sorting, the best possible worst-case time is
>
> $$
> \Omega(n \log n),
> $$
>
> so algorithms with running time
>
> $$
> O(n \log n)
> $$
>
> are asymptotically optimal in that model.
>
> ## Final answer
>
> An $n \log n$ sorting algorithm is a sorting method whose time complexity is proportional to
>
> $$
> n \log n,
> $$
>
> such as merge sort or heap sort. These algorithms are considered efficient and, for comparison-based sorting, asymptotically optimal.
