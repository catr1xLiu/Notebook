> [!example] Question 749 · Citadel
> ## Trader Interview

Suppose I have two pancakes: one with both sides burnt, and one with one burnt side and one unburnt side. I randomly choose one pancake, then randomly look at one of its sides. If the side is burnt, what is the probability that I picked the pancake with both sides burnt?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=750)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let the two pancakes be:
>
> - $P_{BB}$: one pancake with two burnt sides,
> - $P_{BU}$: one pancake with one burnt side and one unburnt side.
>
> A pancake is chosen uniformly at random, so initially
>
> $$
> P(P_{BB}) = P(P_{BU}) = \frac{1}{2}.
> $$
>
> Then one side of the chosen pancake is viewed uniformly at random.
>
> We are told that the observed side is burnt. We want
>
> $$
> P(P_{BB}\mid \text{burnt}).
> $$
>
> Use conditional probability. First compute the probability of seeing a burnt side from each pancake:
>
> - If $P_{BB}$ was chosen, then the observed side is burnt with probability $1$.
> - If $P_{BU}$ was chosen, then the observed side is burnt with probability $\frac{1}{2}$.
>
> Thus
>
> $$
> P(\text{burnt})
> = P(\text{burnt}\mid P_{BB})P(P_{BB}) + P(\text{burnt}\mid P_{BU})P(P_{BU})
> = 1\cdot \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\cdot \frac{1}{2}
> = \frac{3}{4}.
> $$
>
> Now apply Bayes' theorem:
>
> $$
> P(P_{BB}\mid \text{burnt})
> = \frac{P(\text{burnt}\mid P_{BB})P(P_{BB})}{P(\text{burnt})}
> = \frac{1\cdot \frac{1}{2}}{\frac{3}{4}}
> = \frac{2}{3}.
> $$
>
> Therefore, the probability that the chosen pancake was the one with two burnt sides is
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{2}{3}}.
> $$
>
> An equivalent counting argument gives the same result: among all possible visible sides, there are $3$ burnt sides total, and $2$ of them belong to the two-burnt-sides pancake, so the probability is $\frac{2}{3}$.
