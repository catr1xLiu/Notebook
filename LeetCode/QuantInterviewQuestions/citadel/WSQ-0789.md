> [!example] Question 789 · Citadel
> ## Quantitative Research Analyst Interview

How can you generate a probability of 1/4, 1/3, or 1/π using only coin flips?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=790)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> A convenient way to think about these questions is to design a procedure that uses independent fair coin flips and outputs “success” with the desired probability.
>
> ## Probability $\tfrac14$
>
> Flip the coin twice. Declare success only if both flips are heads.
>
> Since the flips are independent and each has probability $\tfrac12$ of being heads,
>
> $$
> P(\text{success}) = \frac12 \cdot \frac12 = \frac14.
> $$
>
> So two flips, requiring $HH$, generate probability $\tfrac14$.
>
> ---
>
> ## Probability $\tfrac13$
>
> Use the following rejection procedure.
>
> 1. Flip the coin twice.
> 2. If the outcome is $HH$, declare success.
> 3. If the outcome is $TT$, declare failure.
> 4. If the outcome is $HT$ or $TH$, ignore the result and start over.
>
> Each round has four equally likely outcomes:
>
> $$
> HH,\ HT,\ TH,\ TT.
> $$
>
> The procedure stops only on $HH$ or $TT$. Conditional on stopping in a given round, these two outcomes are equally likely, so one might first think this gives probability $\tfrac12$; but that would be for success on $HH$ and failure on $TT$ only. To obtain $\tfrac13$, use a three-way construction instead:
>
> A standard method is to generate a uniform choice among $\{1,2,3\}$ and declare success on exactly one of the three outcomes.
>
> Do this by flipping twice:
>
> - $HH \mapsto 1$
> - $HT \mapsto 2$
> - $TH \mapsto 3$
> - $TT \mapsto$ reject and repeat
>
> Now each accepted label $1,2,3$ occurs with equal probability. Indeed, in any given round, each of $HH,HT,TH$ has probability $\tfrac14$, and $TT$ causes a restart. Thus, conditional on acceptance,
>
> $$
> P(1)=P(2)=P(3)=\frac13.
> $$
>
> Therefore, if success is defined as obtaining label $1$, then
>
> $$
> P(\text{success})=\frac13.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Probability $\tfrac1\pi$
>
> Here one needs an infinite-flip procedure. A general fact is that any computable real number in $[0,1]$ can be generated exactly by a suitable coin-flip algorithm, and $\tfrac1\pi$ is computable.
>
> One clean construction is based on the binary expansion of $\tfrac1\pi$.
>
> Write
>
> $$
> \frac1\pi = 0.b_1 b_2 b_3 \cdots \quad \text{in base }2,
> $$
>
> where each $b_n \in \{0,1\}$.
>
> Now use coin flips to generate a random binary number
>
> $$
> U = 0.X_1 X_2 X_3 \cdots,
> $$
>
> where the $X_n$ are independent fair bits produced by the coin:
> heads $=1$, tails $=0$. Then $U$ is uniformly distributed on $[0,1]$.
>
> Compare the bits of $U$ to those of $\tfrac1\pi$ from left to right:
>
> 1. Flip coins to obtain $X_1, X_2, \dots$
> 2. Compute the corresponding bits $b_1, b_2, \dots$ of $\tfrac1\pi$
> 3. Stop at the first position $n$ where $X_n \neq b_n$
> 4. Declare success if $X_n < b_n$, and failure if $X_n > b_n$
>
> This works because comparing binary expansions bit by bit determines whether
>
> $$
> U < \frac1\pi \quad \text{or} \quad U > \frac1\pi.
> $$
>
> Since $U$ is uniform on $[0,1]$,
>
> $$
> P(\text{success}) = P\!\left(U < \frac1\pi\right) = \frac1\pi.
> $$
>
> The event that $U$ is exactly equal to $\tfrac1\pi$ has probability $0$, so the procedure halts with probability $1$.
>
> ---
>
> ## Final answer
>
> - To generate probability $\tfrac14$: flip twice and succeed on $HH$.
> - To generate probability $\tfrac13$: use
>   $$
>   HH \mapsto 1,\quad HT \mapsto 2,\quad TH \mapsto 3,\quad TT \mapsto \text{repeat},
>   $$
>   and succeed only on one chosen label.
> - To generate probability $\tfrac1\pi$: generate a uniform random binary number $U \in [0,1]$ from coin flips and accept iff
>   $$
>   U < \frac1\pi.
>   $$
>
> These procedures use only fair coin flips and produce the required probabilities exactly.
