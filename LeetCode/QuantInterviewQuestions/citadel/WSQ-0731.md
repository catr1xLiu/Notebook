> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

What is the difference between Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB) and logistic regression? Under what circumstances would you choose one over the other?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=732)

> ## Difference between Gaussian Naive Bayes and Logistic Regression
>
> Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB) and Logistic Regression are both commonly used for classification, but they are based on very different modeling assumptions.
>
> ### 1. Type of model
>
> - **Gaussian Naive Bayes** is a **generative** model.
>   - It models the class-conditional distribution $p(x \mid y)$ and the class prior $p(y)$.
>   - Classification is then done using Bayes’ rule:
>   $$
>   p(y \mid x) \propto p(x \mid y)p(y).
>   $$
>
> - **Logistic Regression** is a **discriminative** model.
>   - It directly models the posterior probability $p(y \mid x)$.
>   - For binary classification, it assumes
>   $$
>   p(y=1 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-(w^\top x + b)}}.
>   $$
>
> Thus, GNB tries to model how the data is generated, while logistic regression focuses only on the decision boundary.
>
> ---
>
> ### 2. Assumptions
>
> - **GNB** assumes:
>   1. Features are conditionally independent given the class.
>   2. Each feature follows a Gaussian distribution within each class:
>      $$
>      x_j \mid y=k \sim N(\mu_{jk}, \sigma_{jk}^2).
>      $$
>
> - **Logistic Regression** assumes:
>   1. The log-odds is linear in the features:
>      $$
>      \log \frac{p(y=1 \mid x)}{p(y=0 \mid x)} = w^\top x + b.
>      $$
>   2. It does **not** require feature independence or Gaussian features.
>
> The independence assumption in GNB is often quite strong and may be unrealistic when features are correlated.
>
> ---
>
> ### 3. Decision boundary
>
> - **Logistic Regression** produces a linear decision boundary in feature space.
> - **GNB** can produce linear or nonlinear boundaries depending on the variance assumptions:
>   - If all classes share the same variances, the boundary can be linear.
>   - If variances differ by class, the boundary is generally quadratic or more complex.
>
> ---
>
> ### 4. Parameter estimation
>
> - **GNB** estimates parameters in closed form:
>   - Class priors,
>   - Means,
>   - Variances.
>   
>   This makes training very fast, even on small datasets.
>
> - **Logistic Regression** typically requires iterative optimization, such as gradient descent or quasi-Newton methods, to minimize the log-loss.
>
> Thus, GNB is usually computationally simpler to fit.
>
> ---
>
> ### 5. Data requirements
>
> - **GNB** often works well with **small datasets**, because it makes strong assumptions that reduce variance.
> - **Logistic Regression** usually performs better when there is **enough data**, since it is more flexible and makes fewer distributional assumptions.
>
> A common rule of thumb is:
>
> - With **little data**, GNB may perform surprisingly well.
> - With **more data**, logistic regression often overtakes GNB in predictive accuracy.
>
> ---
>
> ### 6. Probability calibration
>
> - **Logistic Regression** generally gives better calibrated probabilities.
> - **GNB** tends to output overly confident probabilities, especially when the independence assumption is violated.
>
> So if reliable probability estimates matter, logistic regression is often preferable.
>
> ---
>
> ## When to choose each one
>
> ### Choose Gaussian Naive Bayes when:
>
> - The dataset is small.
> - Training speed must be extremely fast.
> - Features are approximately conditionally independent.
> - Feature distributions within each class are roughly Gaussian.
> - You want a simple baseline model.
>
> Typical situations:
> - Quick prototyping,
> - Small medical or sensor datasets,
> - Cases where interpretability of Gaussian class statistics is useful.
>
> ---
>
> ### Choose Logistic Regression when:
>
> - You have moderate or large amounts of data.
> - Features are correlated.
> - You want fewer unrealistic distributional assumptions.
> - You need better calibrated class probabilities.
> - A linear decision boundary is appropriate.
> - Regularization is important, for example with high-dimensional data.
>
> Typical situations:
> - General tabular classification,
> - Credit scoring,
> - Risk prediction,
> - Binary classification with interpretable feature coefficients.
>
> ---
>
> ## Practical comparison
>
> If the true data-generating process really satisfies the Naive Bayes assumptions, then GNB can perform very well. But in many real datasets, features are correlated and not Gaussian, and then logistic regression is often more robust.
>
> In summary:
>
> - **GNB**: stronger assumptions, faster training, often better with very small data.
> - **Logistic Regression**: fewer assumptions, usually better accuracy and probability estimates when enough data is available.
>
> ## Final recommendation
>
> - Use **Gaussian Naive Bayes** as a fast baseline or when data is scarce and its assumptions are roughly reasonable.
> - Use **Logistic Regression** when you have enough data, correlated features, or need more reliable probabilities and a generally stronger tabular-data classifier.
