> [!example] Question 1165 · SIG
> ## Quantitative Trader Interview

Flip coins sequentially until the sequence 'HT' or 'TT' appears. What is the probability that 'HT' appears before 'TT'?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=1166)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let $p$ be the probability that the pattern $HT$ occurs before $TT$.
>
> To determine $p$, condition on the result of the first flip.
>
> - If the first flip is $T$, then the next time a second flip occurs, the process ends with either $TH$, $TT$, etc. In particular, if the second flip is $T$, then $TT$ occurs immediately, so this path does not favor $HT$. More simply, once the first flip is $T$, the next $T$ before an $H$ gives $TT$, and after an $H$ the process is effectively restarted from a state ending in $H$. It is cleaner to use states.
>
> Define:
>
> - $p$ = probability of eventually getting $HT$ before $TT$ starting fresh.
> - $p_H$ = probability of eventually getting $HT$ before $TT$ given that the most recent flip is $H$.
> - $p_T$ = probability of eventually getting $HT$ before $TT$ given that the most recent flip is $T$.
>
> Now write equations from the next flip:
>
> 1. Starting fresh,
> $$
> p = \frac{1}{2}p_H + \frac{1}{2}p_T.
> $$
>
> 2. From state $H$:
> - flip $T$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$, and then $HT$ occurs immediately;
> - flip $H$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$, and we remain in state $H$.
>
> Thus
> $$
> p_H = \frac{1}{2}\cdot 1 + \frac{1}{2}p_H.
> $$
> So
> $$
> p_H = 1.
> $$
>
> 3. From state $T$:
> - flip $T$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$, and then $TT$ occurs immediately, so success probability is $0$;
> - flip $H$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$, moving to state $H$.
>
> Thus
> $$
> p_T = \frac{1}{2}\cdot 0 + \frac{1}{2}p_H = \frac{1}{2}.
> $$
>
> Substitute into the equation for $p$:
> $$
> p = \frac{1}{2}(1) + \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)
> = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4}
> = \frac{3}{4}.
> $$
>
> Therefore, the probability of getting $HT$ before $TT$ is
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{3}{4}}.
> $$
