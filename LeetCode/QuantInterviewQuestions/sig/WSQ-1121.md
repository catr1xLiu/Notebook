> [!example] Question 1121 · SIG
> ## Assistant Trader Interview

You have 17 coins and I have 16 coins. We flip all the coins at the same time. If you have more heads than I do, you win; if we have the same number of heads or you have fewer, then I win. What is your probability of winning?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=1122)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let $X$ be the number of heads obtained with $17$ fair coins, and let $Y$ be the number of heads obtained with $16$ fair coins. Then
>
> $$
> X \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(17,\tfrac12), \qquad Y \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(16,\tfrac12),
> $$
>
> independently. We want
>
> $$
> P(X>Y).
> $$
>
> A direct summation is possible, but there is a simpler symmetry argument.
>
> Consider separating the $17$ coins into:
>
> - $16$ “matched” coins, paired against the other person's $16$ coins, and
> - $1$ extra coin.
>
> Let $A$ be the number of heads among the matched $16$ coins, and let $B$ be the number of heads among the other person's $16$ coins. Then $A$ and $B$ have the same distribution, so by symmetry,
>
> $$
> P(A>B)=P(B>A).
> $$
>
> Also,
>
> $$
> P(A=B)+P(A>B)+P(B>A)=1,
> $$
>
> hence
>
> $$
> P(A>B)=\frac{1-P(A=B)}{2}.
> $$
>
> Now let $E$ be the result of the extra coin, where $E=1$ if it is heads and $E=0$ if it is tails. Then
>
> $$
> X=A+E, \qquad Y=B.
> $$
>
> Thus $X>Y$ occurs in two disjoint cases:
>
> 1. $A>B$, regardless of the extra coin;
> 2. $A=B$ and the extra coin is heads.
>
> Therefore,
>
> $$
> P(X>Y)=P(A>B)+P(A=B)\,P(E=1).
> $$
>
> Since $P(E=1)=\tfrac12$,
>
> $$
> P(X>Y)=\frac{1-P(A=B)}{2}+\frac{P(A=B)}{2}=\frac12.
> $$
>
> So the probability of winning is
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac12}.
> $$
