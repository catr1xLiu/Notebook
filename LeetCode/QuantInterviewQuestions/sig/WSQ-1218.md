> [!example] Question 1218 · SIG
> ## Graduate Quant Trader Interview

You play a game where you have \$100 to split between yourself and a random person. You decide how to split it, and the other person can either accept (so you both receive the split amounts) or refuse (in which case both of you get \$0 and the game ends). What split do you propose? What if the amount to split is \$1,000,000?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=1219)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> This is the **ultimatum game**. One player proposes a division of a fixed amount, and the other player either accepts or rejects. If the offer is rejected, both receive $0$.
>
> ### Step 1: Analyze the responder’s incentives
>
> If the responder is purely self-interested, then any offer strictly greater than $0$ should be accepted, because
>
> $$
> \text{something} > 0.
> $$
>
> So under the standard economic assumption of perfect rationality, the proposer should offer the smallest positive amount possible and keep the rest.
>
> - For a total of $100$, the proposer would offer the minimum positive amount, such as $1$, and keep $99$.
> - For a total of $1{,}000{,}000$, the proposer would again offer the minimum positive amount, such as $1$, and keep $999{,}999$.
>
> ### Step 2: Account for actual human behavior
>
> In practice, people often reject offers they perceive as unfair, even when rejection leaves them with nothing. Thus the proposer must balance:
>
> - keeping as much as possible, and
> - making an offer likely to be accepted.
>
> Experimental evidence from ultimatum games typically shows:
>
> - offers near $50\%$ are common,
> - offers below about $20\%$ are often rejected,
> - “fair” splits are much more likely to be accepted.
>
> ### Step 3: Reasonable proposals
>
> For a $100$ split, a practical offer is often around
>
> $$
> \$40\text{ to }\$50
> $$
>
> for the responder, with a $50$–$50$ split being the safest and most defensible.
>
> For a $1{,}000{,}000$ split, the same strategic logic applies, but the absolute size matters psychologically. Even a small percentage of $1{,}000{,}000$ is a large amount, so some responders may accept less than they would in the $100$ case. Still, fairness concerns remain. A proposer who wants a high probability of acceptance would likely offer a substantial amount, for example:
>
> $$
> \$400{,}000 \text{ to } \$500{,}000.
> $$
>
> ### Conclusion
>
> - **If assuming perfectly rational, self-interested behavior:**  
>   offer the smallest positive amount in both cases:
>   - $\text{\textdollar}1$ out of $100$,
>   - $\text{\textdollar}1$ out of $1{,}000{,}000$.
>
> - **If aiming for what is most likely to be accepted by a real person:**  
>   propose a roughly fair split, especially:
>   - about $\text{\textdollar}50$ out of $100$,
>   - about $\text{\textdollar}500{,}000$ out of $1{,}000{,}000$.
>
> Thus, the theoretical answer is “offer the minimum,” while the practical answer is “offer something close to fair.”
