> [!example] Question 1107 · SIG
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

If you randomly cut a cake n times, what is the expected number of pieces?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=1108)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let the cake be a planar disk, and suppose each cut is a straight chord drawn at random, say with independently and uniformly chosen endpoints on the boundary. This is different from asking for the maximum possible number of pieces: with random cuts, two cuts may or may not actually cross inside the cake, and we need the probability that a given pair of cuts intersects.
>
> ## Step 1: Effect of the $k$-th cut
>
> Before the $k$-th cut, there are $k-1$ previous cuts already made. A new straight cut increases the number of pieces by exactly $1$ plus the number of existing cuts it crosses inside the cake, since each interior crossing splits one additional segment of the new cut, and each such segment divides one existing piece into two.
>
> So if the $k$-th cut crosses $C_k$ of the previous $k-1$ cuts, it increases the piece count by $1+C_k$.
>
> ## Step 2: Probability that two random chords cross
>
> For two chords with independently and uniformly random endpoints on a circle, label the $4$ endpoints in order around the circle. The two chords cross if and only if their endpoints alternate around the circle. Among the $3$ ways to pair up $4$ labeled points into two chords, exactly $1$ pairing is crossing. Hence
>
> $$
> \Pr(\text{two random chords cross}) = \frac{1}{3}.
> $$
>
> ## Step 3: Expected number of pieces
>
> Let $P_n$ be the number of pieces after $n$ random cuts. Starting from $P_0=1$, each new cut adds $1$ (for the cut itself) plus $1$ for every earlier cut it crosses, and each of the $\binom{n}{2}$ pairs of cuts crosses independently with probability $\tfrac13$. Taking expectations,
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[P_n] = 1 + n + \binom{n}{2}\Pr(\text{cross}) = 1 + n + \binom{n}{2}\cdot\frac13.
> $$
>
> Since $\binom{n}{2}=\dfrac{n(n-1)}{2}$,
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[P_n] = 1 + n + \frac{n(n-1)}{6}.
> $$
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> Therefore, the expected number of pieces after $n$ random straight cuts is
>
> $$
> \boxed{1 + n + \frac{n(n-1)}{6}}.
> $$
>
> Two related benchmarks are worth noting. If the cuts were placed in general position so that every pair were guaranteed to cross (the "lazy caterer" setup), the count would be deterministic and maximal:
>
> $$
> 1+\frac{n(n+1)}{2}.
> $$
>
> Random cuts fall short of this maximum because a positive fraction of cut pairs fail to intersect inside the cake. There is also a three-dimensional analogue of this problem, the "cake numbers," which count the maximum number of pieces obtainable by $n$ planar cuts through a solid cake rather than a flat one.
