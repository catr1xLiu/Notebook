> [!example] Question 1154 · SIG
> ## Intern Interview

A frog is traveling from point A(0, 0) to point B(4, 6), and each step can only be 1 unit up or 1 unit to the right. The frog also refuses to move three steps in the same direction consecutively. Compute the number of ways the frog can move from A to B.

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=1155)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> To go from $A(0,0)$ to $B(4,6)$, the frog must make
>
> - $4$ steps to the right, denoted by $R$,
> - $6$ steps up, denoted by $U$.
>
> Thus the problem is to count sequences of length $10$ consisting of $4$ $R$'s and $6$ $U$'s, with the restriction that neither $RRR$ nor $UUU$ may occur.
>
> ## Step 1: Translate the restriction into block lengths
>
> Since no direction may occur $3$ times consecutively, each maximal block of consecutive $R$'s or $U$'s has length either $1$ or $2$.
>
> So:
>
> - the $4$ right-steps must be split into blocks of sizes $1$ and $2$,
> - the $6$ up-steps must be split into blocks of sizes $1$ and $2$.
>
> Also, these blocks must alternate between $R$-blocks and $U$-blocks.
>
> ## Step 2: Count possible block decompositions
>
> ### Right-steps
>
> Write $4$ as a sum of $1$'s and $2$'s:
>
> $$
> 4=2+2,\qquad 4=2+1+1,\qquad 4=1+2+1,\qquad 4=1+1+2,\qquad 4=1+1+1+1.
> $$
>
> Hence the number of possible $R$-block patterns is $5$.
>
> Equivalently, if there are $k_R$ blocks of $R$, then the number of ways is the number of compositions of $4$ into $k_R$ parts each equal to $1$ or $2$.
>
> Possible values are:
>
> - $k_R=2$: only $(2,2)$, so $1$ way;
> - $k_R=3$: one $2$ and two $1$'s, so $3$ ways;
> - $k_R=4$: all $1$'s, so $1$ way.
>
> ### Up-steps
>
> Write $6$ as a sum of $1$'s and $2$'s:
>
> Possible numbers $k_U$ of $U$-blocks satisfy $3 \le k_U \le 6$.
>
> For each $k_U$, let $t$ be the number of $2$'s. Then
>
> $$
> 2t + (k_U-t) = 6 \quad \Rightarrow \quad t = 6-k_U.
> $$
>
> So the number of compositions is
>
> $$
> \binom{k_U}{6-k_U}.
> $$
>
> Thus:
>
> - $k_U=3$: $\binom{3}{3}=1$ way;
> - $k_U=4$: $\binom{4}{2}=6$ ways;
> - $k_U=5$: $\binom{5}{1}=5$ ways;
> - $k_U=6$: $\binom{6}{0}=1$ way.
>
> ## Step 3: Match alternating block structures
>
> Since blocks alternate, the numbers of $R$-blocks and $U$-blocks can differ by at most $1$.
>
> Thus we need pairs $(k_R,k_U)$ with
>
> $$
> |k_R-k_U|\le 1.
> $$
>
> Using the possible values:
>
> - $k_R \in \{2,3,4\}$,
> - $k_U \in \{3,4,5,6\}$,
>
> the valid pairs are
>
> $$
> (k_R,k_U)=(2,3),(3,3),(3,4),(4,3),(4,4),(4,5).
> $$
>
> For each such pair:
>
> - if $k_R=k_U$, there are $2$ possible starting directions;
> - if $|k_R-k_U|=1$, there is only $1$ possible starting direction, namely the direction with more blocks.
>
> Now count case by case.
>
> ### Case 1: $(k_R,k_U)=(2,3)$
>
> - $R$-patterns: $1$,
> - $U$-patterns: $1$,
> - starting direction forced to be $U$.
>
> Contribution:
>
> $$
> 1\cdot 1\cdot 1 = 1.
> $$
>
> ### Case 2: $(k_R,k_U)=(3,3)$
>
> - $R$-patterns: $3$,
> - $U$-patterns: $1$,
> - $2$ starting choices.
>
> Contribution:
>
> $$
> 3\cdot 1\cdot 2 = 6.
> $$
>
> ### Case 3: $(k_R,k_U)=(3,4)$
>
> - $R$-patterns: $3$,
> - $U$-patterns: $6$,
> - starting direction forced to be $U$.
>
> Contribution:
>
> $$
> 3\cdot 6\cdot 1 = 18.
> $$
>
> ### Case 4: $(k_R,k_U)=(4,3)$
>
> - $R$-patterns: $1$,
> - $U$-patterns: $1$,
> - starting direction forced to be $R$.
>
> Contribution:
>
> $$
> 1\cdot 1\cdot 1 = 1.
> $$
>
> ### Case 5: $(k_R,k_U)=(4,4)$
>
> - $R$-patterns: $1$,
> - $U$-patterns: $6$,
> - $2$ starting choices.
>
> Contribution:
>
> $$
> 1\cdot 6\cdot 2 = 12.
> $$
>
> ### Case 6: $(k_R,k_U)=(4,5)$
>
> - $R$-patterns: $1$,
> - $U$-patterns: $5$,
> - starting direction forced to be $U$.
>
> Contribution:
>
> $$
> 1\cdot 5\cdot 1 = 5.
> $$
>
> ## Step 4: Add all contributions
>
> $$
> 1+6+18+1+12+5=43.
> $$
>
> Therefore, the number of valid paths is
>
> $$
> \boxed{43}.
> $$
