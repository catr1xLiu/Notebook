> [!example] Question 1116 · SIG
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

Given an n-by-n real matrix A where every entry is nonnegative and A squared (A^2) is the zero matrix, what is the maximum number of positive entries that A can have?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=1117)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let $A=(a_{ij})$ be an $n\times n$ real matrix with $a_{ij}\ge 0$ for all $i,j$, and suppose that
>
> $$
> A^2=0.
> $$
>
> We seek the maximum possible number of positive entries of $A$.
>
> ## Step 1: Translate the condition $A^2=0$
>
> For each $i,j$,
>
> $$
> (A^2)_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^n a_{ik}a_{kj}=0.
> $$
>
> Since all entries are nonnegative, every term in the sum is $\ge 0$. Therefore, for each triple $(i,k,j)$,
>
> $$
> a_{ik}a_{kj}=0.
> $$
>
> Thus there do not exist indices $i,k,j$ such that both $a_{ik}>0$ and $a_{kj}>0$.
>
> In graph-theoretic language: if we draw a directed edge $u\to v$ whenever $a_{uv}>0$, then there is no directed path of length $2$.
>
> ## Step 2: Row-support and column-support cannot overlap
>
> Define
>
> $$
> R=\{k:\text{row }k\text{ contains a positive entry}\},
> $$
>
> $$
> C=\{k:\text{column }k\text{ contains a positive entry}\}.
> $$
>
> We claim that
>
> $$
> R\cap C=\varnothing.
> $$
>
> Indeed, if some index $k$ lay in both $R$ and $C$, then there would exist $j$ with $a_{kj}>0$ and $i$ with $a_{ik}>0$. But then
>
> $$
> a_{ik}a_{kj}>0,
> $$
>
> contradicting the condition established above.
>
> Hence the index set $\{1,\dots,n\}$ is split into two disjoint parts:
>
> - indices in $C$, which may receive positive entries,
> - indices in $R$, which may send positive entries,
> - and possibly some indices in neither.
>
> Any positive entry $a_{ij}>0$ must have $i\in R$ and $j\in C$. Since $R$ and $C$ are disjoint, all positive entries lie in a rectangular block from rows in $R$ to columns in $C$.
>
> If $|R|=r$ and $|C|=c$, then
>
> $$
> r+c\le n,
> $$
>
> and the number of positive entries is at most
>
> $$
> rc.
> $$
>
> ## Step 3: Maximize $rc$ subject to $r+c\le n$
>
> For fixed $n$, the product $rc$ is maximized when $r+c=n$ and the two parts are as equal as possible. Thus
>
> $$
> rc\le \left\lfloor \frac{n^2}{4}\right\rfloor.
> $$
>
> Indeed, if $n=2m$, the maximum is $m^2$, and if $n=2m+1$, the maximum is $m(m+1)$.
>
> ## Step 4: Show the bound is attainable
>
> Partition the indices into two disjoint sets of sizes
>
> $$
> \left\lfloor \frac n2\right\rfloor
> \quad\text{and}\quad
> \left\lceil \frac n2\right\rceil.
> $$
>
> Construct $A$ so that all entries from the first set of rows to the second set of columns are positive, and all other entries are $0$. Then $A$ has exactly
>
> $$
> \left\lfloor \frac{n^2}{4}\right\rfloor
> $$
>
> positive entries.
>
> Moreover, $A^2=0$, because there is no index $k$ that is simultaneously the endpoint of a positive entry and the starting point of a positive entry; equivalently, there is no directed path of length $2$.
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> The maximum possible number of positive entries in $A$ is
>
> $$
> \boxed{\left\lfloor \frac{n^2}{4}\right\rfloor }.
> $$
