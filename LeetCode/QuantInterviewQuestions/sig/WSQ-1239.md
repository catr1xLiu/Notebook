> [!example] Question 1239 · SIG
> ## Quantitative Research Associate Interview

Let A and B be two covariance matrices. Is A + B also a covariance matrix? How about A squared (A^2)? What about the product AB?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=1240)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> A covariance matrix is, by definition, a symmetric positive semidefinite matrix. Thus the question reduces to checking, for each operation, whether the result is always symmetric and positive semidefinite.
>
> ## 1. The sum $A+B$
>
> Since $A$ and $B$ are covariance matrices, both are symmetric and positive semidefinite.
>
> - **Symmetry**:
>   $$
>   (A+B)^T = A^T + B^T = A + B.
>   $$
>
> - **Positive semidefiniteness**: for any vector $x$,
>   $$
>   x^T(A+B)x = x^TAx + x^TBx \ge 0 + 0 = 0.
>   $$
>
> Therefore $A+B$ is again symmetric positive semidefinite, hence a covariance matrix.
>
> ## 2. The square $A^2$
>
> Write
> $$
> A^2 = AA.
> $$
>
> Since $A$ is a covariance matrix, it is symmetric and positive semidefinite.
>
> - **Symmetry**:
>   $$
>   (A^2)^T = (AA)^T = A^TA^T = AA = A^2.
>   $$
>
> - **Positive semidefiniteness**: for any vector $x$,
>   $$
>   x^T A^2 x = x^T A A x = (Ax)^T (Ax) = \|Ax\|^2 \ge 0.
>   $$
>
> Hence $A^2$ is symmetric positive semidefinite, so it is also a covariance matrix.
>
> ## 3. The product $AB$
>
> In general, $AB$ need **not** be a covariance matrix.
>
> Even if $A$ and $B$ are both symmetric positive semidefinite, the product $AB$ need not be symmetric. Since every covariance matrix must be symmetric, this already causes failure in general.
>
> A simple counterexample is
> $$
> A=
> \begin{pmatrix}
> 1 & 1\\
> 1 & 1
> \end{pmatrix},
> \qquad
> B=
> \begin{pmatrix}
> 1 & 0\\
> 0 & 0
> \end{pmatrix}.
> $$
> Both $A$ and $B$ are symmetric positive semidefinite, hence covariance matrices. But
> $$
> AB=
> \begin{pmatrix}
> 1 & 0\\
> 1 & 0
> \end{pmatrix},
> $$
> which is not symmetric. Therefore $AB$ is not a covariance matrix.
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> - $A+B$: **always** a covariance matrix.
> - $A^2$: **always** a covariance matrix.
> - $AB$: **not necessarily** a covariance matrix.
