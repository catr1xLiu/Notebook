> [!example] Question 1160 · SIG
> ## Intern Interview

A painting has a 20% chance of being real and an 80% chance of being fake. If it's fake, you can sell it for \$10,000; if it's real, it's worth \$500,000.
1. What is the expected value of the painting?
2. If you could buy the option to purchase the painting for \$100,000 after learning whether it is real or fake (but are not obligated to buy), what is the value of this option?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=1161)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let $V$ denote the value of the painting.
>
> The painting is real with probability $0.2$ and fake with probability $0.8$. Thus,
>
> - if real, $V = 500{,}000$,
> - if fake, $V = 10{,}000$.
>
> ## 1. Expected value of the painting
>
> The painting’s worth is its expected value:
>
> $$
> E[V] = 0.2(500{,}000) + 0.8(10{,}000).
> $$
>
> Compute:
>
> $$
> E[V] = 100{,}000 + 8{,}000 = 108{,}000.
> $$
>
> So the painting is worth
>
> $$
> \boxed{108{,}000}.
> $$
>
> ## 2. Value of the option to buy for $100{,}000$
>
> This is an option to buy the painting for a strike price of $100{,}000$ after learning whether it is real or fake. Since there is no obligation to buy, the payoff is
>
> $$
> \max(V - 100{,}000,\, 0).
> $$
>
> Consider the two cases:
>
> - If the painting is real, the payoff is
>
> $$
> 500{,}000 - 100{,}000 = 400{,}000.
> $$
>
> - If the painting is fake, the payoff is
>
> $$
> \max(10{,}000 - 100{,}000,\, 0) = 0.
> $$
>
> Therefore the expected value of the option is
>
> $$
> 0.2(400{,}000) + 0.8(0) = 80{,}000.
> $$
>
> So the option is worth
>
> $$
> \boxed{80{,}000}.
> $$
