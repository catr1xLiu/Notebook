> [!example] Question 57 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Research Interview

Given a standard deck of 52 poker cards, consider the following three choices for creating a deck: (A) 26 black and 26 red cards, (B) 13 black and 13 red cards, or (C) a random selection of 26 cards from the full deck. For each deck, you draw the first two cards and win \$1 if they are the same color, otherwise you lose \$1. Which deck provides the best odds for winning, and why? How would you simulate this scenario? Additionally, how would you select a random set of 26 cards from the deck?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=58)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let $X$ be the payoff from one play:
> - $X = +1$ if the first two cards are the same color,
> - $X = -1$ otherwise.
>
> Thus
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[X] = 1 \cdot \Pr(\text{same color}) + (-1)\cdot \Pr(\text{different color})
> = 2\Pr(\text{same color}) - 1.
> $$
> So the best deck is the one that maximizes $\Pr(\text{same color})$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. Deck $A$: full deck, $26$ black and $26$ red
>
> The probability that the first two cards have the same color is
> $$
> \Pr(\text{same}) = \Pr(BB) + \Pr(RR).
> $$
> Compute:
> $$
> \Pr(BB) = \frac{26}{52}\cdot\frac{25}{51}, \qquad
> \Pr(RR) = \frac{26}{52}\cdot\frac{25}{51}.
> $$
> Hence
> $$
> \Pr(\text{same}) = 2\cdot \frac{26}{52}\cdot\frac{25}{51}
> = \frac{25}{51}.
> $$
> Therefore
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[X_A] = 2\cdot \frac{25}{51} - 1
> = -\frac{1}{51}.
> $$
>
> So deck $A$ is slightly unfavorable.
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Deck $B$: $13$ black and $13$ red
>
> Now there are $26$ cards total. Similarly,
> $$
> \Pr(\text{same}) = 2\cdot \frac{13}{26}\cdot\frac{12}{25}
> = \frac{12}{25}.
> $$
> Thus
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[X_B] = 2\cdot \frac{12}{25} - 1
> = -\frac{1}{25}.
> $$
>
> So deck $B$ is even worse than deck $A$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Deck $C$: a random $26$-card subset from the full deck
>
> Let $K$ be the number of black cards in the random $26$-card pile. Then the pile has $26-K$ red cards. Conditional on $K$,
> $$
> \Pr(\text{same}\mid K)
> = \frac{\binom{K}{2} + \binom{26-K}{2}}{\binom{26}{2}}.
> $$
> So
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[X_C \mid K]
> = 2\Pr(\text{same}\mid K) - 1.
> $$
>
> To compare with $B$, observe that
> $$
> \binom{K}{2} + \binom{26-K}{2}
> $$
> is minimized when the colors are as balanced as possible, namely at $K=13$. Therefore every imbalance increases the chance that two cards have the same color. Since a random $26$-card subset is not always exactly balanced, its average must be strictly better than deck $B$.
>
> To compute the exact value, use symmetry/exchangeability. A random $26$-card subset, when shuffled, is simply like taking the first $26$ cards of a shuffled full deck. Hence the first two cards from deck $C$ have the same joint color distribution as the first two cards from the full deck. Therefore
> $$
> \Pr_C(\text{same}) = \frac{25}{51},
> $$
> the same as for deck $A$.
>
> Thus
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[X_C] = 2\cdot \frac{25}{51} - 1 = -\frac{1}{51}.
> $$
>
> So deck $C$ is exactly as good as deck $A$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. Comparison
>
> We have
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[X_A] = -\frac{1}{51}, \qquad
> \mathbb{E}[X_B] = -\frac{1}{25}, \qquad
> \mathbb{E}[X_C] = -\frac{1}{51}.
> $$
> Since
> $$
> -\frac{1}{51} > -\frac{1}{25},
> $$
> the best choice is either $A$ or $C$, and they are tied.
>
> ### Final conclusion
> $$
> \boxed{\text{Choose } A \text{ or } C. \text{ They are equally best; } B \text{ is worse.}}
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 5. Why $C$ equals $A$
>
> A clean way to see this:
>
> - Shuffle the full $52$-card deck uniformly.
> - Let the first $26$ cards be deck $C$.
> - Then shuffle those $26$ cards internally and draw the first two.
>
> But this produces the same distribution for the two drawn cards as simply looking at the first two cards of the fully shuffled $52$-card deck. Therefore the probability of same color is unchanged:
> $$
> \Pr_C(\text{same}) = \Pr_A(\text{same}) = \frac{25}{51}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 6. How to simulate
>
> A simulation estimates the average payoff by repeating the experiment many times.
>
> ### Deck $A$
> - Create a list with $26$ black and $26$ red labels.
> - Shuffle.
> - Check whether the first two labels are equal.
> - Record $+1$ if equal, otherwise $-1$.
> - Repeat many times and average.
>
> ### Deck $B$
> - Create a list with $13$ black and $13$ red labels.
> - Proceed the same way.
>
> ### Deck $C$
> There are two equivalent methods.
>
> #### Method 1: sample a random $26$-card pile from the $52$-card deck
> - Start with a $52$-card deck labeled by color: $26$ black, $26$ red.
> - Shuffle the $52$ cards.
> - Take the first $26$ cards as the random pile.
> - Shuffle those $26$ cards if desired.
> - Check whether the first two cards in that pile have the same color.
> - Record payoff and repeat.
>
> #### Method 2: directly sample the number of black cards
> - Draw $K$ from the hypergeometric distribution:
> $$
> K \sim \mathrm{Hypergeometric}(N=52,\; M=26,\; n=26),
> $$
> where $K$ is the number of black cards in the random pile.
> - Then create a $26$-card deck with $K$ black and $26-K$ red.
> - Shuffle and test the first two cards.
>
> Method $1$ is simpler.
>
> ---
>
> ## 7. How to draw the random pile of $26$ cards
>
> A “random pile of $26$ cards from the deck” means a uniformly chosen $26$-card subset of the $52$ cards.
>
> The standard way is:
>
> 1. Shuffle the full $52$-card deck uniformly.
> 2. Take the top $26$ cards.
>
> This gives each $26$-card subset equal probability, so it is exactly a random $26$-card pile.
>
> ---
>
> ## 8. Example pseudocode
>
> ```python
> import random
>
> def play(deck):
>     random.shuffle(deck)
>     return 1 if deck[0] == deck[1] else -1
>
> def simulate_A(n_trials):
>     total = 0
>     for _ in range(n_trials):
>         deck = ['B'] * 26 + ['R'] * 26
>         total += play(deck)
>     return total / n_trials
>
> def simulate_B(n_trials):
>     total = 0
>     for _ in range(n_trials):
>         deck = ['B'] * 13 + ['R'] * 13
>         total += play(deck)
>     return total / n_trials
>
> def simulate_C(n_trials):
>     total = 0
>     for _ in range(n_trials):
>         full_deck = ['B'] * 26 + ['R'] * 26
>         random.shuffle(full_deck)
>         pile = full_deck[:26]      # random 26-card pile
>         random.shuffle(pile)       # optional
>         total += 1 if pile[0] == pile[1] else -1
>     return total / n_trials
> ```
>
> With many trials, the estimates should be close to
> $$
> -\frac{1}{51} \approx -0.01961,\qquad
> -\frac{1}{25} = -0.04,\qquad
> -\frac{1}{51} \approx -0.01961.
> $$
>
> So the simulation should confirm that $A$ and $C$ are tied and both better than $B$.
