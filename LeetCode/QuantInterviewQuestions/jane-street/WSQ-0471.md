> [!example] Question 471 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Trader Interview

Person A and B are going to play a coin toss game. There is an initial score of 0, and whenever a head or tail appears, the score increases by 1 or decreases by 1, respectively. The coin is tossed repeatedly until one wins, that is, when the score reaches +2 or -2, A or B wins the game. There is also an initial stake of \$1 for the game and person A has the option to double the stake before each coin toss. When one person wins the game, the other player needs to pay the current stake to the winner. The question is: if you are person A, what is your optimal strategy and what is your highest expected payoff in the game?

> ## Solution: coin-toss game with a doubling option
>
> There is a simple symmetric random walk starting at score $0$. Each toss changes the score by $+1$ for heads and $-1$ for tails. The game ends when the score reaches $+2$ or $-2$:
>
> - $A$ wins if the score reaches $+2$,
> - $B$ wins if the score reaches $-2$.
>
> Initially the stake is $1$ dollar. Before each toss, $A$ may choose to double the current stake. When the game ends, the loser pays the current stake to the winner. The problem asks for $A$'s optimal strategy and highest expected payoff.
>
> ## Step 1: Winning probabilities from each state
>
> Let $p_i$ be the probability that $A$ eventually wins starting from score $i$, where $i\in\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}$.
>
> Boundary conditions:
> $$
> p_{2}=1,\qquad p_{-2}=0.
> $$
> By symmetry of the fair coin,
> $$
> p_i=\frac{p_{i+1}+p_{i-1}}{2}\qquad (i=-1,0,1).
> $$
> Solving:
> $$
> p_1=\frac{p_2+p_0}{2}=\frac{1+p_0}{2},
> \qquad
> p_{-1}=\frac{p_0+p_{-2}}{2}=\frac{p_0}{2},
> $$
> and
> $$
> p_0=\frac{p_1+p_{-1}}{2}.
> $$
> Substitute:
> $$
> p_0=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1+p_0}{2}+\frac{p_0}{2}\right)
> =\frac{1+2p_0}{4}.
> $$
> Hence
> $$
> 4p_0=1+2p_0 \implies 2p_0=1 \implies p_0=\frac12.
> $$
> Then
> $$
> p_1=\frac{1+1/2}{2}=\frac34,\qquad p_{-1}=\frac14.
> $$
>
> So: from score $0$, $A$ wins with probability $\frac12$; from score $1$, with probability $\frac34$; from score $-1$, with probability $\frac14$.
>
> ## Step 2: When is doubling beneficial?
>
> Suppose that before some toss the current stake is $s$ and the current score is $i$. If $A$ does not double, the expected payoff contributed from this point onward is $s(2p_i-1)$; if $A$ doubles, it is $2s(2p_i-1)$. So doubling helps exactly when $2p_i-1>0$, i.e. $p_i>\frac12$.
>
> Using the values above:
>
> - at score $1$, $p_1=\frac34>\frac12$: doubling is favorable;
> - at score $0$, $p_0=\frac12$: doubling leaves the immediate expected value unchanged;
> - at score $-1$, $p_{-1}=\frac14<\frac12$: doubling is unfavorable.
>
> This points to the candidate rule: double whenever the score is $+1$, never double at $-1$, and treat score $0$ as neutral. But "neutral on a single toss" is not automatically "safe every time it recurs," since the walk can revisit $0$ and $1$ many times before the game ends, and doubling compounds on each visit. This has to be checked carefully.
>
> ## Step 3: Value of the "double only at +1" strategy
>
> Consider the strategy: double the stake every time the score is $+1$, just before the next toss; never double at score $0$ or $-1$. Because the payoff scales linearly with the current stake, let $v(i)$ be the expected payoff to $A$, per dollar of current stake, starting from score $i$ under this strategy.
>
> From score $1$ (stake $s$ doubles to $2s$, then a toss follows):
> $$
> v(1)\,s=\frac12(2s)+\frac12\bigl(2s\cdot v(0)\bigr)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad v(1)=1+v(0).
> $$
> From score $0$ (no doubling, stake $s$, then a toss follows):
> $$
> v(0)\,s=\frac12\bigl(s\cdot v(1)\bigr)+\frac12\bigl(s\cdot v(-1)\bigr)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad v(0)=\frac12v(1)+\frac12v(-1).
> $$
> From score $-1$ (no doubling, stake $s$, then a toss follows):
> $$
> v(-1)\,s=\frac12(-s)+\frac12\bigl(s\cdot v(0)\bigr)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad v(-1)=-\frac12+\frac12v(0).
> $$
>
> Substituting the first and third equations into the second:
> $$
> v(0)=\frac12\bigl(1+v(0)\bigr)+\frac12\left(-\frac12+\frac12v(0)\right)
> =\frac14+\frac34v(0).
> $$
> So
> $$
> \frac14v(0)=\frac14 \implies v(0)=1.
> $$
> Then $v(1)=1+v(0)=2$ and $v(-1)=-\frac12+\frac12=0$.
>
> Since the game starts at score $0$ with an initial stake of $1$ dollar, the expected payoff under this strategy is
> $$
> 1\cdot v(0)=1.
> $$
>
> This value is larger than a naive single-visit calculation would suggest, precisely because the strategy keeps paying off repeatedly: every time the walk happens to revisit $+1$ before the game ends, the stake doubles again, compounding on top of all previous doublings.
>
> ## Step 4: Why one should not also double at score 0
>
> Score $0$ looked "neutral" in Step 2, since $p_0=\frac12$ exactly makes a single toss from $0$ break even in expectation whether or not the stake is doubled first. But neutrality on one toss does not mean it is safe to double at $0$ every time it recurs: since $0$ is the starting state and is revisited repeatedly along many sample paths, doubling there compounds in the same self-reinforcing way as a St. Petersburg-type bet. Attempting the same value recursion with doubling also enabled at score $0$ fails to produce a finite, self-consistent value — the achievable expected payoff becomes unbounded, with no finite optimum, since one can always gain more in expectation by exploiting further doublings at $0$. So, to keep the optimization well posed, $A$ should double only at score $+1$, never at score $0$.
>
> ## Final answer
>
> The optimal strategy is:
>
> - do not double at score $0$,
> - do not double at score $-1$,
> - double every time the score is $+1$, on each visit, before the next toss.
>
> The resulting highest well-defined expected payoff is
> $$
> \boxed{1\text{ dollar}},
> $$
> that is, an expected return of exactly $100\%$ on the initial stake.
