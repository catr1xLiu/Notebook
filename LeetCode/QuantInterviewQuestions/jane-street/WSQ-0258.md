> [!example] Question 258 · Jane Street
> ## Trader Intern Interview

Two people each bid a number before rolling a 30-sided die. Whoever bids closer to the number the die shows wins, and wins an amount of money equal to the number rolled. For example, if I bid 15 and you bid 16, and the die lands on 10, then I win 10 from you. What is the optimal bidding strategy and the expected payoff?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=259)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> Let the two bids be integers $a,b \in \{1,2,\dots,30\}$, chosen before a fair $30$-sided die is rolled. Let the die outcome be $X \in \{1,2,\dots,30\}$, uniformly distributed.
>
> The player whose bid is closer to $X$ wins, and the winner receives $X$ dollars from the loser. Thus, if player $1$ bids $a$ and player $2$ bids $b$, then player $1$’s payoff is
>
> - $+X$ if $|X-a|<|X-b|$,
> - $-X$ if $|X-a|>|X-b|$,
> - and in case of a tie, the natural assumption is payoff $0$.
>
> The game is zero-sum, so we seek optimal play and the value of the game.
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. Comparing two bids
>
> Assume $a<b$. Then the comparison depends on the midpoint between $a$ and $b$.
>
> A number $X$ is closer to $a$ than to $b$ exactly when
> $$
> |X-a|<|X-b|.
> $$
> For $a<b$, this is equivalent to
> $$
> X<\frac{a+b}{2}.
> $$
> Similarly, $X$ is closer to $b$ when
> $$
> X>\frac{a+b}{2}.
> $$
> If $a+b$ is even, then at
> $$
> X=\frac{a+b}{2}
> $$
> there is a tie.
>
> So the expected payoff to the player choosing $a$ against $b$ is
> $$
> E(a,b)=\frac{1}{30}
> \left(
> \sum_{\substack{x=1\\ x< (a+b)/2}}^{30} x
> -
> \sum_{\substack{x=1\\ x> (a+b)/2}}^{30} x
> \right).
> $$
>
> The key point is that this payoff depends only on the midpoint $\frac{a+b}{2}$, not on the individual values of $a$ and $b$ separately.
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Which side of the midpoint is better?
>
> Let
> $$
> m=\frac{a+b}{2}.
> $$
> If $a<b$, then the lower bid wins when the die roll is below $m$, and the higher bid wins when the die roll is above $m$.
>
> Since the payoff amount is $X$, larger die rolls matter more. Therefore, being closer to the larger numbers is advantageous unless the midpoint is very high. The optimal bid should balance the total weight of outcomes on each side.
>
> The total of all die outcomes is
> $$
> 1+2+\cdots+30=\frac{30\cdot 31}{2}=465.
> $$
> To make the expected payoff $0$, the sum of winning outcomes on one side should equal the sum on the other side.
>
> We therefore look for a cutoff where the lower-side sum is as close as possible to half of $465$:
> $$
> \frac{465}{2}=232.5.
> $$
>
> Now compute cumulative sums:
> $$
> 1+2+\cdots+21=\frac{21\cdot 22}{2}=231,
> $$
> $$
> 1+2+\cdots+22=\frac{22\cdot 23}{2}=253.
> $$
>
> Thus the balance point lies between $21$ and $22$.
>
> This means the ideal midpoint is
> $$
> m=21.5.
> $$
>
> So the optimal bids must straddle $21.5$, namely
> $$
> 21 \quad \text{and} \quad 22.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Payoff when the bids are $21$ and $22$
>
> If one player bids $21$ and the other bids $22$, then:
>
> - bid $21$ wins for $X=1,2,\dots,21$,
> - bid $22$ wins for $X=22,23,\dots,30$,
> - there is no tie, since the midpoint is $21.5$.
>
> The expected payoff to the player bidding $21$ is
> $$
> \frac{1}{30}\left(\sum_{x=1}^{21} x-\sum_{x=22}^{30} x\right).
> $$
> Now
> $$
> \sum_{x=1}^{21} x=231,
> $$
> and
> $$
> \sum_{x=22}^{30} x=465-231=234.
> $$
> Hence
> $$
> E(21,22)=\frac{231-234}{30}=-\frac{3}{30}=-0.1.
> $$
> So the player bidding $21$ loses on average $\text{\textdollar}0.10$, and the player bidding $22$ gains on average $\text{\textdollar}0.10$.
>
> Thus, between these two adjacent bids, $22$ is slightly better than $21$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. Best response logic
>
> Now compare nearby bids.
>
> - If the opponent bids below $21$ or $22$, moving upward is good because it captures more high-value outcomes.
> - If the opponent bids above $22$, moving downward is good because too much weight is given away on the low side.
>
> The equilibrium occurs when neither player can improve by changing unilaterally. Since $21$ and $22$ are the two bids around the balancing point, the optimal strategy is to randomize between them.
>
> Let a player choose
>
> - $21$ with probability $p$,
> - $22$ with probability $1-p$.
>
> To make the opponent indifferent between bidding $21$ and $22$, we choose $p$ so that these two pure strategies have equal expected payoff against the mixture.
>
> Because the game is symmetric and zero-sum, the equilibrium mixture must put equal weight on the two balancing bids:
> $$
> p=\frac12.
> $$
>
> So the optimal strategy is:
>
> - bid $21$ with probability $\frac12$,
> - bid $22$ with probability $\frac12$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 5. Value of the game
>
> Under this symmetric optimal mixed strategy, the expected payoff is $0$.
>
> Indeed, since both players use the same mixed strategy in a symmetric zero-sum game, neither player has an advantage, so the value must be
> $$
> 0.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Final answer
>
> The optimal strategy is to randomize equally between the two central bids:
> $$
> 21 \text{ with probability } \frac12,\qquad 22 \text{ with probability } \frac12.
> $$
>
> The expected payoff under optimal play is
> $$
> 0.
> $$
>
> So neither player has an advantage if both play optimally.
