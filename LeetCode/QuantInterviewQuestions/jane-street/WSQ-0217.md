> [!example] Question 217 · Jane Street
> ## Internship Interview

Six cups and saucers come in pairs: there are two red, two white, and two with stars. If the cups are placed randomly onto the saucers (one on each), what is the probability that no cup is placed on a saucer of the same pattern?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=218)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Treat the $6$ cups and the $6$ saucers as distinct objects, with colors/patterns distributed as follows:
>
> - $2$ red cups and $2$ red saucers,
> - $2$ white cups and $2$ white saucers,
> - $2$ starred cups and $2$ starred saucers.
>
> A random placement of cups onto saucers is a random bijection from the $6$ cups to the $6$ saucers, so the total number of placements is
>
> $$
> 6!.
> $$
>
> We must count those placements in which no cup is placed on a saucer of the same pattern.
>
> ## Step 1: Reduce to a pattern-assignment problem
>
> Ignore for the moment which individual cup of a given pattern goes to which individual saucer of a given pattern. We first count the valid ways to assign each cup-pattern to saucer-patterns.
>
> There are $2$ cups of each of the three patterns $R,W,S$, and likewise $2$ saucers of each pattern. We seek a $3\times 3$ table of nonnegative integers $(a_{ij})$ where:
>
> - $a_{ij}$ is the number of cups of pattern $i$ placed on saucers of pattern $j$,
> - $a_{ii}=0$ for all $i$,
> - each row sum is $2$,
> - each column sum is $2$.
>
> Thus the table has the form
>
> $$
> \begin{array}{c|ccc}
>  & R & W & S\\ \hline
> R & 0 & a & b\\
> W & c & 0 & d\\
> S & e & f & 0
> \end{array}
> $$
>
> with
>
> $$
> a+b=2,\qquad c+d=2,\qquad e+f=2,
> $$
>
> and
>
> $$
> c+e=2,\qquad a+f=2,\qquad b+d=2.
> $$
>
> Solving these gives the valid tables.
>
> From $a+b=2$, let $b=2-a$.
> From $a+f=2$, $f=2-a$.
> From $b+d=2$, $d=a$.
> From $c+d=2$, $c=2-a$.
> From $c+e=2$, $e=a$.
>
> So every solution is determined by $a$, with
>
> $$
> a\in\{0,1,2\}.
> $$
>
> Hence there are exactly $3$ possible pattern-distributions:
>
> ### Case 1: $a=0$
> $$
> \begin{array}{c|ccc}
>  & R & W & S\\ \hline
> R & 0 & 0 & 2\\
> W & 2 & 0 & 0\\
> S & 0 & 2 & 0
> \end{array}
> $$
>
> ### Case 2: $a=1$
> $$
> \begin{array}{c|ccc}
>  & R & W & S\\ \hline
> R & 0 & 1 & 1\\
> W & 1 & 0 & 1\\
> S & 1 & 1 & 0
> \end{array}
> $$
>
> ### Case 3: $a=2$
> $$
> \begin{array}{c|ccc}
>  & R & W & S\\ \hline
> R & 0 & 2 & 0\\
> W & 0 & 0 & 2\\
> S & 2 & 0 & 0
> \end{array}
> $$
>
> ## Step 2: Count actual placements for each case
>
> Now restore the individuality of the cups and saucers.
>
> ### Case 1
> All $2$ red cups go to the $2$ starred saucers: this can be done in $2!$ ways.
>
> All $2$ white cups go to the $2$ red saucers: $2!$ ways.
>
> All $2$ starred cups go to the $2$ white saucers: $2!$ ways.
>
> So this case gives
>
> $$
> (2!)^3=8
> $$
>
> placements.
>
> ### Case 2
> The $2$ red cups must be split: one to a white saucer and one to a starred saucer.
>
> - Choose which red cup goes to white: $2$ ways.
> - Choose which white saucer it occupies: $2$ ways.
> - Choose which starred saucer the other red cup occupies: $2$ ways.
>
> Thus red cups contribute $2\cdot 2\cdot 2=8$ ways.
>
> Similarly, the white cups contribute $8$ ways, and the starred cups contribute $8$ ways, but these choices are not independent because saucers are shared. It is cleaner to count by matching within the prescribed counts:
>
> - Assign the $2$ red cups bijectively to the chosen $1$ white and $1$ starred saucer positions: $2!\cdot 2\cdot 2 /2 = 4$; more simply, choose which red cup goes to white ($2$ ways), then choose the white saucer ($2$ ways), and the rest is forced, giving $4$ ways.
> - Likewise for the white cups: $4$ ways.
> - Likewise for the starred cups: $4$ ways.
>
> Hence this case gives
>
> $$
> 4^3=64
> $$
>
> placements.
>
> ### Case 3
> This is analogous to Case 1, giving again
>
> $$
> (2!)^3=8
> $$
>
> placements.
>
> Therefore the total number of valid placements is
>
> $$
> 8+64+8=80.
> $$
>
> ## Step 3: Compute the probability
>
> The required probability is
>
> $$
> \frac{80}{6!}=\frac{80}{720}=\frac{1}{9}.
> $$
>
> ## Final answer
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{1}{9}}
> $$
