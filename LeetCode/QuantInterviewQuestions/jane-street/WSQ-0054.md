> [!example] Question 54 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

You throw 1,000 darts. Each dart has a 50% chance to score. For the first 500 darts, each is worth 1 point; for the next 500 darts, each is worth 3 points. If your total score is 1,500 points, what is the most likely number of 3-point darts you have scored?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=55)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let $X$ be the number of successful $1$-point darts among the first $500$, and let $Y$ be the number of successful $3$-point darts among the second $500$.
>
> Then
> $$
> X \sim \operatorname{Bin}(500, 1/2), \qquad Y \sim \operatorname{Bin}(500, 1/2),
> $$
> and $X$ and $Y$ are independent.
>
> The total score is
> $$
> X + 3Y.
> $$
> We are told that the total score is $1500$, so
> $$
> X + 3Y = 1500.
> $$
>
> We want the most likely value of $Y$ subject to this condition.
>
> Since
> $$
> X = 1500 - 3Y,
> $$
> the conditional probability is proportional to
> $$
> P(X = 1500 - 3Y,\; Y=y)
> = P(X = 1500 - 3y)\,P(Y=y),
> $$
> because of independence.
>
> Now $X$ and $Y$ are both $\operatorname{Bin}(500, 1/2)$, so
> $$
> P(X=k) = \binom{500}{k}2^{-500}, \qquad P(Y=y)=\binom{500}{y}2^{-500}.
> $$
> Hence, for feasible $y$,
> $$
> P(Y=y \mid X+3Y=1500) \propto \binom{500}{1500-3y}\binom{500}{y}.
> $$
>
> The constraints $0 \le X \le 500$ and $0 \le Y \le 500$ give
> $$
> 0 \le 1500-3y \le 500.
> $$
> Thus
> $$
> \frac{1000}{3} \le y \le 500.
> $$
> So $y$ must lie between $334$ and $500$.
>
> ## Finding the true maximizer
>
> To find the most likely $y$, maximize
> $$
> f(y)=\binom{500}{1500-3y}\binom{500}{y}.
> $$
> Let
> $$
> k = 1500-3y,
> $$
> so $k$ runs from $498$ down to $0$ as $y$ runs from $334$ up to $500$.
>
> A first instinct is to note that $\binom{500}{m}$ is largest when $m$ is closest to $250$, and to choose $y$ so that $k=1500-3y$ lands as close as possible to $250$; solving $1500-3y=250$ gives $y=1250/3\approx416.67$. This instinct is incomplete, because it only accounts for $\binom{500}{k}$ approaching its own peak, while ignoring two things: $\binom{500}{y}$ *also* wants $y$ close to $250$, and moving $y$ by one unit shifts $k$ by *three* units in the opposite direction, since $k=1500-3y$. The correct optimum balances both effects, weighted by how quickly each binomial coefficient changes.
>
> ### Balancing the two terms
>
> Using the ratio of consecutive binomial coefficients,
> $$
> \frac{\binom{500}{y+1}}{\binom{500}{y}}=\frac{500-y}{y+1},
> \qquad
> \frac{\binom{500}{k-3}}{\binom{500}{k}}=\frac{k(k-1)(k-2)}{(501-k)(502-k)(503-k)},
> $$
> the ratio of consecutive values of $f$ is
> $$
> \frac{f(y+1)}{f(y)}=\frac{500-y}{y+1}\cdot\frac{k(k-1)(k-2)}{(501-k)(502-k)(503-k)}.
> $$
> The maximizing $y$ is where this ratio crosses from above $1$ to below $1$. Approximating the discrete ratios by the corresponding logarithmic derivatives of $\log\binom{500}{m}$, which behave like $\log\frac{500-m}{m}$, gives the balance condition
> $$
> \log\frac{500-y}{y} \approx 3\log\frac{500-k}{k},
> \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad
> \frac{500-y}{y}\approx\left(\frac{500-k}{k}\right)^{3},
> $$
> where the factor of $3$ enters because $k$ moves three times as fast as $y$. Solving this together with $k=1500-3y$ gives
>
> $$
> y\approx 398, \qquad k\approx 306,
> $$
>
> in sharp contrast with the naive estimate of $417$.
>
> ### Checking the exact integer optimum
>
> Comparing $f(y)$ directly at integers near $398$ confirms this is indeed the maximum. The exact ratios are
>
> $$
> \frac{f(398)}{f(397)}\approx 1.052 > 1, \qquad \frac{f(399)}{f(398)}\approx 0.963 < 1,
> $$
>
> so $f$ increases up through $y=398$ and decreases immediately after, confirming a peak there. By contrast, the naive candidate $y=417$ is nowhere near the true maximum:
>
> $$
> \frac{f(398)}{f(417)}\approx 5.4\times 10^{6},
> $$
>
> so $y=398$ is millions of times more likely than $y=417$.
>
> ## Final answer
>
> Therefore, the most likely number of successful $3$-point darts is
>
> $$
> \boxed{398}.
> $$
