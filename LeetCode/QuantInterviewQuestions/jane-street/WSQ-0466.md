> [!example] Question 466 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Trader Interview

If you have x coins, into how many stacks, and with how many coins per stack, should you divide them in order to maximize the product of the stack sizes?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=467)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let the $x$ coins be split into $n$ stacks, with stack sizes
>
> $$
> a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n,
> $$
>
> where each $a_i$ is a positive integer and
>
> $$
> a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n=x.
> $$
>
> We want to maximize the product
>
> $$
> P=a_1a_2\cdots a_n.
> $$
>
> ## Step 1: The stacks should be as equal as possible
>
> For a fixed number of stacks $n$, the product is maximized when the parts are as equal as possible. This follows from the arithmetic mean–geometric mean principle.
>
> So the main question is: which stack sizes should be used?
>
> ## Step 2: Large stacks should be broken up
>
> Suppose one stack has size $m\ge 5$. Replacing it by two stacks of sizes $3$ and $m-3$ changes the product by a factor of
>
> $$
> \frac{3(m-3)}{m}.
> $$
>
> Since
>
> $$
> 3(m-3)>m \qquad \text{for } m\ge 5,
> $$
>
> breaking a stack of size at least $5$ increases the product. Hence, in an optimal decomposition, no stack has size greater than $4$.
>
> ## Step 3: A stack of size $4$ is equivalent to two stacks of size $2$
>
> Since
>
> $$
> 4=2+2
> $$
>
> and
>
> $$
> 4=2\cdot 2,
> $$
>
> a stack of size $4$ may be replaced by two stacks of size $2$ without changing the product.
>
> Thus it is enough to use only stacks of size $2$ and $3$.
>
> ## Step 4: Use as many $3$'s as possible
>
> Compare:
>
> $$
> 3 > 2,
> $$
>
> and for the same total sum,
>
> $$
> 3\cdot 3 = 9 > 2\cdot 2\cdot 2 = 8.
> $$
>
> So $3$'s are more efficient than $2$'s, and we should use as many stacks of size $3$ as possible.
>
> The only exception is when a remainder of $1$ would occur, because a stack of size $1$ is bad for the product. In that case, replace one $3+1$ by $2+2$, since
>
> $$
> 3\cdot 1 = 3 < 2\cdot 2 = 4.
> $$
>
> ## Final rule
>
> Write $x$ in the form
>
> $$
> x=3q+r, \qquad r\in\{0,1,2\}.
> $$
>
> Then the maximum product is obtained as follows:
>
> - If $r=0$, use $q$ stacks of size $3$.
>   
>   Product:
>   $$
>   3^q.
>   $$
>
> - If $r=1$, use $q-1$ stacks of size $3$ and $2$ stacks of size $2$.
>   
>   Product:
>   $$
>   3^{q-1}\cdot 2^2 = 4\cdot 3^{q-1}.
>   $$
>
> - If $r=2$, use $q$ stacks of size $3$ and $1$ stack of size $2$.
>   
>   Product:
>   $$
>   2\cdot 3^q.
>   $$
>
> ## Number of stacks
>
> Accordingly, the number of stacks is:
>
> - If $x\equiv 0 \pmod 3$, then $x/3$ stacks.
> - If $x\equiv 1 \pmod 3$, then $(x-4)/3+2=(x+2)/3$ stacks.
> - If $x\equiv 2 \pmod 3$, then $(x-2)/3+1=(x+1)/3$ stacks.
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> To maximize the product of the stack sizes:
>
> - split the coins into stacks of size $3$ as much as possible,
> - except that you should never leave a remainder of $1$,
> - so if that would happen, replace one $3+1$ by $2+2$.
>
> Thus the optimal partition is:
>
> $$
> x=
> \begin{cases}
> 3+3+\cdots+3, & x\equiv 0 \pmod 3,\\[4pt]
> 3+3+\cdots+3+2+2, & x\equiv 1 \pmod 3,\\[4pt]
> 3+3+\cdots+3+2, & x\equiv 2 \pmod 3.
> \end{cases}
> $$
>
> with the corresponding maximum product given above.
