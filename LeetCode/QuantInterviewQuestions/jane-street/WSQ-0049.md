> [!example] Question 49 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

You toss two dice. If the sum is 7, you win a dollar. If the sum is even, you lose a dollar. Otherwise, roll again. What is the expected payoff?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=50)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let $E$ denote the expected payoff from the game.
>
> ## Step 1: Classify the outcomes of one roll
>
> When two fair dice are tossed, there are $36$ equally likely outcomes.
>
> - Sum $7$: this occurs in $6$ ways, so
>   $$
>   P(\text{win } \$1)=\frac{6}{36}=\frac{1}{6}.
>   $$
>
> - Even sum: this occurs when both dice have the same parity, giving $18$ outcomes. Since sum $7$ is odd, there is no overlap. Thus
>   $$
>   P(\text{lose } \$1)=\frac{18}{36}=\frac{1}{2}.
>   $$
>
> - Otherwise: this means the sum is odd but not $7$. Its probability is
>   $$
>   1-\frac{1}{6}-\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{3}.
>   $$
>
> In this last case, the game restarts under the same conditions, so the expected payoff is again $E$.
>
> ## Step 2: Set up the expectation equation
>
> Conditioning on the result of the first roll,
> $$
> E=\frac{1}{6}(1)+\frac{1}{2}(-1)+\frac{1}{3}E.
> $$
>
> Simplify:
> $$
> E=\frac{1}{6}-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{3}E
> = -\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{3}E.
> $$
>
> ## Step 3: Solve for $E$
>
> Subtract $\frac{1}{3}E$ from both sides:
> $$
> E-\frac{1}{3}E=-\frac{1}{3}.
> $$
>
> So
> $$
> \frac{2}{3}E=-\frac{1}{3},
> $$
> and hence
> $$
> E=-\frac{1}{2}.
> $$
>
> ## Final answer
>
> The expected payoff is
>
> $$
> \boxed{-\frac{1}{2}\text{ dollar}}.
> $$
>
> So, on average, you lose $\text{\textdollar}0.50$ per game.
