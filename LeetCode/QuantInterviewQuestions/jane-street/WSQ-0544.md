> [!example] Question 544 · Jane Street
> ## Assistant Trader Interview

1. Four fair coins are tossed:
  a) What is the probability of getting more than 2 heads?
  b) What are the expected winnings if you receive \$1 for each head?
  c) What are the expected winnings if you may re-toss one coin of your choice?

2. A 20-sided die is rolled. Player A and B each choose a number before the roll. Whichever player's number is closest to the value of the die wins the amount shown on the die. What is the optimal strategy? Is it better to choose first? What are the expected winnings?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=545)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## 1. Four fair coin tosses
>
> Let $H$ be the number of heads in $4$ fair tosses. Then
>
> $$
> H \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(4,\tfrac12).
> $$
>
> ### (a) Probability of more than $2$ heads
>
> We want
>
> $$
> P(H>2)=P(H=3)+P(H=4).
> $$
>
> Using the binomial formula,
>
> $$
> P(H=3)=\binom{4}{3}\left(\tfrac12\right)^4=\frac{4}{16},
> \qquad
> P(H=4)=\binom{4}{4}\left(\tfrac12\right)^4=\frac{1}{16}.
> $$
>
> Hence
>
> $$
> P(H>2)=\frac{4}{16}+\frac{1}{16}=\frac{5}{16}.
> $$
>
> ## (b) Expected winnings at $1 per head
>
> If the payoff is \(\text{\textdollar}1\) for each head, then the winnings are exactly $H$ dollars. Therefore
>
> $$
> E[\text{winnings}]=E[H].
> $$
>
> For a binomial random variable,
>
> $$
> E[H]=np=4\cdot \frac12=2.
> $$
>
> So the expected winnings are
>
> $$
> \$2.
> $$
>
> ## (c) Expected winnings if one chosen coin may be re-tossed
>
> After seeing the $4$ tosses, one may choose at most one coin to re-toss.
>
> The optimal rule is clear:
>
> - if there is at least one tail, re-toss a tail;
> - if all $4$ are heads, do not re-toss.
>
> Why? Re-tossing a head can only reduce expected payoff, while re-tossing a tail changes that coin from value $0$ to expected value
>
> $$
> E[\text{value after re-toss}]=0\cdot \tfrac12+1\cdot \tfrac12=\tfrac12.
> $$
>
> Thus each outcome with at least one tail gains an additional expected
>
> $$
> \frac12
> $$
>
> from the re-toss. The only time there is no improvement is when all $4$ tosses are already heads, which happens with probability
>
> $$
> P(H=4)=\frac{1}{16}.
> $$
>
> So the expected winnings become
>
> $$
> E[H]+\frac12\,P(H<4)
> =2+\frac12\left(1-\frac{1}{16}\right)
> =2+\frac12\cdot \frac{15}{16}
> =2+\frac{15}{32}
> =\frac{79}{32}.
> $$
>
> Hence the expected winnings are
>
> $$
> \frac{79}{32}\approx \$2.46875.
> $$
>

>
> ## 2. One $20$-sided die game
>
> A fair die shows one of the values $1,2,\dots,20$. Players $A$ and $B$ each choose a number in $\{1,\dots,20\}$. Whoever is closer to the die value wins the amount shown on the die. Assume ties are split equally.
>
> We determine the optimal strategy, whether it is better to go first, and the expected winnings.
>
> ---
>
> ## Step 1: Best response to a fixed choice
>
> Suppose one player chooses $a$ and the other chooses $b$, with $a<b$.
>
> For a die roll $x$:
>
> - player choosing $a$ wins when $x<\dfrac{a+b}{2}$,
> - player choosing $b$ wins when $x>\dfrac{a+b}{2}$,
> - if $a+b$ is even and $x=\dfrac{a+b}{2}$, the round is tied.
>
> Since the payoff is the die value $x$, larger die values are more valuable. Thus being closer to the higher end of the die tends to be advantageous.
>
> ---
>
> ## Step 2: Find the second player's best response
>
> Let the first player choose $a$. The second player wants to choose a number that captures as many large die values as possible.
>
> A direct comparison shows:
>
> - choosing a number larger than the opponent is generally advantageous because the larger die outcomes carry larger payoffs;
> - the best reply is to choose either $a+1$ or a nearby larger number, except near the upper endpoint.
>
> Testing the extreme case is especially revealing. If the first player chooses $20$, then the second player can choose $19$.
>
> - For die values $1$ through $18$, $19$ is closer than $20$ only at $18$? More carefully:
>   - $20$ wins for values closer to $20$ than to $19$, namely $20$;
>   - $19$ wins for $1$ through $19$ except that at $19$ it is exact.
> - Thus $19$ captures die values $1$ through $19$, while $20$ captures only $20$.
>
> But because the payoff equals the die value, we must compare total expected winnings:
>
> - choosing $19$ against $20$ wins on $x=1,\dots,19$,
> - choosing $20$ against $19$ wins on $x=20$.
>
> So the expected payoff to the player choosing $19$ is
>
> $$
> \frac{1+2+\cdots+19}{20}=\frac{190}{20}=9.5,
> $$
>
> while the player choosing $20$ gets
>
> $$
> \frac{20}{20}=1.
> $$
>
> Thus $19$ is far better than $20$ against $20$.
>
> Now compare $18$ against $19$:
>
> - midpoint is $18.5$,
> - $18$ wins on $1,\dots,18$,
> - $19$ wins on $19,20$.
>
> Expected payoffs:
>
> $$
> \text{payoff}(18 \text{ vs } 19)=\frac{1+\cdots+18}{20}=\frac{171}{20}=8.55,
> $$
>
> $$
> \text{payoff}(19 \text{ vs } 18)=\frac{19+20}{20}=\frac{39}{20}=1.95.
> $$
>
> So $18$ beats $19$.
>
> Continuing this logic, each number is beaten by the number just below it, until we reach the bottom. For example, $1$ versus $2$:
>
> - midpoint is $1.5$,
> - $1$ wins only on die value $1$,
> - $2$ wins on $2,\dots,20$.
>
> Expected payoffs:
>
> $$
> \text{payoff}(1 \text{ vs } 2)=\frac{1}{20},
> \qquad
> \text{payoff}(2 \text{ vs } 1)=\frac{2+\cdots+20}{20}=\frac{209}{20}=10.45.
> $$
>
> Thus $2$ beats $1$.
>
> Therefore the chain is
>
> $$
> 1 \text{ is beaten by } 2,\quad
> 2 \text{ is beaten by } 1? 
> $$
>
> We must check carefully: against $2$, does $1$ or $3$ do better? The previous pattern must be analyzed correctly.
>
> ---
>
> ## Step 3: Correct local comparison
>
> If the opponent chooses $k$, compare choosing $k-1$ versus $k+1$.
>
> ### Choosing $k-1$ against $k$
>
> The midpoint is $k-\tfrac12$. So $k-1$ wins on die values
>
> $$
> 1,2,\dots,k-1,
> $$
>
> and $k$ wins on
>
> $$
> k,k+1,\dots,20.
> $$
>
> Thus the expected payoff to $k-1$ is
>
> $$
> \frac{1+2+\cdots+(k-1)}{20}
> =\frac{(k-1)k}{40}.
> $$
>
> ### Choosing $k+1$ against $k$
>
> The midpoint is $k+\tfrac12$. So $k+1$ wins on
>
> $$
> k+1,k+2,\dots,20,
> $$
>
> and $k$ wins on
>
> $$
> 1,2,\dots,k.
> $$
>
> Thus the expected payoff to $k+1$ is
>
> $$
> \frac{(k+1)+(k+2)+\cdots+20}{20}.
> $$
>
> Since high die values are worth more, choosing above $k$ is usually better than choosing below $k$.
>
> In fact, the game is solved by choosing the middle, so that moving above gains high values while not sacrificing too many outcomes.
>
> ---
>
> ## Step 4: Compute expected payoff when choices are adjacent
>
> If the numbers are $k$ and $k+1$, then:
>
> - $k$ wins on $1,\dots,k$,
> - $k+1$ wins on $k+1,\dots,20$.
>
> So the expected payoff to the player choosing $k$ is
>
> $$
> \frac{1+2+\cdots+k}{20}
> =\frac{k(k+1)}{40},
> $$
>
> and to the player choosing $k+1$ is
>
> $$
> \frac{(k+1)+\cdots+20}{20}
> =\frac{210-\frac{k(k+1)}{2}}{20}.
> $$
>
> To make these as balanced as possible, choose $k$ near the point where
>
> $$
> 1+2+\cdots+k \approx (k+1)+\cdots+20.
> $$
>
> Since
>
> $$
> 1+2+\cdots+20=210,
> $$
>
> we want
>
> $$
> 1+2+\cdots+k \approx 105.
> $$
>
> Now
>
> $$
> 1+2+\cdots+14=105.
> $$
>
> So the split occurs exactly between $14$ and $15$.
>
> Thus:
>
> - if one player chooses $14$, the other should choose $15$;
> - if one player chooses $15$, the other should choose $14$.
>
> These are the central optimal choices.
>
> ---
>
> ## Step 5: Compare $14$ and $15$
>
> If one player chooses $14$ and the other chooses $15$, then
>
> - $14$ wins die values $1$ through $14$,
> - $15$ wins die values $15$ through $20$.
>
> Expected payoff to the player choosing $14$:
>
> $$
> \frac{1+2+\cdots+14}{20}
> =\frac{105}{20}
> =5.25.
> $$
>
> Expected payoff to the player choosing $15$:
>
> $$
> \frac{15+16+\cdots+20}{20}
> =\frac{105}{20}
> =5.25.
> $$
>
> So both receive the same expected payoff.
>
> Since the total expected die amount is
>
> $$
> E[\text{die}]=\frac{1+20}{2}=10.5,
> $$
>
> each player gets half in expectation:
>
> $$
> \frac{10.5}{2}=5.25.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Conclusions
>
> ### Four coins
> 1. Probability of more than $2$ heads:
>    $$
>    \frac{5}{16}.
>    $$
>
> 2. Expected winnings at $1 per head:
>    $$
>    \$2.
>    $$
>
> 3. Expected winnings with the option to re-toss one chosen coin optimally:
>    $$
>    \frac{79}{32}\approx \$2.46875.
>    $$
>
> ### $20$-sided die game
> - The optimal choices are the middle pair:
>   $$
>   14 \text{ and } 15.
>   $$
> - If one player chooses first, the second player can choose the other middle number, so **there is no first-mover advantage**.
> - Under optimal play, the expected winnings for each player are
>   $$
>   \$5.25.
>   $$
