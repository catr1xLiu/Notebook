> [!example] Question 190 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Trading Intern Interview

You are given a stack of 10 chips. Arrange the chips into piles so that the product of the number of chips in each pile is maximized.

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=191)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> To maximize the product of the pile sizes, partition $10$ into positive integers whose product is as large as possible.
>
> Let the pile sizes be positive integers
> $$
> a_1,a_2,\dots,a_k
> $$
> with
> $$
> a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_k=10.
> $$
> We want to maximize
> $$
> P=a_1a_2\cdots a_k.
> $$
>
> ## Step 1: Eliminate large parts
>
> If a pile has size at least $5$, say $n\ge 5$, then replacing it by two piles of sizes $3$ and $n-3$ increases the product, since
> $$
> 3(n-3)>n
> $$
> for $n\ge 5$:
> $$
> 3n-9>n \iff 2n>9,
> $$
> which is true for $n\ge 5$.
>
> Thus, in an optimal partition, no pile size is greater than $4$.
>
> ## Step 2: Compare small pile sizes
>
> A pile of size $4$ is equivalent to two piles of size $2$, since
> $$
> 4=2+2 \quad \text{and} \quad 4=2\cdot 2.
> $$
> So using $4$ is no better than using two $2$'s.
>
> A pile of size $1$ is undesirable, because if another pile has size at least $3$, then
> $$
> 1\cdot n < 2(n-1)
> $$
> for $n\ge 3$,
> so replacing $1$ and $n$ by $2$ and $n-1$ increases the product.
>
> Hence the best pile sizes are mostly $3$'s, with possibly one $2$ if needed.
>
> ## Step 3: Partition $10$
>
> Divide $10$ by $3$:
> $$
> 10=3+3+4.
> $$
> Since $4=2+2$, the best partition is
> $$
> 10=3+3+2+2.
> $$
> Its product is
> $$
> 3\cdot 3\cdot 2\cdot 2=36.
> $$
>
> Other nearby partitions give smaller products:
> $$
> 5+5 \to 25,\qquad 4+3+3 \to 36,\qquad 6+4 \to 24,\qquad 2+2+2+2+2 \to 32.
> $$
>
> Therefore, the maximum product is
> $$
> 36.
> $$
>
> This is achieved, for example, by arranging the chips into piles of sizes
> $$
> 3,\ 3,\ 4
> $$
> or equivalently
> $$
> 3,\ 3,\ 2,\ 2.
> $$
