> [!example] Question 575 · Jane Street
> ## Assistant Trader Interview

You have a tricycle (with three tyres) and two spare tyres, making five tyres in total. You plan to travel 1,000 miles. a) If you want each tyre to be worn equally by the end of the journey, how many miles will each tyre travel on the ground? b) What is the minimum number of stops needed to achieve this?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=576)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let the five tyres be labeled $T_1,T_2,T_3,T_4,T_5$. At any time, exactly $3$ tyres are on the ground, since the vehicle is a tricycle.
>
> ## (a) Equal wear
>
> Over the whole trip of $1000$ miles, the total tyre-ground mileage accumulated by all tyres is
>
> $$
> 3 \times 1000 = 3000 \text{ tyre-miles}.
> $$
>
> If all $5$ tyres are to be worn equally, each must accumulate the same ground mileage, namely
>
> $$
> \frac{3000}{5} = 600 \text{ miles}.
> $$
>
> Thus each tyre must travel on the ground for
>
> $$
> \boxed{600 \text{ miles}}.
> $$
>
> ## (b) Minimum number of stops
>
> A stop is needed only when tyres are exchanged. We seek the fewest exchanges so that each tyre is used for exactly $600$ miles.
>
> ### Step 1: Show that one stop is impossible
>
> With no stop, only $3$ tyres are ever used, so equal wear is impossible.
>
> With exactly one stop, the trip is split into two intervals:
> - first interval of length $x$,
> - second interval of length $1000-x$.
>
> Suppose the first set of $3$ tyres used is $A$, and the second set is $B$, also of size $3$.
>
> Since there are $5$ tyres total, the two $3$-element sets must overlap in at least one tyre. A tyre in the overlap would be on the ground for
>
> $$
> x + (1000-x) = 1000 \text{ miles},
> $$
>
> which cannot equal the required $600$ miles. Hence one stop cannot work.
>
> So at least $2$ stops are necessary.
>
> ### Step 2: Construct a scheme with two stops
>
> It remains to show that $2$ stops suffice.
>
> Use the tyres in three stages of lengths $200$, $400$, and $400$ miles as follows:
>
> - Miles $0$ to $200$: use $T_1,T_2,T_3$.
> - Miles $200$ to $600$: use $T_1,T_4,T_5$.
> - Miles $600$ to $1000$: use $T_2,T_3,T_4$.
>
> Now compute each tyre’s total ground mileage:
>
> - $T_1$: $200 + 400 = 600$,
> - $T_2$: $200 + 400 = 600$,
> - $T_3$: $200 + 400 = 600$,
> - $T_4$: $400 + 400 = 800$,
>
> so this particular trial does not yet work. We need a better arrangement.
>
> Instead, choose three stages whose active tyre-sets are
>
> - Stage $1$: $T_1,T_2,T_3$ for $a$ miles,
> - Stage $2$: $T_1,T_4,T_5$ for $b$ miles,
> - Stage $3$: $T_2,T_3,T_4$ for $c$ miles,
>
> with
>
> $$
> a+b+c=1000.
> $$
>
> The tyre mile conditions are
>
> $$
> T_1: a+b=600,
> $$
> $$
> T_2: a+c=600,
> $$
> $$
> T_3: a+c=600,
> $$
> $$
> T_4: b+c=600,
> $$
> $$
> T_5: b=600.
> $$
>
> From $b=600$, we get
>
> $$
> a=0,\qquad c=400,
> $$
>
> which is degenerate, so this choice of sets cannot work. We therefore choose a symmetric arrangement of three stages where each tyre appears in exactly two stages except one pair distribution adjusted to total $600$.
>
> Take:
>
> - Miles $0$ to $200$: use $T_1,T_2,T_3$,
> - Miles $200$ to $400$: use $T_1,T_4,T_5$,
> - Miles $400$ to $600$: use $T_2,T_4,T_5$,
> - Miles $600$ to $800$: use $T_3,T_4,T_5$,
> - Miles $800$ to $1000$: use $T_1,T_2,T_3$.
>
> This achieves equal wear, but it uses $4$ stops, not minimal.
>
> So we return to the lower bound and refine it.
>
> ### Step 3: Two stops are in fact impossible
>
> With two stops, there are three stages, with lengths $x,y,z$, and corresponding $3$-tyre sets $A,B,C$.
>
> Each tyre must total $600$ miles, so for each tyre, the sum of the lengths of the stages in which it appears must be $600$.
>
> Now observe that a tyre can appear in:
> - exactly one stage, giving mileage one of $x,y,z$;
> - exactly two stages, giving mileage one of $x+y$, $x+z$, $y+z$;
> - all three stages, giving mileage $1000$.
>
> Since each tyre must get $600$, no tyre can appear in all three stages.
>
> Also, if a tyre appears in exactly one stage, then one of $x,y,z$ must equal $600$.
>
> If a tyre appears in exactly two stages, then one of
> $$
> x+y,\quad x+z,\quad y+z
> $$
> must equal $600$.
>
> But because each stage uses $3$ tyres, the total number of stage-appearances is
>
> $$
> 3+3+3=9.
> $$
>
> If all $5$ tyres appear in exactly two stages, that would require $10$ appearances, impossible. Therefore at least one tyre appears in exactly one stage, so one of $x,y,z=600$.
>
> Without loss of generality, let $x=600$. Then the remaining two stages total $400$, so any tyre appearing in stage $1$ and one other stage would accumulate more than $600$, impossible. Thus the $3$ tyres in stage $1$ can appear nowhere else, already accounting for $3$ distinct tyres. The remaining two stages must then use only the remaining $2$ tyres, but each stage needs $3$ tyres, impossible.
>
> Hence two stops are impossible.
>
> Therefore at least $3$ stops are necessary.
>
> ### Step 4: Construct a solution with three stops
>
> Split the trip into four stages of $250$ miles each:
>
> 1. $0$–$250$: use $T_1,T_2,T_3$,
> 2. $250$–$500$: use $T_1,T_4,T_5$,
> 3. $500$–$750$: use $T_2,T_4,T_5$,
> 4. $750$–$1000$: use $T_3,T_4,T_5$.
>
> Then the mileages are:
>
> - $T_1$: $250+250=500$,
> - $T_2$: $250+250=500$,
> - $T_3$: $250+250=500$,
> - $T_4$: $250+250+250=750$,
> - $T_5$: $250+250+250=750$.
>
> This is not equal, so adjust the stage lengths.
>
> Let the four stages have lengths $a,b,c,d$ with sets
>
> - $T_1,T_2,T_3$,
> - $T_1,T_4,T_5$,
> - $T_2,T_4,T_5$,
> - $T_3,T_4,T_5$.
>
> Then the equal-wear equations are
>
> $$
> a+b=600,
> $$
> $$
> a+c=600,
> $$
> $$
> a+d=600,
> $$
> $$
> b+c+d=600,
> $$
> $$
> b+c+d=600.
> $$
>
> From the first three equations,
>
> $$
> b=c=d=600-a.
> $$
>
> Substitute into $b+c+d=600$:
>
> $$
> 3(600-a)=600,
> $$
>
> so
>
> $$
> 1800-3a=600,
> $$
> $$
> 3a=1200,
> $$
> $$
> a=400.
> $$
>
> Hence
>
> $$
> b=c=d=200.
> $$
>
> Check the total distance:
>
> $$
> a+b+c+d=400+200+200+200=1000.
> $$
>
> Thus a valid schedule is:
>
> - Miles $0$ to $400$: use $T_1,T_2,T_3$,
> - Miles $400$ to $600$: use $T_1,T_4,T_5$,
> - Miles $600$ to $800$: use $T_2,T_4,T_5$,
> - Miles $800$ to $1000$: use $T_3,T_4,T_5$.
>
> Now each tyre gets exactly $600$ miles:
>
> - $T_1$: $400+200=600$,
> - $T_2$: $400+200=600$,
> - $T_3$: $400+200=600$,
> - $T_4$: $200+200+200=600$,
> - $T_5$: $200+200+200=600$.
>
> So the minimum number of stops is
>
> $$
> \boxed{3}.
> $$
>
> ## Final answers
>
> $$
> \boxed{(a)\ 600 \text{ miles per tyre}}
> $$
>
> $$
> \boxed{(b)\ 3 \text{ stops}}
> $$
