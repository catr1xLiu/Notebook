> [!example] Question 132 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Trader - Intern Interview

There are 1000 people in a hall. One person has their hand painted. Every minute, everyone shakes hands with someone else. How much time is needed to paint all the hands? What is the best-case scenario? What is the worst-case scenario?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=133)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let $P_t$ denote the number of people whose hands are painted after $t$ minutes.
>
> Each minute, everyone pairs up and shakes hands, so the $1000$ people are partitioned into $500$ disjoint pairs. A painted hand transfers paint to an unpainted hand whenever such a pairing occurs.
>
> The question asks for the time needed to get all $1000$ hands painted, and for the best and worst possible scenarios.
>
> ## Best-case scenario
>
> To spread the paint as fast as possible, every painted person should shake hands with an unpainted person whenever possible.
>
> Thus, as long as there are at least as many unpainted people as painted people, the number of painted hands can at most double each minute:
>
> $$
> P_{t+1} \le 2P_t.
> $$
>
> Starting with one painted hand,
>
> $$
> P_0 = 1.
> $$
>
> So in the fastest possible case,
>
> $$
> P_t = 2^t
> $$
>
> until this reaches $1000$.
>
> We need the smallest $t$ such that
>
> $$
> 2^t \ge 1000.
> $$
>
> Now,
>
> $$
> 2^9 = 512,\qquad 2^{10} = 1024.
> $$
>
> Hence the minimum possible time is
>
> $$
> t = 10.
> $$
>
> This is achievable: for the first $9$ minutes, the number of painted hands can double:
>
> $$
> 1,2,4,8,16,32,64,128,256,512.
> $$
>
> At minute $10$, the $512$ painted people can each shake hands with $488$ of the $488$ unpainted people (and the remaining $24$ painted people shake among themselves), so all $1000$ become painted.
>
> Therefore, the **best-case time** is
>
> $$
> \boxed{10\text{ minutes}}.
> $$
>
> ## Worst-case scenario
>
> To slow the spread down as much as possible, we need to look more carefully at what a single minute's round of handshakes can and cannot do to $P_t$.
>
> Since everyone pairs up, split the $500$ pairs formed in a given minute into three kinds: pairs of two painted people, pairs of two unpainted people, and "crossing" pairs of one painted and one unpainted person. If there are $z$ crossing pairs and $x$ painted-painted pairs, counting painted people on each side gives
>
> $$
> 2x+z=P_t.
> $$
>
> Since $2x$ is even, this forces
>
> $$
> z\equiv P_t \pmod 2:
> $$
>
> the number of crossing pairs in any given minute must have the **same parity** as the current number of painted people. Each crossing pair paints exactly one new person, so
>
> $$
> P_{t+1}=P_t+z.
> $$
>
> Initially $P_0=1$, which is odd, so $z$ must be odd. Since at most $\min(P_0,1000-P_0)=1$ crossing pair is even possible, the only option is $z=1$ — this first step cannot be avoided or slowed down. So after the first minute,
>
> $$
> P_1 = 1+1 = 2.
> $$
>
> From this point on, $P_t=2$ is even, so every subsequent $z$ must also be even. To delay the process as much as possible while still making progress, the slowest legal choice each minute is the smallest positive even number of crossings, $z=2$. Under this strategy, $P_t$ increases by exactly $2$ each minute:
>
> $$
> 2,4,6,\dots,1000.
> $$
>
> Reaching $1000$ from $2$ in steps of $2$ takes
>
> $$
> \frac{1000-2}{2}=499
> $$
>
> more minutes.
>
> Hence the worst-case total time is
>
> $$
> 1+499=500
> $$
>
> minutes.
>
> Thus the **worst-case time** is
>
> $$
> \boxed{500\text{ minutes}}.
> $$
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> - **Best scenario:** $\boxed{10\text{ minutes}}$
> - **Worst scenario:** $\boxed{500\text{ minutes}}$
