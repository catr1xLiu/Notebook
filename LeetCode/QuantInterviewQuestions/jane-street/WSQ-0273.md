> [!example] Question 273 · Jane Street
> ## Junior Trader Interview

Choose a subset of numbers from 1 to 30 such that no two elements in the subset share a common factor (i.e., they are pairwise coprime). What is the subset with the largest possible sum?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=274)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> To maximize the sum, we want to choose as many large numbers as possible, subject to the condition that any two chosen numbers have no common factor greater than $1$; that is, they must be **pairwise coprime**.
>
> ## Step 1: Key observation
>
> If two numbers are both divisible by a prime $p$, they cannot both be in the subset. Thus each chosen number must use a set of prime factors disjoint from those of every other chosen number — for each prime $p\le 30$, at most one chosen number may be a multiple of $p$.
>
> The number $1$ is coprime to every other integer, so it never conflicts with anything. It should always be included, since it adds to the sum for free.
>
> ## Step 2: Primes with no competing multiple
>
> For primes $p$ with $15<p\le 30$ — namely $17,19,23,29$ — the smallest multiple of $p$ other than $p$ itself exceeds $30$, so nothing else in range can use these primes. They cost nothing to include, so an optimal set always contains all four.
>
> ## Step 3: Primes with a genuine trade-off
>
> For the remaining primes $2,3,5,7,11,13$ (each at most $15$), a multiple other than the prime itself does lie in $\{2,\dots,30\}$, so there is a real choice between using the prime directly or using a composite built from it:
>
> - **Prime $5$:** using $25=5^2$ instead of $5$ gains $25-5=20$, using only the slot for $5$.
> - **Prime $3$:** using $27=3^3$ instead of $3$ gains $27-3=24$, using only the slot for $3$.
> - **Primes $2$ and $7$ together:** pairing them as $28=2^2\cdot 7$ is worth $28$, versus $2+7=9$ separately, or versus the best single-slot alternative ($16$ for $2$ alone, plus $7$) which totals only $23$. Pairing wins.
> - **Primes $11$ and $13$:** the only available composites are $22=2\cdot 11$ and $26=2\cdot 13$, both of which would require giving up $28$ (since they also need the slot for $2$). For example, trading $28$ and $11$ (total $39$) for $22$ and a lone $7$ (total $29$) is a loss. So $11$ and $13$ are best kept as themselves.
>
> Grouping more than two small primes into a single composite does not help either: $30=2\cdot3\cdot5$ ties up the slots for $2,3,5$ at once but is worth only $30$, far less than $28+27+25=80$ for those same slots.
>
> ## Step 4: Assemble the optimal set
>
> Combining:
>
> - the free element $1$,
> - the forced large primes $17,19,23,29$,
> - the small primes $11,13$ kept as themselves,
> - the composites $25,27,28$ in place of $5,3,\{2,7\}$,
>
> gives the set
> $$
> \{1,11,13,17,19,23,25,27,28,29\}.
> $$
>
> These are pairwise coprime: $25=5^2$, $27=3^3$, $28=2^2\cdot 7$, and $1,11,13,17,19,23,29$ share no prime factors with any of them or with each other.
>
> Its sum is
> $$
> 1+11+13+17+19+23+25+27+28+29 = 193.
> $$
>
> ## Step 5: Why this is maximal
>
> The four primes $17,19,23,29$ are compulsory, since nothing else can use those prime slots. For each of the small-prime slots $2,3,5,7,11,13$, the comparisons above show the chosen number is at least as good as any competing use of that slot, and $1$ can always be added for free. Hence no pairwise coprime subset of $\{1,2,\dots,30\}$ has sum exceeding $193$.
>
> ## Final answer
>
> A maximum-sum subset is $\{1,11,13,17,19,23,25,27,28,29\}$, and the largest possible sum is $193$.
