> [!example] Question 659 · Jane Street
> ## Quant Trader Intern Interview

You repeatedly roll a fair six-sided die. After each roll, you add the result to your cumulative score. At any point, you may choose to stop the game and keep your current score. However, if your total score ever becomes a perfect square, you immediately lose and receive zero points. What is the optimal strategy to maximize your expected score?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=660)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> Let $V(s)$ denote the maximum expected payoff when the current total is $s$, under optimal play.
>
> At state $s$:
>
> - you may **stop** and receive $s$;
> - or **roll** once more.
>
> If you roll, then with probability $\frac16$ you move to each of the states $s+1,\dots,s+6$, except that if $s+k$ is a perfect square, the game ends immediately with payoff $0$.
>
> Thus
> $$
> V(s)=\max\left\{\,s,\ \frac16\sum_{k=1}^6 W(s+k)\right\},
> $$
> where
> $$
> W(t)=\begin{cases}0, & t \text{ is a perfect square},\\ V(t), & t \text{ is not a perfect square}.\end{cases}
> $$
>
> We seek the optimal decision at $s=0$, and the value $V(0)$.
>
> A perfect-square total is never itself a "live" state you get to choose from — landing on one ends the game immediately at $0$ — so the stop-or-roll decision only needs to be resolved at non-square totals.
>
> ## 1. Large totals: stopping is eventually optimal
>
> The dangerous totals are the perfect squares:
> $$
> 1,4,9,16,25,36,49,\dots
> $$
> The gaps between consecutive squares are
> $$
> (n+1)^2-n^2=2n+1,
> $$
> which eventually exceed $6$. In particular, for $n\ge3$, $2n+1\ge7$, so from the gap between $9$ and $16$ onward there is never more than one square among any six consecutive integers.
>
> Once the current total $s$ is large, a roll can hit at most one square, and the guaranteed payoff $s$ from stopping is already substantial, so the small chance of losing everything eventually outweighs the modest potential gain from one more roll. Stopping is therefore optimal once $s$ is large enough — but exactly how large depends delicately on the interaction between the size of $s$ and the location of the next square, and it has to be worked out by genuine backward induction rather than guessed.
>
> ## 2. The decision does not reduce to a single threshold
>
> A natural first guess is that there is one cutoff value, below which you should always roll and above which you should always stop. This turns out to be false. The correct policy, obtained by carrying out the backward induction $V(s)=\max\{s,\tfrac16\sum_k W(s+k)\}$ from large $s$ down to $s=0$, keeps rolling almost everywhere and stops only in narrow windows located just below the perfect squares $36,49,64,81,100,\dots$ — and even then, only once the square is large enough. We check this directly for the states surrounding the smaller squares, where a naive threshold guess is most tempting.
>
> ### States just below $25$
>
> Consider $s=19,20,\dots,24$, all sitting just below the square $25$. Rolling from $s=24$, for instance, reaches $25,26,27,28,29,30$; a roll of $1$ busts the game (payoff $0$), while the other five outcomes continue with values $V(26),\dots,V(30)$, each comfortably above $s$, since none of $26,\dots,30$ is a square and continuing from there remains attractive. Working this out gives
> $$
> \text{roll value at }24=\frac{0+V(26)+V(27)+V(28)+V(29)+V(30)}{6}\approx26.91,
> $$
> which comfortably exceeds $24$, so rolling is optimal at $24$ — not stopping. The same conclusion holds throughout $s=19,\dots,24$: each of these totals is still far enough below the accumulated stakes that make stopping attractive, and close enough to a run of appealing non-square continuations, that rolling remains strictly better than stopping. So $19$–$24$ is **not** a stopping zone, even though it sits directly below a perfect square.
>
> ### The first real stopping window: just below $36$
>
> The picture changes once the squares themselves are bigger. Just below $36$, at $s=30$ and $s=31$, stopping finally becomes optimal: rolling from $s=30$ reaches $31,\dots,36$, and since $36$ is a square, that outcome contributes $0$; working out the roll value gives approximately $28.67$, now less than $30$, so stopping is optimal at $30$ (and similarly at $31$). But move just two steps further back, to $s=32,\dots,35$: rolling from these totals reaches past $36$ into the comfortably higher totals $37,\dots,41$, and the roll value once again exceeds $s$, so rolling remains optimal there. The stopping window below $36$ is therefore only the two states $\{30,31\}$, not the full run of six states below the square.
>
> ## 3. The full stopping policy
>
> Carrying out the same backward induction systematically — it does not reduce to a clean closed form; the values must be computed recursively, state by state — shows that this pattern continues and widens as the squares grow larger: rolling almost everywhere, with only a narrow stopping window just below each sufficiently large perfect square.
>
> - below $36$: stop at $\{30,31\}$;
> - below $49$: stop at $\{43,44,45\}$;
> - below $64$: stop at $\{58,59,60,61,62\}$;
> - below $81$: stop at $\{75,76,77,78,79,80\}$;
> - below $100$: stop at $\{94,95,96,97,98,99\}$;
> - and so on, the stopping window widening toward the full $6$ states as the squares grow larger.
>
> Everywhere else — including the entire range from $0$ through $29$, and the gaps between each stopping window and the next square — rolling is strictly optimal.
>
> ## 4. The value of the game
>
> Carrying the backward induction all the way down to $s=0$ gives
> $$
> V(0)\approx7.176.
> $$
>
> ## Final answer
>
> The optimal strategy is **not** a simple "roll below a cutoff, stop above it" rule. Instead:
>
> - keep rolling at every total from $0$ up to $29$, and indeed at almost every total in general;
> - stop only within the narrow windows $\{30,31\}$, $\{43,44,45\}$, $\{58,\dots,62\}$, $\{75,\dots,80\}$, $\{94,\dots,99\}$, and so on, just below each sufficiently large perfect square;
> - everywhere else, keep rolling.
>
> With this policy, the expected score starting from $0$ is
>
> $$
> \boxed{V(0)\approx7.176.}
> $$
