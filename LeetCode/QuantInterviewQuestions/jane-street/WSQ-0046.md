> [!example] Question 46 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

You play rock, paper, scissors against an opponent who cannot play rock. To maximize your expected profit, what should you play, given that you win \$1 for a win, lose \$1 for a loss, and win \$0 for a draw?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=47)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let the opponent choose between only **paper** and **scissors**. Since the opponent cannot play rock, compare the expected payoff of each of your possible actions.
>
> Assume first that the opponent plays paper with probability $p$ and scissors with probability $1-p$.
>
> ## Expected payoff of each move
>
> - If you play **rock**:
>   - against paper: you lose $1$
>   - against scissors: you win $1$
>
>   Hence
>   $$
>   E_R = (-1)p + (1)(1-p) = 1 - 2p.
>   $$
>
> - If you play **paper**:
>   - against paper: draw, payoff $0$
>   - against scissors: lose $1$
>
>   Hence
>   $$
>   E_P = 0\cdot p + (-1)(1-p) = p - 1.
>   $$
>
> - If you play **scissors**:
>   - against paper: win $1$
>   - against scissors: draw, payoff $0$
>
>   Hence
>   $$
>   E_S = (1)p + 0\cdot(1-p) = p.
>   $$
>
> ## Compare the payoffs
>
> First compare scissors and paper:
> $$
> E_S - E_P = p - (p-1) = 1 > 0.
> $$
> So scissors is always better than paper.
>
> Now compare scissors and rock:
> $$
> E_S - E_R = p - (1-2p) = 3p - 1.
> $$
> Thus:
>
> - if $p > \tfrac{1}{3}$, play scissors;
> - if $p < \tfrac{1}{3}$, play rock;
> - if $p = \tfrac{1}{3}$, rock and scissors are equally good.
>
> Therefore, without further information about how often the opponent chooses paper versus scissors, there is no single universally optimal pure move.
>
> If the opponent is assumed to choose paper and scissors equally likely, so $p=\tfrac12$, then
> $$
> E_R = 0,\qquad E_P = -\tfrac12,\qquad E_S = \tfrac12,
> $$
> so the optimal choice is **scissors**.
>
> ## Final answer
>
> If the opponent is equally likely to play paper or scissors, you should play **scissors**. More generally, if the opponent plays paper with probability $p$, the best response is:
>
> $$
> \text{play scissors if } p>\tfrac13,\quad \text{play rock if } p<\tfrac13.
> $$
