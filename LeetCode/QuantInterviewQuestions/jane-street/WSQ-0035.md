> [!example] Question 35 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Research Interview

You have a standard 52-card deck. Cards are drawn one by one and placed face up, without replacement. At any point, you may stop and name a color (red or black). If the next two cards drawn are both of the chosen color, you win; otherwise, you lose. What is the optimal strategy?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=36)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> Let a state of the game be described by the numbers of red and black cards remaining in the deck:
> $$
> (r,b),
> $$
> where initially
> $$
> (r,b) = (26,26).
> $$
>
> At any state $(r,b)$, you may stop and name either red or black. If you name red, you win exactly when the next two cards are both red, which has probability
> $$
> \frac{r}{r+b}\cdot\frac{r-1}{r+b-1}
> = \frac{r(r-1)}{(r+b)(r+b-1)}.
> $$
> Similarly, if you name black, your winning probability is
> $$
> \frac{b(b-1)}{(r+b)(r+b-1)}.
> $$
>
> If instead you continue, one card is exposed. With probability $\frac{r}{r+b}$ the next state is $(r-1,b)$, and with probability $\frac{b}{r+b}$ the next state is $(r,b-1)$.
>
> Thus the problem is a dynamic programming problem. Let $V(r,b)$ be the maximum possible probability of eventually winning starting from state $(r,b)$. Then
> $$
> V(r,b)
> =
> \max\!\left\{
> \frac{r(r-1)}{(r+b)(r+b-1)},
> \frac{b(b-1)}{(r+b)(r+b-1)},
> \frac{r}{r+b}V(r-1,b)+\frac{b}{r+b}V(r,b-1)
> \right\}.
> $$
>
> We now determine the optimal rule.
>
> ---
>
> ## Step 1: If you stop, you should name the majority color
>
> At state $(r,b)$, comparing the two stopping probabilities gives
> $$
> \frac{r(r-1)}{(r+b)(r+b-1)}
> \quad\text{vs.}\quad
> \frac{b(b-1)}{(r+b)(r+b-1)}.
> $$
> Since the denominators are the same, the larger is obtained by choosing the larger of $r$ and $b$. Hence if you stop, you should always name the color that is currently in the majority (or either color, in case of a tie).
>
> So the stopping value is
> $$
> S(r,b)=\frac{\max\{r(r-1),\,b(b-1)\}}{(r+b)(r+b-1)}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Step 2: Waiting is often strictly better than stopping
>
> It is tempting to guess that waiting one more card never changes your winning probability, so that $V(r,b)=S(r,b)$ always. This guess is false as soon as both colors still have at least two cards left, as the following direct computation shows.
>
> Take $r=b=2$ (two red and two black cards remain). Stopping now and naming either color gives
> $$
> S(2,2)=\frac{2\cdot1}{4\cdot3}=\frac16.
> $$
>
> Now compute the value of waiting one more card. If the next card is red (probability $\tfrac12$), the state becomes $(1,2)$; if black (probability $\tfrac12$), it becomes $(2,1)$, which is symmetric. Working out $(1,2)$ directly:
>
> - Stopping at $(1,2)$ and naming black gives $\dfrac{2\cdot1}{3\cdot2}=\dfrac13$.
> - Waiting once more from $(1,2)$: with probability $\tfrac13$ the next card is red, leaving state $(0,2)$ — only two black cards remain, so naming black is a **certain** win, value $1$; with probability $\tfrac23$ the next card is black, leaving state $(1,1)$ — one card of each color remains, so no single-color bet on the last two cards can ever succeed, value $0$. This gives a continuation value of $\tfrac13(1)+\tfrac23(0)=\tfrac13$, tying the immediate stopping value at $(1,2)$.
>
> So $V(1,2)=\tfrac13$, and by symmetry $V(2,1)=\tfrac13$. Waiting one card from $(2,2)$ is therefore worth
> $$
> \frac12\cdot\frac13+\frac12\cdot\frac13=\frac13.
> $$
>
> Since $\dfrac13>\dfrac16$, waiting is **strictly better** than stopping at $(2,2)$:
> $$
> V(2,2)=\frac13 \neq S(2,2)=\frac16.
> $$
>
> The flaw in the naive equality claim is that it implicitly assumes the value at the *next* states already equals the naive stopping formula. That assumption only turns out to be true once one of the colors has been reduced to at most one card; once both colors still have two or more cards left, waiting genuinely creates new opportunities (such as the guaranteed win found at $(0,2)$ above), and it is never worse.
>
> ---
>
> ## Step 3: The optimal stopping rule
>
> Solving the recursion by backward induction confirms the pattern suggested above:
>
> - Whenever **both** colors have at least $2$ cards remaining, continuing (waiting) is strictly better than stopping.
> - As soon as **either** color has been reduced to $1$ or $0$ cards remaining, stopping and naming the majority (or the only remaining) color is optimal, and waiting further can no longer help.
>
> Since each revealed card reduces exactly one color's count by exactly $1$, the count of whichever color is smaller can only decrease one step at a time — so it must pass through the value $1$ before it could ever reach $0$. Consequently, the optimal strategy always stops at the first moment one color's remaining count drops to exactly $1$, never earlier and never later.
>
> ---
>
> ## Step 4: Apply to the initial deck
>
> Initially,
> $$
> (r,b)=(26,26).
> $$
>
> Since both colors have far more than $2$ cards remaining, Step 3 says you should **not** stop immediately; you should keep watching cards until one color's remaining count first falls to $1$, and only then stop and name the other (majority) color.
>
> Solving the recursion by backward induction over all states $0\le r,b\le26$ gives the value of the game from the full deck:
> $$
> V(26,26)=\frac{25}{51}\approx 0.4902.
> $$
>
> As a check on the size of the improvement, recall that stopping immediately and naming either color gives only
> $$
> S(26,26)=\frac{26\cdot25}{52\cdot51}=\frac{25}{102}\approx0.2451,
> $$
> so the optimal strategy of waiting exactly **doubles** the naive one-shot winning probability.
>
> ---
>
> ## Optimal strategy
>
> Keep exposing cards for as long as both colors still have at least $2$ cards remaining. Stop the first time one color's remaining count reaches $1$, and name whichever color is then in the majority.
>
> The probability of winning under this optimal strategy, starting from a full shuffled deck, is
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{25}{51}}.
> $$
