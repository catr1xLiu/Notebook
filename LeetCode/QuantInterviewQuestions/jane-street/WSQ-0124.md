> [!example] Question 124 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Trader Intern Interview

We randomly select 4 numbers from the set of the first 20 prime numbers, without replacement. What is the probability that their sum is even? Explain your reasoning.

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=125)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> To have an even sum of $4$ selected primes, the number of odd selected primes must be even.
>
> Among the first $20$ prime numbers, only one prime is even, namely $2$. The other $19$ primes are odd.
>
> Since we choose $4$ numbers without replacement, the selection can contain either:
>
> - no even prime (that is, all $4$ chosen are odd), or
> - exactly one even prime (the number $2$), since there is only one even prime available.
>
> Now analyze the parity of the sum:
>
> - If all $4$ chosen primes are odd, then the sum of $4$ odd numbers is even.
> - If the set includes $2$ and $3$ odd primes, then the sum is even $+$ odd $+$ odd $+$ odd, which is odd.
>
> So the sum is even **exactly when $2$ is not chosen**.
>
> The total number of ways to choose $4$ primes from $20$ is
>
> $$
> \binom{20}{4}.
> $$
>
> The favorable choices are those taken entirely from the $19$ odd primes:
>
> $$
> \binom{19}{4}.
> $$
>
> Hence the probability is
>
> $$
> \frac{\binom{19}{4}}{\binom{20}{4}}.
> $$
>
> Simplify:
>
> $$
> \frac{\binom{19}{4}}{\binom{20}{4}}
> =
> \frac{\frac{19!}{4!15!}}{\frac{20!}{4!16!}}
> =
> \frac{19!\cdot 16!}{15!\cdot 20!}
> =
> \frac{16}{20}
> =
> \frac{4}{5}.
> $$
>
> Therefore, the probability that the sum is even is
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{4}{5}}.
> $$
>
> The reason is that among the first $20$ primes, only $2$ is even, so the sum is even precisely when $2$ is not selected.
