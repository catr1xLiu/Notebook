> [!example] Question 315 · Jane Street
> ## Trader Interview

What is the sum of the odd positive integers less than 60? What is the probability that n coins have an even number of heads, and how does this probability change if one of the coins is biased?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=316)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## 1. Sum of the odd positive integers less than $60$
>
> The odd positive integers less than $60$ are
>
> $$
> 1, 3, 5, \dots, 59.
> $$
>
> This is an arithmetic sequence with first term $1$, last term $59$, and common difference $2$.
>
> The number of terms is
>
> $$
> n=\frac{59-1}{2}+1=30.
> $$
>
> Hence the sum is
>
> $$
> S=\frac{n}{2}(a_1+a_n)=\frac{30}{2}(1+59)=15\cdot 60=900.
> $$
>
> Therefore, the sum is
>
> $$
> \boxed{900}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Probability that $n$ coins have an even number of heads
>
> Assume first that all $n$ coins are fair and independent.
>
> Let $P_n$ be the probability of getting an even number of heads. Since each of the $2^n$ possible outcomes is equally likely, it suffices to count how many outcomes have an even number of heads.
>
> For every outcome with an even number of heads, flipping the first coin produces an outcome with an odd number of heads, and vice versa. This gives a one-to-one correspondence between outcomes with even and odd numbers of heads. Hence the two counts are equal.
>
> Therefore,
>
> $$
> P_n=\frac{1}{2}.
> $$
>
> So for $n$ fair independent coins,
>
> $$
> \boxed{\Pr(\text{even number of heads})=\frac{1}{2}}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. If one coin is biased
>
> Suppose one coin has probability $p$ of landing heads, while the other $n-1$ coins are fair and all coins are independent.
>
> Let $E$ be the event that the total number of heads is even. Condition on the biased coin:
>
> - If the biased coin is tails, which occurs with probability $1-p$, then the remaining $n-1$ fair coins must have an even number of heads.
> - If the biased coin is heads, which occurs with probability $p$, then the remaining $n-1$ fair coins must have an odd number of heads.
>
> For $n-1 \ge 1$, the remaining fair coins have equal probability of producing an even or odd number of heads, namely $\frac12$. Thus,
>
> $$
> \Pr(E)=(1-p)\cdot \frac12 + p\cdot \frac12=\frac12.
> $$
>
> Hence, as long as there is at least one other fair coin,
>
> $$
> \boxed{\Pr(\text{even number of heads})=\frac12}.
> $$
>
> ### Special case: $n=1$
>
> If there is only one coin and it is biased with head probability $p$, then an even number of heads means $0$ heads, so
>
> $$
> \Pr(\text{even number of heads})=1-p.
> $$
>
> Thus:
>
> - For $n$ fair coins: $\boxed{\frac12}$.
> - If one of the $n$ coins is biased and the other $n-1$ are fair, then for $n\ge 2$ the probability is still $\boxed{\frac12}$.
> - If $n=1$, the probability is $\boxed{1-p}$.
