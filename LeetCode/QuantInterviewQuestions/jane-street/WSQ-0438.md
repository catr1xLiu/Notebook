> [!example] Question 438 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Trader Interview

You and another player each independently sample a number uniformly from \[0,1\] (each number is private information). Both numbers are put into a pot, and you take turns bidding for the pot. What is your optimal bidding strategy?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=439)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let your private value be $x \in [0,1]$, and let the other player’s private value be $y \sim \mathrm{Unif}[0,1]$, independent of $x$. The object being bid on is the pot, whose total value is
>
> $$
> x+y.
> $$
>
> Since your own contribution $x$ is already sunk, the only strategic question is how much you are willing to pay to win the pot rather than lose it.
>
> ## 1. Value of winning versus losing
>
> If you win the auction at price $p$, your payoff is
>
> $$
> (x+y) - p.
> $$
>
> If you lose, you receive $0$ from the auction stage, but your own $x$ is already in the pot and gone either way. Thus, relative to losing, the gain from winning at price $p$ is
>
> $$
> x+y-p.
> $$
>
> Because $y$ is unknown when you bid, conditional on your private signal $x$, your expected value of the pot is
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[x+y \mid x] = x + \mathbb{E}[y] = x + \frac12.
> $$
>
> Therefore your expected surplus from winning at price $p$ is
>
> $$
> x+\frac12-p.
> $$
>
> So the break-even price is
>
> $$
> p^*(x)=x+\frac12.
> $$
>
> ## 2. Optimal bidding principle
>
> At any point in the bidding, if the current price is $p$, then:
>
> - if $p < x+\frac12$, continuing to bid yields positive expected payoff;
> - if $p > x+\frac12$, continuing to bid yields negative expected payoff;
> - if $p = x+\frac12$, you are indifferent.
>
> Hence the optimal strategy is:
>
> $$
> \text{Stay in until the price reaches } x+\frac12,\text{ then stop.}
> $$
>
> Equivalently, your maximum bid is
>
> $$
> b(x)=x+\frac12.
> $$
>
> If bids are capped at the pot’s maximum possible size $2$, this is already within range since $x+\frac12 \in \left[\frac12,\frac32\right]$.
>
> ## 3. Interpretation
>
> This is exactly the same logic as bidding your expected value in an ascending auction:
>
> - your private information is $x$;
> - the unknown part of the pot is the opponent’s contribution $y$;
> - since $y \sim \mathrm{Unif}[0,1]$, its expectation is $\frac12$;
> - so your expected total value is $x+\frac12$.
>
> Thus your strategy is increasing in your own number: higher $x$ means you should bid more aggressively.
>
> ## 4. Final strategy
>
> Your bidding strategy is:
>
> $$
> \boxed{b(x)=x+\frac12.}
> $$
>
> That is, if your private number is $x$, continue bidding up to price $x+\frac12$, and drop out above that price.
