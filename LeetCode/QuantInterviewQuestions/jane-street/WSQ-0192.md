> [!example] Question 192 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Trading Intern Interview

What is the expected value of a fair six-sided die roll, given that after observing the result of your first roll, you can choose whether to keep it or reroll once and must keep the second result?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=193)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let $X$ denote the result of the first roll of a fair six-sided die. After observing $X$, you may either keep it or discard it and take a second roll $Y$, which must then be accepted.
>
> ## Approach
>
> Use optimal stopping: after seeing the first roll $X=x$, compare the value $x$ with the expected value of the second roll. Since the second roll is a fair die roll, its expected value is
>
> $$
> E[Y] = \frac{1+2+3+4+5+6}{6} = \frac{21}{6} = 3.5.
> $$
>
> Thus:
>
> - if $x > 3.5$, keep the first roll;
> - if $x < 3.5$, roll again;
> - if $x = 3.5$, either choice is equivalent, but this cannot occur on a die.
>
> So the optimal rule is:
>
> - reroll on $1,2,3$,
> - keep on $4,5,6$.
>
> ## Compute the expected value under the optimal strategy
>
> Let $V$ be the payoff under this strategy. Then
>
> $$
> E[V]
> = \frac{1}{6}\bigl(E[Y] + E[Y] + E[Y] + 4 + 5 + 6\bigr).
> $$
>
> Since $E[Y] = 3.5$,
>
> $$
> E[V]
> = \frac{1}{6}\bigl(3.5 + 3.5 + 3.5 + 4 + 5 + 6\bigr)
> = \frac{1}{6}(25.5)
> = 4.25.
> $$
>
> Equivalently,
>
> $$
> E[V]
> = \frac{1}{6}\left(3 \cdot \frac{7}{2} + 4 + 5 + 6\right)
> = \frac{1}{6}\left(\frac{21}{2} + 15\right)
> = \frac{1}{6}\cdot \frac{51}{2}
> = \frac{17}{4}.
> $$
>
> ## Final answer
>
> The optimal strategy is to reroll if the first roll is $1,2,$ or $3$, and keep it if it is $4,5,$ or $6$. The expected value is
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{17}{4} = 4.25}.
> $$
