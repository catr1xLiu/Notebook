> [!example] Question 7 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

You are given a biased coin with a known probability p of landing heads. How would you determine the expected number of flips until it shows heads twice in a row?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=8)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let $T$ be the number of flips needed to obtain two consecutive heads.
>
> ## Approach
>
> Model the process by tracking the relevant “state” after each flip:
>
> - **State $S_0$**: no previous head is currently available to start a run of two heads.
> - **State $S_1$**: the most recent flip was a head, so one more head would complete the goal.
>
> Let
>
> - $E_0$ be the expected additional number of flips needed starting from state $S_0$,
> - $E_1$ be the expected additional number of flips needed starting from state $S_1$.
>
> We seek $E_0$, since initially no flips have occurred.
>
> ## Step 1: Set up equations
>
> From state $S_0$:
>
> - On the next flip, with probability $p$, a head occurs and the process moves to state $S_1$.
> - With probability $1-p$, a tail occurs and the process remains in state $S_0$.
>
> Thus,
> $$
> E_0 = 1 + pE_1 + (1-p)E_0.
> $$
>
> From state $S_1$:
>
> - With probability $p$, the next flip is head, so the process ends.
> - With probability $1-p$, the next flip is tail, which breaks the streak and returns to state $S_0$.
>
> Thus,
> $$
> E_1 = 1 + p\cdot 0 + (1-p)E_0
> = 1 + (1-p)E_0.
> $$
>
> ## Step 2: Solve the system
>
> From
> $$
> E_0 = 1 + pE_1 + (1-p)E_0,
> $$
> move the $(1-p)E_0$ term to the left:
> $$
> pE_0 = 1 + pE_1.
> $$
> So
> $$
> E_0 = \frac{1}{p} + E_1.
> $$
>
> Now substitute
> $$
> E_1 = 1 + (1-p)E_0
> $$
> into this:
> $$
> E_0 = \frac{1}{p} + 1 + (1-p)E_0.
> $$
>
> Bring the $(1-p)E_0$ term to the left:
> $$
> E_0 - (1-p)E_0 = \frac{1}{p} + 1.
> $$
> Hence
> $$
> pE_0 = \frac{1}{p} + 1.
> $$
>
> Therefore,
> $$
> E_0 = \frac{1/p + 1}{p}
> = \frac{1+p}{p^2}.
> $$
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> The expected number of flips until the first occurrence of two consecutive heads is
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{1+p}{p^2}}.
> $$
>
> As a check, for a fair coin with $p=\tfrac12$,
> $$
> \frac{1+\tfrac12}{(\tfrac12)^2}
> = \frac{3/2}{1/4}
> = 6,
> $$
> which is the standard result.
