> [!example] Question 242 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Analyst Interview

Suppose two players play a game where Player A and then Player B each pick an integer between 1 and 30. Then, a 30-sided die is rolled. Whoever guessed closer to the value of the roll wins an amount of money equal to the value of the roll from the other player. Given the choice, should you go first or second? What number should you choose? What is the expected value of your position?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=243)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> Let the die roll be $X \in \{1,2,\dots,30\}$, uniformly distributed.  
> Player A chooses an integer $a$, then Player B chooses an integer $b$.  
> If $|X-a|<|X-b|$, then A wins $X$ dollars from B; if $|X-b|<|X-a|$, then A loses $X$ dollars; if $|X-a|=|X-b|$, the natural interpretation is a tie, so no money changes hands.
>
> We determine whether it is better to move first or second, what number to choose, and the expected value under optimal play.
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. Payoff for fixed choices
>
> Fix distinct guesses $a$ and $b$.
>
> Because closeness depends only on the midpoint between $a$ and $b$, the set of die outcomes won by each player is easy to describe.
>
> Assume first that $a<b$. Then:
>
> - A wins when $X<\dfrac{a+b}{2}$,
> - B wins when $X>\dfrac{a+b}{2}$,
> - if $a+b$ is even, then $X=\dfrac{a+b}{2}$ is a tie.
>
> Since the amount transferred is $X$, A’s expected payoff is
>
> $$
> \frac1{30}\left(\sum_{\substack{x=1\\ |x-a|<|x-b|}}^{30} x
> -
> \sum_{\substack{x=1\\ |x-b|<|x-a|}}^{30} x\right).
> $$
>
> So the game is not merely about winning more outcomes; larger values of $X$ are more valuable. Hence guesses near the high end are favored.
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Best response of the second player
>
> Suppose Player A chooses $a$. Player B then chooses $b$ to maximize B’s expected gain, equivalently to minimize A’s expected gain.
>
> Because the payoff weight is increasing in $X$, the second player wants to place the dividing point so that the larger die values fall on B’s side. This strongly suggests choosing a number just above A’s number.
>
> We compute this exactly.
>
> ---
>
> ### Case 1: $b=a+1$
>
> Then for every $x\le a$, A is closer, and for every $x\ge a+1$, B is closer. There is no tie, since the midpoint is a half-integer.
>
> Thus A’s expected payoff is
>
> $$
> \frac1{30}\left(\sum_{x=1}^{a} x-\sum_{x=a+1}^{30} x\right).
> $$
>
> Using
>
> $$
> \sum_{x=1}^{n} x=\frac{n(n+1)}{2},
> \qquad
> \sum_{x=1}^{30}x=465,
> $$
>
> this becomes
>
> $$
> \frac1{30}\left(\frac{a(a+1)}{2}-\left(465-\frac{a(a+1)}{2}\right)\right)
> =
> \frac{a(a+1)-465}{30}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### Case 2: $b=a-1$
>
> Then B gets the lower values and A gets the higher values, so A’s payoff is
>
> $$
> \frac1{30}\left(\sum_{x=a}^{30}x-\sum_{x=1}^{a-1}x\right)
> =
> \frac1{30}\left(\left(465-\frac{(a-1)a}{2}\right)-\frac{(a-1)a}{2}\right)
> =
> \frac{465-a(a-1)}{30}.
> $$
>
> This is positive and typically large, so B would never choose $a-1$.
>
> Thus, among adjacent choices, the best response is to choose $a+1$ whenever possible.
>
> ---
>
> ### Why choosing farther above is worse for B
>
> If $b>a+1$, then the midpoint moves upward, so A wins more low and medium values, while B gives up some outcomes without gaining enough compensation, because the outcomes newly transferred to B are not all the highest ones. In a weighted one-dimensional location game with increasing weights, the best reply is the nearest point on the advantageous side, namely $a+1$.
>
> So if $a<30$, B’s best response is $b=a+1$.
>
> If $a=30$, B cannot choose $31$, so B chooses $29$, giving A all outcomes $30$ and B all outcomes $1$ through $29$. Then A’s expected payoff is
>
> $$
> \frac1{30}\left(30-\sum_{x=1}^{29}x\right)
> =
> \frac1{30}(30-435)
> =
> -\frac{405}{30}
> =
> -13.5.
> $$
>
> This is very bad for A, so choosing $30$ first is not optimal.
>
> Hence if A goes first and chooses $a\le 29$, the value to A is
>
> $$
> V(a)=\frac{a(a+1)-465}{30},
> $$
>
> since B responds with $a+1$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Optimal first move
>
> We maximize
>
> $$
> V(a)=\frac{a(a+1)-465}{30}
> $$
>
> over $a=1,2,\dots,29$.
>
> Since $a(a+1)$ is increasing in $a$, $V(a)$ is increasing in $a$. Therefore the best first move is $a=29$.
>
> Then B chooses $30$, and A’s expected payoff is
>
> $$
> V(29)=\frac{29\cdot 30-465}{30}
> =\frac{870-465}{30}
> =\frac{405}{30}
> =13.5.
> $$
>
> So the player who moves first can guarantee an expected profit of $13.5$ dollars by choosing $29$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. Should one go first or second?
>
> If you go first, you can choose $29$ and secure expected value
>
> $$
> 13.5.
> $$
>
> If instead you go second, the first player can choose $29$, and your best response is $30$, giving the first player expected value $13.5$ and you expected value
>
> $$
> -13.5.
> $$
>
> Therefore it is strictly better to go first.
>
> ---
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> The optimal decision is:
>
> - **Go first**.
> - **Choose $29$**.
>
> Then the opponent’s best response is $30$, and your expected payoff is
>
> $$
> \frac1{30}\left(\sum_{x=1}^{29}x-30\right)
> =
> \frac{435-30}{30}
> =
> \frac{405}{30}
> =
> 13.5.
> $$
>
> Thus the expected value of the position is
>
> $$
> \boxed{13.5}
> $$
>
> dollars in favor of the first player.
