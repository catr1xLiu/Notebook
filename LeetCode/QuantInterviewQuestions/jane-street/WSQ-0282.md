> [!example] Question 282 · Jane Street
> ## Quant Trading Intern Interview

For an odd number of fair coin flips, what is the probability of getting an even number of heads? What about for an even number of flips? How does this probability change if some of the coins are unfair?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=283)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let $X$ be the number of heads in $n$ independent coin flips.
>
> We seek
> $$
> P(X \text{ is even}).
> $$
>
> ## 1. Fair coins
>
> If the coins are fair, then $X \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n,\tfrac12)$, so
> $$
> P(X \text{ is even})
> = \sum_{\substack{k=0\\ k\text{ even}}}^{n} \binom{n}{k}\left(\frac12\right)^n.
> $$
>
> A standard identity gives
> $$
> \sum_{\substack{k=0\\ k\text{ even}}}^{n} \binom{n}{k}
> =
> \sum_{\substack{k=0\\ k\text{ odd}}}^{n} \binom{n}{k}
> =
> 2^{n-1}
> \quad \text{for } n\ge 1.
> $$
> Hence
> $$
> P(X \text{ is even})=\frac{2^{n-1}}{2^n}=\frac12.
> $$
>
> Therefore:
>
> - for an odd number of fair flips,
>   $$
>   P(\text{even number of heads})=\frac12;
>   $$
> - for an even number of fair flips,
>   $$
>   P(\text{even number of heads})=\frac12.
>   $$
>
> So for any positive integer $n$,
> $$
> P(X \text{ is even})=\frac12.
> $$
>
> (If $n=0$, then $X=0$ is even, so the probability is $1$.)
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Unfair coins
>
> Now suppose the flips are independent, but the $i$th coin has
> $$
> P(\text{head on flip } i)=p_i.
> $$
> The coins need not have the same bias.
>
> Let
> $$
> E_n = P(X \text{ is even}), \qquad O_n = P(X \text{ is odd}).
> $$
> Then
> $$
> E_n - O_n = \mathbb{E}[(-1)^X].
> $$
> Since $X=\sum_{i=1}^n I_i$, where $I_i$ is the indicator of a head on flip $i$,
> $$
> (-1)^X = \prod_{i=1}^n (-1)^{I_i}.
> $$
> By independence,
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[(-1)^X]
> =
> \prod_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}[(-1)^{I_i}].
> $$
> For each $i$,
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[(-1)^{I_i}]
> = (1-p_i)(1) + p_i(-1)
> = 1-2p_i.
> $$
> Thus
> $$
> E_n - O_n = \prod_{i=1}^n (1-2p_i).
> $$
>
> Also,
> $$
> E_n + O_n = 1.
> $$
> Solving these two equations,
> $$
> E_n = \frac{1 + \prod_{i=1}^n (1-2p_i)}{2},
> $$
> and
> $$
> O_n = \frac{1 - \prod_{i=1}^n (1-2p_i)}{2}.
> $$
>
> Hence, for independent possibly unfair coins,
> $$
> P(\text{even number of heads})
> =
> \frac{1 + \prod_{i=1}^n (1-2p_i)}{2}.
> $$
>
> ### Special case: all coins have the same bias
>
> If each flip has head probability $p$, then
> $$
> P(\text{even number of heads})
> =
> \frac{1+(1-2p)^n}{2}.
> $$
>
> For $p=\tfrac12$, this becomes
> $$
> \frac{1+0^n}{2}=\frac12
> \qquad (n\ge 1),
> $$
> recovering the fair-coin result.
>
> ## Final answers
>
> For $n\ge 1$ fair coin flips, regardless of whether $n$ is odd or even,
> $$
> P(\text{even number of heads})=\frac12.
> $$
>
> For independent unfair coins with head probabilities $p_1,\dots,p_n$,
> $$
> P(\text{even number of heads})
> =
> \frac{1 + \prod_{i=1}^n (1-2p_i)}{2}.
> $$
>
> In particular, if all flips have the same bias $p$, then
> $$
> P(\text{even number of heads})
> =
> \frac{1+(1-2p)^n}{2}.
> $$
