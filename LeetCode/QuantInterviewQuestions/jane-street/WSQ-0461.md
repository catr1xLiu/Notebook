> ## Quantitative Trader Interview

Q1: What is the smallest number whose digits multiply to 216? What about 10,000? Q2: Calculate the probability of getting 3 heads in 4 coin flips. What is the probability of getting an odd number of heads in 4 flips? What about in 9 flips? In N flips? Q3: What is the next date whose digits are all unique? Q4: After three coin flips, heads-tails-heads and heads-heads-tails are equally probable. But if I keep flipping, one combination becomes more probable than the other. Why is that?

> ## Q1
>
> To make the smallest number whose digits multiply to a given integer, one should use as few digits as possible, and among factorizations into digits $2$ through $9$ with that minimum digit count, arrange the digits in increasing order.
>
> ### Product 216
>
> Since the largest possible product of two digits is $9\times9=81<216$, at least three digits are needed.
>
> Looking for all ways to write $216$ as a product of exactly three digits from $1$ to $9$:
> $$
> 216=3\times8\times9=4\times6\times9=6\times6\times6.
> $$
> Arranging each factorization in ascending order gives the candidate numbers $389$, $469$, and $666$. The smallest of these is
> $$
> \boxed{389}.
> $$
>
> ### Product 10,000
>
> Factor:
> $$
> 10{,}000 = 2^4 \cdot 5^4.
> $$
>
> No digit from $1$ to $9$ other than $5$ itself is divisible by $5$, and no digit can combine a factor of $5$ with anything else (that would make it a multiple of $10$). So the four factors of $5$ must come from four separate digit-$5$'s. The remaining factor $2^4=16$ must be built from digits in $\{2,4,8\}$ (representing $2^1,2^2,2^3$) using as few digits as possible: splitting the exponent $4$ as $3+1$ (digits $8,2$) or $2+2$ (digits $4,4$) both take two digits, and no single digit reaches $2^4$. So the minimum total digit count is $4+2=6$.
>
> The two candidate digit multisets are $\{2,5,5,5,5,8\}$ and $\{4,4,5,5,5,5\}$, giving the ascending numbers $255558$ and $445555$. The smaller is
> $$
> \boxed{255558}.
> $$

> ## Q2
>
> ### Probability of exactly 3 heads in 4 flips
>
> There are
> $$
> 2^4 = 16
> $$
> equally likely outcomes.
>
> The number of ways to get exactly $3$ heads is
> $$
> \binom{4}{3} = 4.
> $$
>
> Therefore,
> $$
> P(\text{exactly }3\text{ heads in }4\text{ flips})=\frac{\binom{4}{3}}{2^4}=\frac{4}{16}=\boxed{\frac14}.
> $$
>
> ### Probability of an odd number of heads in 4 flips
>
> An odd number of heads means $1$ or $3$ heads. Thus
> $$
> P(\text{odd heads in }4\text{ flips})
> = \frac{\binom{4}{1}+\binom{4}{3}}{2^4}
> = \frac{4+4}{16}
> = \frac{8}{16}
> = \boxed{\frac12}.
> $$
>
> ### Probability of an odd number of heads in 9 flips
>
> Similarly,
> $$
> P(\text{odd heads in }9\text{ flips})
> = \frac{\binom{9}{1}+\binom{9}{3}+\binom{9}{5}+\binom{9}{7}+\binom{9}{9}}{2^9}.
> $$
>
> The sum of the odd binomial coefficients equals half of the total:
> $$
> \sum_{k \text{ odd}} \binom{9}{k} = 2^{8}.
> $$
>
> So
> $$
> P(\text{odd heads in }9\text{ flips})=\frac{2^8}{2^9}=\boxed{\frac12}.
> $$
>
> ### Probability of an odd number of heads in N flips
>
> For any $N \ge 1$,
> $$
> \sum_{k=0}^N \binom{N}{k} = 2^N,
> $$
> and
> $$
> \sum_{k \text{ even}} \binom{N}{k}
> =
> \sum_{k \text{ odd}} \binom{N}{k}
> =
> 2^{N-1}.
> $$
>
> Therefore,
> $$
> P(\text{odd number of heads in }N\text{ flips})
> =
> \frac{2^{N-1}}{2^N}
> =
> \boxed{\frac12}.
> $$
>
> So the general answer is:
> $$
> \boxed{P(\text{odd number of heads in }N\text{ flips})=\frac12 \quad \text{for all } N\ge 1.}
> $$

> ## Q3
>
> Interpret the date in the numeric form $MMDDYYYY$ (month, day, year, run together) and seek the next date, from today onward, whose eight digits are all different from one another.
>
> Today is May 1, 2026, written $05012026$; this already repeats the digit $2$ (and the digit $0$), so it does not qualify.
>
> Two structural obstacles make this much harder than it first looks:
>
> - Every valid month, $01$ through $12$, begins with $0$ or $1$. So if the year's digits already include **both** $0$ and $1$, no month at all is available, and the year is immediately ruled out.
> - Years from $2026$ through $2999$ all begin with the digit $2$, so that digit is already used by the year and cannot appear again in the month or day. This rules out day values like $2$, $12$, and $20$ through $29$, and month values like $02$ and $12$, for every year in this range.
>
> Combined with the requirement that the year's own four digits be pairwise distinct, these constraints eliminate every year for a very long stretch. Checking systematically, the first year whose digits leave enough room for a valid, fully non-repeating month and day is $2345$ (digits $2,3,4,5$, which avoids both $0$ and $1$ entirely). Within that year, the earliest valid date is June $17$:
> $$
> \text{month }06,\ \text{day }17,\ \text{year }2345\ \Longrightarrow\ 06172345,
> $$
> whose eight digits are $0,6,1,7,2,3,4,5$ — the digits $0$ through $7$, each appearing exactly once.
>
> So the next date with all distinct digits is
> $$
> \boxed{\text{June }17,\ 2345}\qquad(06/17/2345).
> $$

> ## Q4
>
> The key distinction is between:
>
> 1. the probability of seeing a particular $3$-flip sequence in exactly the first $3$ flips, and
> 2. the probability that one sequence appears before the other in an ongoing stream of flips.
>
> For exactly $3$ flips, the sequences $HTH$ and $HHT$ are each just one of the $2^3=8$ possible outcomes, so they are equally likely:
> $$
> P(HTH)=P(HHT)=\frac18.
> $$
>
> However, when flipping continues until one of these patterns appears, the patterns overlap with themselves and with partial progress differently.
>
> ### Why the difference occurs
>
> - After seeing $HH$, one more $T$ completes $HHT$ immediately.
> - But after seeing $HT$, one more $H$ completes $HTH$.
>
> The crucial point is what happens after a mismatch:
>
> - If one is building toward $HHT$ and flips another $H$, the suffix $HH$ is still useful; progress is retained.
> - If one is building toward $HTH$ and the wrong continuation occurs, progress is often lost.
>
> Thus $HHT$ has a structural advantage in the race to appear first.
>
> ### A quick state analysis
>
> Let the states record the relevant suffix:
>
> - $S$: no useful suffix,
> - $H$: last flip is $H$,
> - $HH$: last two flips are $HH$,
> - $HT$: last two flips are $HT$.
>
> Let $p_S, p_H, p_{HH}, p_{HT}$ be the probabilities that $HHT$ appears before $HTH$ starting from those states.
>
> Then:
> $$
> p_S = \frac12 p_H + \frac12 p_S,
> $$
> so
> $$
> p_S = p_H.
> $$
>
> From state $H$:
> $$
> p_H = \frac12 p_{HH} + \frac12 p_{HT}.
> $$
>
> From state $HH$:
>
> - flip $T$ and $HHT$ occurs first,
> - flip $H$ and we remain in state $HH$.
>
> Hence
> $$
> p_{HH} = \frac12 \cdot 1 + \frac12 p_{HH},
> $$
> so
> $$
> p_{HH}=1.
> $$
>
> From state $HT$:
>
> - flip $H$ and $HTH$ occurs first, so probability $0$ for $HHT$,
> - flip $T$ gives suffix $T$, which is equivalent to state $S$.
>
> Thus
> $$
> p_{HT} = \frac12 \cdot 0 + \frac12 p_S = \frac12 p_S.
> $$
>
> Now substitute:
> $$
> p_H = \frac12(1) + \frac12\left(\frac12 p_S\right)
> = \frac12 + \frac14 p_S.
> $$
>
> Since $p_S=p_H$,
> $$
> p_S = \frac12 + \frac14 p_S.
> $$
>
> Therefore,
> $$
> \frac34 p_S = \frac12,
> \qquad
> p_S = \frac{2}{3}.
> $$
>
> So
> $$
> P(HHT \text{ appears before } HTH)=\boxed{\frac23},
> $$
> and therefore
> $$
> P(HTH \text{ appears before } HHT)=\boxed{\frac13}.
> $$
>
> Thus the two patterns are equally likely in a fixed block of $3$ flips, but not equally likely in a race to appear first, because their overlap structures differ.
