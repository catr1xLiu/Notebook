Walk me through the solution to the birthday problem (i.e., calculate the probability that at least two people in a group share the same birthday).

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=252)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## The birthday problem
>
> The classical birthday problem asks:
>
> In a group of $n$ people, what is the probability that **at least two people share the same birthday**?
>
> The standard assumptions are:
>
> - There are $365$ possible birthdays.
> - Each birthday is equally likely.
> - Birthdays are independent.
> - Leap day is ignored.
>
> ---
>
> ## Step 1: Use the complement
>
> It is much easier to compute the probability that **no two people share a birthday**, and then subtract from $1$.
>
> Let
>
> - $A =$ “at least two people share a birthday,”
> - $A^c =$ “all $n$ birthdays are different.”
>
> Then
>
> $$
> P(A) = 1 - P(A^c).
> $$
>
> So the main task is to find $P(A^c)$.
>
> ---
>
> ## Step 2: Compute the probability that all birthdays are different
>
> Assign birthdays one person at a time.
>
> - The first person can have any birthday, so the probability is $1$.
> - The second person must avoid the first person’s birthday, so the probability is $\frac{364}{365}$.
> - The third person must avoid the first two birthdays, so the probability is $\frac{363}{365}$.
> - Continuing in this way, the $k$th person must avoid the previous $k-1$ birthdays, so the probability is
>
> $$
> \frac{365-(k-1)}{365}.
> $$
>
> Thus,
>
> $$
> P(A^c)
> =
> \frac{365}{365}\cdot\frac{364}{365}\cdot\frac{363}{365}\cdots\frac{365-n+1}{365}.
> $$
>
> Equivalently,
>
> $$
> P(A^c)
> =
> \prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{365-k}{365}.
> $$
>
> Therefore the probability of at least one shared birthday is
>
> $$
> P(A)
> =
> 1-\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{365-k}{365}.
> $$
>
> This is the exact formula.
>
> ---
>
> ## Step 3: Example with $n=23$
>
> The famous case is $n=23$.
>
> Then
>
> $$
> P(\text{no match})
> =
> \frac{365}{365}\cdot\frac{364}{365}\cdot\frac{363}{365}\cdots\frac{343}{365},
> $$
>
> so
>
> $$
> P(\text{at least one match})
> =
> 1-
> \frac{365}{365}\cdot\frac{364}{365}\cdot\frac{363}{365}\cdots\frac{343}{365}.
> $$
>
> Numerically,
>
> $$
> P(\text{no match}) \approx 0.4927,
> $$
>
> and hence
>
> $$
> P(\text{at least one match}) \approx 1 - 0.4927 = 0.5073.
> $$
>
> So with just $23$ people, the probability of a shared birthday is about
>
> $$
> 50.7\%.
> $$
>
> This is why the result feels surprising.
>
> ---
>
> ## Step 4: Why the probability grows so quickly
>
> The key point is that we are not comparing one person to everyone else only once.  
> Among $n$ people, there are many possible pairs.
>
> The number of pairs is
>
> $$
> \binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}.
> $$
>
> For $n=23$,
>
> $$
> \binom{23}{2} = \frac{23\cdot 22}{2} = 253.
> $$
>
> So there are $253$ different pairs of people who could potentially match, which makes a shared birthday much more likely than intuition suggests.
>
> ---
>
> ## Step 5: Useful approximation
>
> For moderate $n$, one often uses the approximation
>
> $$
> P(\text{no match})
> \approx
> e^{-n(n-1)/(2\cdot 365)}.
> $$
>
> Therefore,
>
> $$
> P(\text{at least one match})
> \approx
> 1 - e^{-n(n-1)/(2\cdot 365)}.
> $$
>
> For $n=23$,
>
> $$
> \frac{23\cdot 22}{2\cdot 365} = \frac{253}{365} \approx 0.693,
> $$
>
> so
>
> $$
> P(\text{at least one match})
> \approx
> 1 - e^{-0.693}
> \approx
> 1 - 0.500
> =
> 0.500.
> $$
>
> This is very close to the exact value $0.5073$.
>
> ---
>
> ## Final answer
>
> For $n$ people, the probability that **at least two share a birthday** is
>
> $$
> \boxed{
> 1-\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{365-k}{365}
> }
> $$
>
> under the usual assumptions of $365$ equally likely birthdays.
>
> In particular, for $n=23$,
>
> $$
> \boxed{
> P(\text{at least one shared birthday}) \approx 0.5073
> }
> $$
>
> so a group of $23$ people already gives a probability just over $50\%$.
