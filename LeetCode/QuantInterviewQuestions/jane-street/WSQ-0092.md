> [!example] Question 92 · Jane Street
> ## Trading Interview

If you have 100 coins, at least one fair and at least one unfair, is the probability of getting an even number of heads sometimes 1/2, always 1/2, or never 1/2?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=93)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let the $i$th coin show heads with probability $p_i$, where at least one coin is fair, so for some $i$ we have $p_i=\tfrac12$, and at least one coin is not fair, so for some $j$ we have $p_j\neq \tfrac12$.
>
> We want the probability that the total number of heads among the $100$ coins is even.
>
> ## Key fact
>
> For independent Bernoulli trials, the probability of an even number of successes is
>
> $$
> P(\text{even})=\frac12\left(1+\prod_{i=1}^{100}(1-2p_i)\right).
> $$
>
> This can be derived from the identity
>
> $$
> E[(-1)^X]=P(X \text{ even})-P(X \text{ odd}),
> $$
>
> where $X$ is the total number of heads. Since $X=\sum_{i=1}^{100} X_i$ with independent $X_i$,
>
> $$
> E[(-1)^X]
> =
> \prod_{i=1}^{100} E[(-1)^{X_i}].
> $$
>
> For each coin,
>
> $$
> E[(-1)^{X_i}]
> =
> (1-p_i)(1)+p_i(-1)
> =
> 1-2p_i.
> $$
>
> Hence
>
> $$
> P(\text{even})-P(\text{odd})
> =
> \prod_{i=1}^{100}(1-2p_i).
> $$
>
> Also,
>
> $$
> P(\text{even})+P(\text{odd})=1.
> $$
>
> Solving these two equations gives
>
> $$
> P(\text{even})=\frac12\left(1+\prod_{i=1}^{100}(1-2p_i)\right).
> $$
>
> ## Apply the condition that at least one coin is fair
>
> If one coin is fair, say $p_k=\tfrac12$, then
>
> $$
> 1-2p_k = 1-2\cdot \frac12 = 0.
> $$
>
> Therefore the product satisfies
>
> $$
> \prod_{i=1}^{100}(1-2p_i)=0.
> $$
>
> So
>
> $$
> P(\text{even})=\frac12(1+0)=\frac12.
> $$
>
> This conclusion does not depend on how the other $99$ coins are biased.
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> The chance of getting an even number of heads is **always** 
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac12}.
> $$
