> [!example] Question 383 · Jane Street
> ## Trader Interview

You roll a 30-sided die (numbered 1 to 30) repeatedly and sum all the outcomes. You stop rolling when the sum is greater than or equal to 300. What is the most likely final value of the sum?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=384)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let $S_n = X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n$, where the $X_i$ are independent and uniformly distributed on $\{1,2,\dots,30\}$. Define the stopping time
> $$
> T=\min\{n:S_n\ge 300\}.
> $$
> We want the most likely value of the final sum
> $$
> S_T.
> $$
>
> ## 1. Reformulation
>
> The process stops at total $s$ exactly when:
>
> - for some previous total $t<300$, a roll carries the sum to $s\ge 300$;
> - equivalently, just before the last roll the sum was some $t$ with
> $$
> 300-30 \le t \le 299,
> $$
> and the final roll was $s-t$.
>
> Thus the distribution of $S_T$ is determined by the probabilities of landing at the various totals just below $300$.
>
> ## 2. A recursion
>
> Let $p(n)$ be the probability that the running total ever hits exactly $n$ before stopping. Then:
>
> - $p(0)=1$,
> - $p(n)=0$ for $n<0$,
> - for $n\ge 1$,
> $$
> p(n)=\frac1{30}\sum_{k=1}^{30} p(n-k).
> $$
>
> This is because to hit $n$, the previous total must be one of $n-1,n-2,\dots,n-30$, and each corresponding roll occurs with probability $\frac1{30}$.
>
> Write $s=300+j$ for $j=0,1,\dots,29$. The stopping total is $s$ exactly when the previous total was $s-k<300$ for some roll $k\in\{1,\dots,30\}$. The condition $s-k<300$ means $k>j$, so only the $30-j$ rolls $k=j+1,\dots,30$ can produce a landing at exactly $s$. Hence
> $$
> \Pr(S_T=300+j)=\frac1{30}\sum_{k=j+1}^{30} p(300+j-k),
> $$
> a sum of $30-j$ terms, each a value of $p(\cdot)$ just below $300$.
>
> ## 3. Steady-state behavior
>
> For large $n$, the recursion
> $$
> p(n)=\frac1{30}\sum_{k=1}^{30} p(n-k)
> $$
> approaches a constant, by renewal theory: for a positive-integer-valued step distribution with mean $\mu$,
> $$
> p(n)\to \frac1{\mu}.
> $$
> Here
> $$
> \mu=\frac{1+30}{2}=15.5=\frac{31}{2},
> $$
> so
> $$
> p(n)\approx \frac1{15.5}=\frac{2}{31}
> $$
> for all $n$ close to (and below) $300$, since $300$ is large relative to the step size $30$.
>
> Substituting this constant into the formula from Step 2, the sum has $30-j$ terms, each approximately $\frac{2}{31}$, so
> $$
> \Pr(S_T=300+j)\approx \frac{30-j}{30}\cdot\frac{2}{31}=\frac{2(30-j)}{930}.
> $$
>
> This is not uniform: it is a decreasing (triangular) function of $j$, largest at $j=0$ and smallest at $j=29$.
>
> As a check, these probabilities sum to $1$:
> $$
> \sum_{j=0}^{29}\frac{2(30-j)}{930}=\frac{2}{930}\sum_{k=1}^{30}k=\frac{2}{930}\cdot 465=\frac{930}{930}=1.
> $$
>
> ## 4. Determining the most likely total
>
> Since $\Pr(S_T=300+j)=\dfrac{2(30-j)}{930}$ is strictly decreasing in $j$, the maximum occurs uniquely at $j=0$, that is, at
> $$
> S_T=300.
> $$
>
> The corresponding probability is
> $$
> \Pr(S_T=300)=\frac{2(30-0)}{930}=\frac{60}{930}=\frac{2}{31}\approx 0.0645.
> $$
>
> ## Final answer
>
> The distribution of the final sum is triangular, decreasing from $j=0$ to $j=29$:
> $$
> \Pr(S_T=300+j)=\frac{2(30-j)}{930},\qquad j=0,1,\dots,29.
> $$
>
> The unique most likely final value is
> $$
> \boxed{S_T=300},
> $$
> occurring with probability
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{2}{31}\approx 6.45\%}.
> $$
