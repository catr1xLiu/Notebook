> [!example] Question 410 · Jane Street
> ## Trader Interview

Player A had a better batting average than Player B in both the first and second halves of the season. Is it possible for Player B to have a better average than Player A over the entire season?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=411)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Yes. This is possible because the overall batting average is a **weighted average** of the averages in the two halves, and the weights are the numbers of at-bats in each half. If the players have very different numbers of at-bats in the two halves, the combined averages can reverse the comparison. This is an instance of **Simpson’s paradox**.
>
> Let Player A have averages
> $$
> a_1 > b_1
> \quad\text{and}\quad
> a_2 > b_2
> $$
> in the first and second halves, respectively.  
> Their overall averages are
> $$
> \frac{H_{A1}+H_{A2}}{N_{A1}+N_{A2}}
> \quad\text{and}\quad
> \frac{H_{B1}+H_{B2}}{N_{B1}+N_{B2}},
> $$
> where $H$ denotes hits and $N$ denotes at-bats.
>
> Because these are weighted averages,
> $$
> \frac{H_{A1}+H_{A2}}{N_{A1}+N_{A2}}
> =
> \frac{N_{A1}a_1+N_{A2}a_2}{N_{A1}+N_{A2}},
> $$
> and similarly for Player B. Different weights can make Player B’s overall average larger even though $b_1<a_1$ and $b_2<a_2$.
>
> A concrete example:
>
> - First half:
>   - Player A: $3$ hits in $10$ at-bats, so average $= \frac{3}{10} = .300$
>   - Player B: $25$ hits in $100$ at-bats, so average $= \frac{25}{100} = .250$
>
> - Second half:
>   - Player A: $28$ hits in $100$ at-bats, so average $= \frac{28}{100} = .280$
>   - Player B: $2$ hits in $10$ at-bats, so average $= \frac{2}{10} = .200$
>
> Thus, in each half,
> $$
> .300 > .250
> \quad\text{and}\quad
> .280 > .200.
> $$
>
> Now compute the season averages:
>
> - Player A:
> $$
> \frac{3+28}{10+100}=\frac{31}{110}\approx .282
> $$
>
> - Player B:
> $$
> \frac{25+2}{100+10}=\frac{27}{110}\approx .245
> $$
>
> This example does not reverse the totals, so adjust the at-bats more strongly:
>
> - First half:
>   - Player A: $1$ hit in $2$ at-bats, average $= .500$
>   - Player B: $48$ hits in $100$ at-bats, average $= .480$
>
> - Second half:
>   - Player A: $25$ hits in $100$ at-bats, average $= .250$
>   - Player B: $1$ hit in $5$ at-bats, average $= .200$
>
> Again,
> $$
> .500 > .480
> \quad\text{and}\quad
> .250 > .200.
> $$
>
> Overall:
>
> - Player A:
> $$
> \frac{1+25}{2+100}=\frac{26}{102}\approx .255
> $$
>
> - Player B:
> $$
> \frac{48+1}{100+5}=\frac{49}{105}\approx .467
> $$
>
> So Player B has the better overall average, even though Player A had the better average in each half.
>
> Therefore, **yes, it is possible**.
