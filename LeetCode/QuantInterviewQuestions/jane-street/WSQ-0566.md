> [!example] Question 566 · Jane Street
> ## Assistant Trader Interview

You have a dartboard that is split in half. If you hit the left half, you get 2 points; if you hit the right half, you get 3 points. You have an 80% chance of hitting the dartboard on any given throw and, if you hit the board, a 70% chance of hitting the side you are aiming for (and a 30% chance of hitting the other side). With an unlimited number of throws, what is the probability that you achieve a score of exactly 7?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=567)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> On a single throw the possible outcomes are:
>
> - $0$ points, if the dart misses the board,
> - $2$ points, if it hits the left half,
> - $3$ points, if it hits the right half.
>
> Since there is an unlimited number of throws, the aiming side may be chosen adaptively after seeing the running total. On any throw:
>
> - the probability of missing is $0.2$,
> - if aiming at the left half, $P(2)=0.8\times 0.7=0.56$ and $P(3)=0.8\times 0.3=0.24$,
> - if aiming at the right half, $P(3)=0.56$ and $P(2)=0.24$.
>
> ### Overshoot is possible
>
> The running total only ever increases, and it increases by $2$ or $3$ on each successful throw. This means the total can jump straight past $7$: for instance, from a total of $6$, a throw of either $2$ or $3$ produces $8$ or $9$, both beyond $7$, and the total can never come back down. So the game is not guaranteed to reach exactly $7$; the probability of doing so depends on steering the running total through the right intermediate values while avoiding an overshoot.
>
> ### Setting up the recursion
>
> Let $f(s)$ be the probability of eventually landing on a total of exactly $7$, starting from a current total of $s$, under optimal choice of aim on each subsequent throw. A miss simply repeats the same state and does not affect which aim is best, so
>
> $$
> f(s)=\max\Bigl(0.7f(s+2)+0.3f(s+3),\ \ 0.3f(s+2)+0.7f(s+3)\Bigr),
> $$
>
> where the first option is aiming left and the second is aiming right. The boundary conditions are $f(7)=1$ and $f(s)=0$ for every $s>7$, since an overshoot can never be corrected. Because every throw adds $2$ or $3$, the reachable totals are $0,2,3,4,5,6,7,8,\dots$ and the value $1$ is never visited.
>
> ### Solving backward from 7
>
> Since $6+2=8$ and $6+3=9$ both overshoot,
>
> $$
> f(6)=0.
> $$
>
> From $5$, the successors are $7$ and $8$, so aiming left gives $0.7f(7)+0.3f(6)=0.7$ while aiming right gives $0.3f(7)+0.7f(6)=0.3$. Aiming left is better:
>
> $$
> f(5)=0.7.
> $$
>
> From $4$, the successors are $6$ and $7$, so aiming left gives $0.7f(6)+0.3f(7)=0.3$ while aiming right gives $0.3f(6)+0.7f(7)=0.7$. Aiming right is better:
>
> $$
> f(4)=0.7.
> $$
>
> From $3$, the successors are $5$ and $6$, so aiming left gives $0.7f(5)+0.3f(6)=0.7\times0.7=0.49$ while aiming right gives $0.3f(5)+0.7f(6)=0.3\times0.7=0.21$. Aiming left is better:
>
> $$
> f(3)=0.49.
> $$
>
> From $2$, the successors are $4$ and $5$, so aiming left gives $0.7f(4)+0.3f(5)=0.7\times0.7+0.3\times0.7=0.7$ and aiming right gives the same value by symmetry:
>
> $$
> f(2)=0.7.
> $$
>
> Finally, from $0$, the successors are $2$ and $3$, so aiming left gives
> $$
> \text{left: } 0.7f(2)+0.3f(3)=0.7\times0.7+0.3\times0.49=0.49+0.147=0.637,
> $$
> while aiming right gives
> $$
> \text{right: } 0.3f(2)+0.7f(3)=0.3\times0.7+0.7\times0.49=0.21+0.343=0.553.
> $$
> Aiming left is better, so
>
> $$
> f(0)=0.637.
> $$
>
> ### Conclusion
>
> Starting from a total of $0$, the optimal policy is to aim left first, then follow the aim dictated by whichever running total results (left from $0$, right from $4$, left from $3$ and $5$, either from $2$), stopping the moment the total reaches $7$ and accepting that totals of $6$ or higher without hitting $7$ exactly can no longer succeed.
>
> The probability of achieving a score of exactly $7$ is
>
> $$
> \boxed{0.637}.
> $$
