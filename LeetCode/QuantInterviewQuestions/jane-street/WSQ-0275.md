> [!example] Question 275 · Jane Street
> ## Junior Trader Interview

Imagine an infinite chessboard. If a knight starts from a particular square, in how many different squares can it possibly end up after 10 moves? You do not need to calculate an exact number, but instead provide a 95% confidence interval for the number of possible ending squares. Pen and paper allowed.

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=276)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Model the knight’s position after each move as a two-dimensional random walk on the infinite chessboard.
>
> ## 1. One move of the knight
>
> From any square, a knight has $8$ possible moves:
> $$
> (\pm 2,\pm 1), \qquad (\pm 1,\pm 2).
> $$
> Assume each of these $8$ moves is chosen with equal probability.
>
> Let one move be the random vector
> $$
> (X,Y).
> $$
> Then by symmetry,
> $$
> E[X]=E[Y]=0.
> $$
>
> Compute the second moments. Among the $8$ moves, the $x$-coordinate is $\pm 2$ in $4$ cases and $\pm 1$ in $4$ cases, so
> $$
> E[X^2]=\frac{4\cdot 2^2+4\cdot 1^2}{8}
> =\frac{16+4}{8}
> =\frac{5}{2}.
> $$
> Similarly,
> $$
> E[Y^2]=\frac{5}{2}.
> $$
> Also, by symmetry,
> $$
> E[XY]=0.
> $$
> Hence the covariance matrix of one move is
> $$
> \Sigma_1=
> \begin{pmatrix}
> \frac{5}{2} & 0\\[4pt]
> 0 & \frac{5}{2}
> \end{pmatrix}.
> $$
>
> ## 2. Position after $10$ moves
>
> Let
> $$
> S_{10}=(X_1,Y_1)+\cdots+(X_{10},Y_{10}),
> $$
> where the $10$ moves are independent and identically distributed.
>
> Then
> $$
> E[S_{10}]=(0,0),
> $$
> and
> $$
> \Sigma_{10}=10\Sigma_1=
> \begin{pmatrix}
> 25 & 0\\[4pt]
> 0 & 25
> \end{pmatrix}.
> $$
> So each coordinate has variance $25$, hence standard deviation $5$.
>
> For a pen-and-paper approximation, invoke the two-dimensional central limit theorem:
> $$
> S_{10}\approx N\!\left(
> \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix},
> \begin{pmatrix}25&0\\0&25\end{pmatrix}
> \right).
> $$
> Thus the endpoint is approximately distributed like an isotropic Gaussian with density concentrated in a disk around the origin.
>
> ## 3. A $95\%$ confidence region
>
> For a bivariate normal with covariance $25I$, the quantity
> $$
> \frac{X^2+Y^2}{25}
> $$
> is approximately $\chi^2$ with $2$ degrees of freedom. Therefore a $95\%$ confidence region is
> $$
> \frac{x^2+y^2}{25}\le \chi^2_{2,\,0.95}.
> $$
> Since
> $$
> \chi^2_{2,\,0.95}\approx 5.99,
> $$
> this becomes
> $$
> x^2+y^2\le 25\cdot 5.99\approx 149.8.
> $$
> So the knight is with approximately $95\%$ probability within the disk
> $$
> x^2+y^2\lesssim 150,
> $$
> that is, within radius
> $$
> \sqrt{150}\approx 12.2.
> $$
>
> ## 4. Interpreting this as a number of possible squares
>
> The number of lattice squares in a large disk of radius $r$ is approximately its area:
> $$
> \pi r^2.
> $$
> Using $r^2\approx 150$ gives
> $$
> \pi\cdot 150 \approx 471.
> $$
> So a reasonable $95\%$ estimate for the number of squares where the knight may end up after $10$ random moves is about
> $$
> 470 \text{ squares}.
> $$
>
> Because a knight always alternates square color, after an even number of moves it must end on the same color as it started. Hence only about half the lattice points in that disk are actually reachable at parity level, giving a refined estimate
> $$
> \frac{1}{2}\pi\cdot 150 \approx 235.
> $$
>
> ## Final answer
>
> After $10$ random knight moves, the endpoint is approximately distributed as
> $$
> N\!\left((0,0),\,25I\right).
> $$
> A $95\%$ confidence region is therefore
> $$
> x^2+y^2\le 150,
> $$
> that is, a disk of radius about $12.2$ squares.
>
> This corresponds to roughly
>
> - $$\pi\cdot 150\approx 470$$ lattice squares in the disk, or
> - accounting for the knight’s color parity, about $$235$$ actually reachable squares.
>
> So the natural pen-and-paper answer is:
>
> $$
> \boxed{\text{After 10 moves, the knight is with }95\%\text{ probability within about }12\text{ squares of the start,}}
> $$
>
> and this means on the order of
>
> $$
> \boxed{2.3\times 10^2 \text{ reachable squares}}
> $$
>
> for a parity-corrected estimate.
