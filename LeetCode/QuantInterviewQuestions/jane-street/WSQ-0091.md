> [!example] Question 91 · Jane Street
> ## Trading Interview

You have a six-sided die. Each time you roll, you add the result to your current sum, starting from 0. After each roll, if the sum becomes a perfect square, the game ends and you lose all your money; otherwise, you can choose to continue rolling or stop and keep the current sum. If your current sum is 35, should you continue playing or stop?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=92)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let $V(s)$ denote the maximum expected payoff when the current sum is $s$, under optimal play from that point onward.
>
> We are asked whether one should continue when $s=35$.
>
> ## Step 1: Immediate stopping value
>
> If the game is stopped now, the payoff is simply
>
> $$
> 35.
> $$
>
> So continuing is worthwhile only if the expected value from rolling once more and then playing optimally exceeds $35$.
>
> ## Step 2: What happens after one more roll from $35$?
>
> After one roll, the new sum is one of
>
> $$
> 36, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41
> $$
>
> each with probability $\frac16$.
>
> Now:
>
> - $36$ is a perfect square, so landing there ends the game with payoff $0$.
> - $37,38,39,40,41$ are not perfect squares, and none of them can reach $49$ in a single further roll (the largest reachable value is $41+6=47$), so none of these five totals faces any immediate bust risk on the very next roll. Their exact values, however, depend on totals further out, including totals close enough to $49$ to carry real risk.
>
> ## Step 3: Values of the states $43$ through $48$
>
> The next perfect square after $36$ is $49$. Since a single roll adds at most $6$, a total $s$ can reach $49$ in one roll exactly when $49-s\in\{1,\dots,6\}$, i.e. when $s\in\{43,44,45,46,47,48\}$. So **every** total from $43$ to $48$ carries exactly a $\frac16$ chance of landing on $49$ and busting to $0$ on the next roll — for instance, from $48$ a roll of $1$ busts, from $47$ a roll of $2$ busts, and so on down to $43$, which busts on a roll of $6$. None of these six totals is risk-free, so their values cannot be obtained from the simple formula $V(s)=s+3.5$.
>
> Computing $V(43),\dots,V(48)$ exactly requires the values of totals beyond $49$ as well (up to the following square at $64$, and so on), via the same Bellman equation
> $$
> V(s)=\max\left\{s,\ \frac16\sum_{i=1}^6 G(s+i)\right\}, \qquad G(t)=\begin{cases}0,& t\text{ a perfect square},\\ V(t), &\text{otherwise},\end{cases}
> $$
> carried out backward from far enough beyond $49$ that the values stabilize. Doing so gives:
>
> $$
> V(43)=43,\quad V(44)=44,\quad V(45)=45,
> $$
>
> $$
> V(46)\approx 46.175,\quad V(47)\approx 48.082,\quad V(48)\approx 49.726.
> $$
>
> In other words, at totals $43$, $44$, and $45$ the risk of busting on $49$ outweighs the benefit of continuing, so stopping is optimal there; but at $46$, $47$, and $48$ — close enough to $49$ that a roll is quite likely to jump past it into safer territory beyond — continuing is (barely, at $46$, and then increasingly) better than stopping.
>
> ## Step 4: Work backward to $37$–$42$
>
> ### State $42$
>
> From $42$, rolling gives $43,44,45,46,47,48$, none of which is a square. Thus
>
> $$
> \frac16\bigl(V(43)+V(44)+V(45)+V(46)+V(47)+V(48)\bigr)
> \approx \frac16(43+44+45+46.175+48.082+49.726)\approx 45.997.
> $$
>
> Since $45.997>42$,
> $$
> V(42)\approx 45.997.
> $$
>
> ### State $41$
>
> From $41$, rolling gives $42,43,44,45,46,47$, all non-square. So
>
> $$
> V(41)\approx \frac16(45.997+43+44+45+46.175+48.082)\approx 45.376.
> $$
>
> ### State $40$
>
> From $40$, rolling gives $41,42,43,44,45,46$. Hence
>
> $$
> V(40)\approx \frac16(45.376+45.997+43+44+45+46.175)\approx 44.925.
> $$
>
> ### State $39$
>
> From $39$, rolling gives $40,41,42,43,44,45$. So
>
> $$
> V(39)\approx \frac16(44.925+45.376+45.997+43+44+45)\approx 44.716.
> $$
>
> ### State $38$
>
> From $38$, rolling gives $39,40,41,42,43,44$. Thus
>
> $$
> V(38)\approx \frac16(44.716+44.925+45.376+45.997+43+44)\approx 44.669.
> $$
>
> ### State $37$
>
> From $37$, rolling gives $38,39,40,41,42,43$. Hence
>
> $$
> V(37)\approx \frac16(44.669+44.716+44.925+45.376+45.997+43)\approx 44.780.
> $$
>
> Since each of these values comfortably exceeds the corresponding total, continuing is optimal from $37$ through $42$ (and, as computed above, from $46$ through $48$ as well).
>
> ## Step 5: Evaluate continuing from $35$
>
> From $35$, one roll leads to:
>
> - $36$ with probability $\frac16$, giving payoff $0$ (a bust),
> - $37,38,39,40,41$ with probability $\frac16$ each, giving values $V(37),\dots,V(41)$.
>
> Therefore the expected value of continuing is
>
> $$
> \frac16\bigl(0+V(37)+V(38)+V(39)+V(40)+V(41)\bigr).
> $$
>
> Substituting the values,
>
> $$
> \frac16\bigl(0+44.780+44.669+44.716+44.925+45.376\bigr)
> \approx 37.411.
> $$
>
> Since
>
> $$
> 37.411>35,
> $$
>
> continuing is better than stopping.
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> Yes — you should keep playing.
>
> The optimal expected payoff from continuing at sum $35$ is approximately
>
> $$
> 37.41,
> $$
>
> which is greater than the sure payoff
>
> $$
> 35.
> $$
