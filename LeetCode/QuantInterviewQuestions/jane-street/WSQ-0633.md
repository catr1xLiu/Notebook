> [!example] Question 633 · Jane Street
> ## Assistant Trader Interview

There is a bus with n people. At the first stop, 2/3 of the people get off and 7 get on. At the second stop, again 2/3 of the people get off and 7 get on. At the third stop, the same happens. What is the minimum possible number of people on the bus after the third stop?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=634)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let the initial number of people on the bus be $n$.
>
> At each stop, $\frac{2}{3}$ of the people get off, so $\frac{1}{3}$ remain, and then $7$ people get on.
>
> We want the number of people after each stop to remain a whole number. Thus, before each stop, the number of people must be divisible by $3$.
>
> Define the number of people after each stop:
>
> - Initially: $n$
> - After first stop:
>   $$
>   \frac{n}{3} + 7
>   $$
> - After second stop:
>   $$
>   \frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{n}{3}+7\right) + 7
>   $$
> - After third stop:
>   $$
>   \frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{n}{3}+7\right)+7\right)+7
>   $$
>
> Since $\frac{2}{3}$ get off at each stop, the number before each stop must be divisible by $3$.
>
> ## Step 1: First stop
> For $\frac{2}{3}$ of $n$ to be an integer, $n$ must be divisible by $3$:
> $$
> n \equiv 0 \pmod{3}
> $$
>
> ## Step 2: Second stop
> After the first stop, the number is
> $$
> \frac{n}{3}+7
> $$
> This must also be divisible by $3$:
> $$
> \frac{n}{3}+7 \equiv 0 \pmod{3}
> $$
> Since $7 \equiv 1 \pmod{3}$,
> $$
> \frac{n}{3}+1 \equiv 0 \pmod{3}
> $$
> so
> $$
> \frac{n}{3} \equiv 2 \pmod{3}
> $$
> Hence
> $$
> n \equiv 6 \pmod{9}
> $$
>
> ## Step 3: Third stop
> Let
> $$
> n = 9k+6
> $$
> Then after the first stop:
> $$
> \frac{n}{3}+7 = 3k+2+7 = 3k+9 = 3(k+3)
> $$
> So after the first stop the bus has $3(k+3)$ people.
>
> After the second stop:
> $$
> \frac{1}{3}\cdot 3(k+3)+7 = (k+3)+7 = k+10
> $$
> For the third stop to work, this must be divisible by $3$:
> $$
> k+10 \equiv 0 \pmod{3}
> $$
> so
> $$
> k \equiv 2 \pmod{3}
> $$
>
> Thus let
> $$
> k=3m+2
> $$
> Then
> $$
> n = 9k+6 = 9(3m+2)+6 = 27m+24
> $$
>
> The smallest nonnegative value of $m$ gives the smallest valid starting population,
> $$
> n=24 \quad (\text{at } m=0).
> $$
>
> ## General formula for the final count
>
> Tracking the count symbolically in terms of $m$: after the first stop,
> $$
> \frac{n}{3}+7=9m+8+7=9m+15.
> $$
> After the second stop,
> $$
> \frac{9m+15}{3}+7=3m+5+7=3m+12.
> $$
> After the third stop,
> $$
> \frac{3m+12}{3}+7=m+4+7=m+11.
> $$
>
> So the number of people **after the third stop** equals $m+11$ — this is exactly the quantity the question asks for.
>
> ## Check at $m=0$
>
> - Start: $24$
> - After first stop: $\frac{1}{3}\cdot 24 + 7 = 8+7=15$
> - After second stop: $\frac{1}{3}\cdot 15 + 7 = 5+7=12$
> - After third stop: $\frac{1}{3}\cdot 12 + 7 = 4+7=11$
>
> All numbers are whole numbers, confirming that with $m=0$ the count after the third stop is $0+11=11$.
>
> ## Minimizing the final count
>
> Since the final count equals $m+11$ for $m=0,1,2,\dots$, it is smallest when $m=0$, which also corresponds to the smallest valid starting population, $n=24$. Larger $m$ increases the starting population and the final count together, so no smaller final count is achievable.
>
> Therefore, the minimum possible number of people on the bus after the third stop is
>
> $$
> \boxed{11}
> $$
