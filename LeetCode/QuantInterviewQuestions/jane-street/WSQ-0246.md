> [!example] Question 246 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Analyst Interview

There are 30 blue balls and 30 red balls, and two urns. Your opponent may arrange the balls in the two urns in any way he chooses, without telling you the arrangement. You then select one urn and draw a ball at random from it. You win \$10 if you draw a blue ball, and \$0 otherwise. How much should you be willing to pay to play this game?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=247)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> Let the two urns be labeled $1$ and $2$. Your opponent may distribute the $30$ blue and $30$ red balls between them in any way, and you will not be told the arrangement before you choose an urn.
>
> Suppose urn $1$ ends up with $b_1$ blue and $r_1$ red balls, and urn $2$ with $b_2=30-b_1$ blue and $r_2=30-r_1$ red balls. If you draw from urn $1$, your probability of blue is
> $$
> p_1=\frac{b_1}{b_1+r_1},
> $$
> and if you draw from urn $2$,
> $$
> p_2=\frac{b_2}{b_2+r_2}.
> $$
>
> ## 1. A strategy that guarantees $(p_1+p_2)/2$
>
> Since you have no information distinguishing the two urns, the only strategy that cannot be exploited by however the opponent chooses to label them is to pick urn $1$ or urn $2$ each with probability $\tfrac12$. Under this randomized choice, your probability of winning is exactly
> $$
> \frac{p_1+p_2}{2},
> $$
> regardless of which urn the opponent calls "$1$" and which he calls "$2$". This is the value you can guarantee no matter how the balls are split.
>
> The opponent, anticipating this, will choose $b_1,r_1$ (equivalently $b_2,r_2$) to make $(p_1+p_2)/2$ as small as possible.
>
> ## 2. The opponent's best split
>
> Suppose the opponent puts **no blue balls** in urn $1$, so $b_1=0$ and urn $1$ contains only red balls. Then $p_1=0$ outright — urn $1$ never pays out. All $30$ blue balls are now in urn $2$, together with $30-r_1$ red balls, so
> $$
> p_2=\frac{30}{30+(30-r_1)}=\frac{30}{60-r_1}.
> $$
> Since $p_1=0$, the guaranteed value is
> $$
> \frac{p_1+p_2}{2}=\frac{p_2}{2}=\frac{15}{60-r_1}.
> $$
> This is smallest when $r_1$ is as small as possible. Urn $1$ must contain at least one ball (otherwise no draw from it is possible), so the opponent's best move is
> $$
> r_1=1: \quad \text{urn }1\text{ holds a single red ball, urn }2\text{ holds the other }59\text{ balls.}
> $$
> Then
> $$
> p_1=0,\qquad p_2=\frac{30}{59},
> $$
> and the value of the game is
> $$
> \frac{p_1+p_2}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{30}{59}=\frac{15}{59}.
> $$
>
> To see that isolating a single ball is indeed better for the opponent than isolating more, compare $r_1=1$ with $r_1=2$: the value would be $15/(60-2)=15/58\approx 0.2586$, which exceeds $15/59\approx 0.2542$. Shrinking the isolated urn down to its minimum possible size — one ball — makes the "good" urn's blue fraction as diluted as possible, and gives the smallest guaranteed value. Isolating a single **blue** ball instead would do the opposite: it would hand you $p_1=1$ on that urn, making the average far larger, so the opponent isolates a red ball, not a blue one.
>
> Checking other splits confirms this is globally optimal: any split that keeps blue balls in both urns lets you exploit whichever urn happens to be blue-heavy, and any split that isolates more than one ball dilutes the concentration less than the single-ball extreme. So the opponent's optimal arrangement is exactly: one urn holds a single red ball, and the other holds the remaining $29$ red and $30$ blue balls.
>
> ## 3. Value of the game
>
> With this arrangement, your best guaranteed probability of drawing blue is
> $$
> \frac{15}{59}.
> $$
>
> Since the payoff is $10$ dollars for a blue ball and $0$ otherwise, the fair value of the game is
> $$
> 10\cdot\frac{15}{59}=\frac{150}{59}\approx 2.54\text{ dollars}.
> $$
>
> ## Final answer
>
> The opponent's optimal (worst-case) arrangement isolates a single red ball alone in one urn, with all $59$ remaining balls in the other. Choosing an urn at random then wins with probability $15/59$, so the game is worth
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{150}{59}\approx 2.54\text{ dollars}}.
> $$
>
> This is the most you should be willing to pay to play.
