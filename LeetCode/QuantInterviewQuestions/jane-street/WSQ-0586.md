> [!example] Question 586 · Jane Street
> ## Assistant Trader Interview

Two people play a game with a 20-sided die. Player A chooses a number, and then Player B chooses a number. The die is rolled, and whichever player's number the result is closer to wins, and gets paid an amount. Who should be allowed to choose their number first to maximize fairness or advantage?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=587)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Consider a fair $20$-sided die with outcomes $\{1,2,\dots,20\}$. Player $A$ chooses a number first, then player $B$ chooses a number. After the die is rolled, the outcome is compared to the two chosen numbers, and the player whose number is closer to the roll receives the amount shown on the die.
>
> The question is: **does moving first or second give an advantage?**
>
> ## 1. Reformulating the game
>
> Let $A$ choose $a$ and $B$ choose $b$, where $a,b \in \{1,\dots,20\}$.
>
> For each die outcome $x$:
>
> - if $|x-a| < |x-b|$, then $A$ wins $x$;
> - if $|x-b| < |x-a|$, then $B$ wins $x$;
> - if $|x-a| = |x-b|$, then there is a tie, so neither has a strict advantage on that outcome.
>
> Thus each player wants to choose a number so that the outcomes closer to that number have as large a total value as possible.
>
> ## 2. Best response to a given first choice
>
> Suppose $A$ chooses $a$. Then $B$ chooses $b$ after seeing $a$.
>
> The crucial observation is that the outcomes awarded to each player are determined by the midpoint between $a$ and $b$.
>
> ### Case 1: $b>a$
>
> Then the dividing point is halfway between $a$ and $b$:
>
> - outcomes below the midpoint are closer to $a$,
> - outcomes above the midpoint are closer to $b$.
>
> So if $B$ wants the **larger die values**, $B$ should choose a number just to the right of $a$, because that gives $B$ the upper part of the range.
>
> Similarly, if $b<a$, then $B$ gets the lower part of the range, which is generally worse because lower rolls pay less.
>
> Therefore, unless $a$ is already near $20$, the second player can usually improve by choosing a number slightly larger than $a$ and capturing the high-paying outcomes.
>
> ## 3. Check the first player's possible choices
>
> Let us see what happens when $A$ chooses various numbers.
>
> ### If $A$ chooses an interior number
>
> Suppose $A$ chooses $a<20$. Then $B$ can choose $b=a+1$.
>
> Now compare distances to $a$ and $a+1$:
>
> - outcomes $1,2,\dots,a$ are closer to $a$;
> - outcomes $a+1,a+2,\dots,20$ are closer to $a+1$.
>
> So $A$ gets total payoff
>
> $$
> 1+2+\cdots+a=\frac{a(a+1)}{2},
> $$
>
> while $B$ gets
>
> $$
> (a+1)+(a+2)+\cdots+20
> = \frac{20\cdot 21}{2}-\frac{a(a+1)}{2}
> =210-\frac{a(a+1)}{2}.
> $$
>
> Thus $B$ does better whenever
>
> $$
> 210-\frac{a(a+1)}{2} > \frac{a(a+1)}{2},
> $$
>
> that is,
>
> $$
> 210 > a(a+1).
> $$
>
> Since $14\cdot 15=210$, this inequality holds for $a\le 13$.
>
> So if $A$ chooses any number up to $13$, then $B$ can choose $a+1$ and obtain a strictly larger expected total.
>
> ### If $A$ chooses a large number
>
> If $A$ chooses $a>1$, then $B$ can also consider choosing $b=a-1$, which gives $B$ the lower outcomes and $A$ the higher ones. Usually that is worse for $B$, but for very large $a$, the first player may already be pushed too far right.
>
> So the best first move should be near the center, where neither side can take too much of the high end.
>
> ## 4. Optimal first move
>
> To minimize the second player’s advantage, $A$ should choose the number that balances the total value on both sides as evenly as possible.
>
> The total sum of all die values is
>
> $$
> 1+2+\cdots+20 = 210.
> $$
>
> Half of this is
>
> $$
> 105.
> $$
>
> We seek $a$ so that
>
> $$
> 1+2+\cdots+a \approx 105.
> $$
>
> Now
>
> $$
> 1+\cdots+13 = 91,\qquad 1+\cdots+14 = 105.
> $$
>
> So choosing $a=14$ is exactly the balance point.
>
> If $A$ chooses $14$:
>
> - if $B$ chooses $15$, then $A$ gets $1$ through $14$, total $105$, and $B$ gets $15$ through $20$, total $105$;
> - if $B$ chooses $13$, then $B$ gets $1$ through $13$, total $91$, and $A$ gets $14$ through $20$, total $119$.
>
> So $B$’s best response is $15$, and this only ties the game.
>
> Hence the first player can guarantee at least a tie by choosing $14$.
>
> ## 5. Conclusion
>
> The first player should move first and choose $14$.
>
> Then the second player’s best response is $15$, and the expected payoffs are equal:
>
> $$
> 1+2+\cdots+14 = 15+16+\cdots+20 = 105.
> $$
>
> So **going first is at least as good as going second**, because the first player can force a tie by choosing $14$.
>
> ## Final answer
>
> **The first player has the advantage of being able to force a draw:** choose $14$.  
> The second player’s best reply is $15$, and then both sides have equal expected winnings, namely $105/20$ per roll. Thus **you would rather give the number first**.
