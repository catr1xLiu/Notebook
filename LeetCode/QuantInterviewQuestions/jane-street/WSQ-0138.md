> [!example] Question 138 · Jane Street
> ## Intern Interview

What is the optimal strategy for winning in rock-paper-scissors if your opponent can only choose between rock and paper?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=139)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let the payoffs be $+1$ for a win, $0$ for a tie, and $-1$ for a loss.
>
> Suppose the opponent is restricted to playing only rock or paper. Let
>
> - $q$ = probability the opponent plays rock,
> - $1-q$ = probability the opponent plays paper.
>
> Let your mixed strategy be
>
> - $x_R$ = probability you play rock,
> - $x_P$ = probability you play paper,
> - $x_S$ = probability you play scissors,
>
> with
> $$
> x_R + x_P + x_S = 1, \qquad x_R,x_P,x_S \ge 0.
> $$
>
> ## Step 1: Compute the expected payoff of each pure action
>
> If you play rock:
>
> - against rock: payoff $0$,
> - against paper: payoff $-1$,
>
> so
> $$
> u(R)=q\cdot 0 + (1-q)(-1)=q-1.
> $$
>
> If you play paper:
>
> - against rock: payoff $+1$,
> - against paper: payoff $0$,
>
> so
> $$
> u(P)=q(+1)+(1-q)\cdot 0=q.
> $$
>
> If you play scissors:
>
> - against rock: payoff $-1$,
> - against paper: payoff $+1$,
>
> so
> $$
> u(S)=q(-1)+(1-q)(+1)=1-2q.
> $$
>
> ## Step 2: Compare the pure actions
>
> Since
> $$
> u(P)=q \ge q-1=u(R)
> $$
> for every $q$, paper always does at least as well as rock, and strictly better unless $q=1$. Thus rock is never optimal.
>
> So the only relevant comparison is between paper and scissors:
>
> - paper gives payoff $q$,
> - scissors gives payoff $1-2q$.
>
> Choose paper when
> $$
> q \ge 1-2q,
> $$
> that is,
> $$
> 3q \ge 1
> \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
> q \ge \frac13.
> $$
>
> Choose scissors when
> $$
> 1-2q \ge q,
> $$
> that is,
> $$
> q \le \frac13.
> $$
>
> At $q=\frac13$, both give the same payoff:
> $$
> u(P)=u(S)=\frac13.
> $$
>
> ## Step 3: Optimal strategy
>
> Therefore the optimal response is:
>
> $$
> \text{Play scissors if } q<\frac13,
> $$
> $$
> \text{play paper if } q>\frac13,
> $$
> and if
> $$
> q=\frac13,
> $$
> any mixture of paper and scissors is optimal.
>
> Equivalently, in mixed-strategy form:
>
> - if the opponent plays rock with probability less than $\frac13$, use
>   $$
>   (x_R,x_P,x_S)=(0,0,1);
>   $$
> - if the opponent plays rock with probability greater than $\frac13$, use
>   $$
>   (x_R,x_P,x_S)=(0,1,0);
>   $$
> - if the opponent plays rock with probability exactly $\frac13$, use any
>   $$
>   (x_R,x_P,x_S)=(0,\lambda,1-\lambda), \qquad 0\le \lambda \le 1.
>   $$
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> If the opponent can only play rock and paper, then rock is never a best reply for you. The optimal strategy is:
>
> $$
> \boxed{
> \text{Play paper if the opponent uses rock with probability at least } \frac13,
> \text{ and scissors if it is less than } \frac13.
> }
> $$
>
> If no probabilities are known and only the restriction “rock or paper” is given, then the minimax-optimal choice is paper, since against any mixture of rock and paper it guarantees expected payoff at least $0$, whereas scissors can do badly when rock is frequent.
