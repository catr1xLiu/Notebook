> [!example] Question 576 · Jane Street
> ## Assistant Trader Interview

You play a game with an urn containing 75 blue balls, 25 red balls, and 1 yellow ball. You earn a dollar for every red ball drawn. If you draw the yellow ball, you lose all accumulated winnings. After drawing each ball, you can choose to stop and keep your winnings or continue drawing. What should be your optimal strategy in this game?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=577)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> At any point before the yellow ball is drawn, suppose the urn contains $r$ red balls, $b$ blue balls, and the $1$ yellow ball. Every red ball drawn adds $1$ dollar, every blue ball adds nothing, and drawing the yellow ball instantly forfeits all accumulated winnings, not merely future ones. Consequently, the winnings accumulated so far are always exactly $25-r$ dollars, the number of red balls already drawn, regardless of how many blue balls have appeared. This means the state of the decision problem is fully captured by the pair $(r,b)$, with the current winnings $25-r$ determined by $r$ alone.
>
> ### A tempting but costly strategy
>
> It is tempting to reason that, since red balls are the only source of profit, one should simply keep drawing for as long as red balls remain, stopping only once $r=0$. To see why this is costly, consider committing in advance to exactly this rule: never stop early, and bank whatever has accumulated only once all $25$ red balls have been drawn.
>
> Among the $25$ red balls and the $1$ yellow ball, every relative order is equally likely, regardless of where the $75$ blue balls fall. This rule succeeds in banking the full $25$ dollars only if the yellow ball happens to be the very last of these $26$ special balls, which happens with probability $\frac1{26}$. In every other case, probability $\frac{25}{26}$, the yellow ball appears before all $25$ reds have been drawn, and since the rule never stops early, everything accumulated up to that point is wiped out to $0$. So this rule is worth only
>
> $$
> 25\cdot\frac1{26}+0\cdot\frac{25}{26}=\frac{25}{26}\approx 0.96\text{ dollars},
> $$
>
> far less than a well-chosen stopping rule can achieve. The flaw is that riding things out risks losing everything already won, not merely forgoing future gains, so the decision to continue must weigh the amount already banked, not just the reds still available.
>
> ### Correct dynamic-programming formulation
>
> Let $V(r,b)$ denote the maximum expected final payoff obtainable from state $(r,b)$ under optimal play, where stopping immediately banks the current winnings $25-r$. If you continue from $(r,b)$ with $r\ge 1$:
>
> - with probability $\dfrac{r}{r+b+1}$, you draw red, gain $1$ dollar, and move to $(r-1,b)$;
> - with probability $\dfrac{b}{r+b+1}$, you draw blue, and move to $(r,b-1)$ with your winnings unchanged;
> - with probability $\dfrac{1}{r+b+1}$, you draw the yellow ball and the game ends immediately with $0$.
>
> So
>
> $$
> V(r,b)=\max\left\{25-r,\ \ \frac{r}{r+b+1}V(r-1,b)+\frac{b}{r+b+1}V(r,b-1)\right\},
> $$
>
> with the boundary condition $V(0,b)=25$ for every $b$, since once no red balls remain, continuing only risks the banked $25$ dollars for no possible further gain, so stopping is always at least as good.
>
> ### Solving the recursion
>
> This recursion can be evaluated exactly by working backward through all states $(r,b)$ with $r$ from $0$ to $25$ and $b$ from $0$ to $75$. Doing so reveals a strikingly clean optimal policy that depends only on $r$, not on $b$: it is optimal to keep drawing as long as at least $13$ red balls remain in the urn, and to stop the instant only $12$ or fewer red balls remain, that is, the moment your accumulated winnings first reach $13$ dollars.
>
> Evaluating $V(25,75)$ under this policy gives, exactly,
>
> $$
> V(25,75)=\frac{13}{2}=6.50.
> $$
>
> ## Final answer
>
> The optimal strategy is to keep drawing as long as at least $13$ of the original $25$ red balls remain in the urn, and to stop the moment your accumulated winnings first reach $13$ dollars (equivalently, once only $12$ or fewer red balls remain), regardless of how many blue balls are left.
>
> The value of the game under this optimal strategy is
>
> $$
> \boxed{6.50\text{ dollars}}.
> $$
