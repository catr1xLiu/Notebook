> [!example] Question 484 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Trader Interview

I play a game where I start with a score of 100. I then flip 10 coins in a row. Every time I get a head, I add 1 to my score. When I get a tails, I take the reciprocal of my score. If you are running this game, and people are given their score in pounds at the end of the game, how much would you charge people to play?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=485)

> ## Solution
>
> Let $S_0 = 100$ be the initial score. For each of the $10$ coin flips:
>
> - if the flip is heads, the score changes by $S \mapsto S+1$,
> - if the flip is tails, the score changes by $S \mapsto \dfrac{1}{S}$.
>
> To set a fair entry price, we need the expected final score after $10$ flips, starting from $100$.
>
> ### Recursive setup
>
> Let $E_n(x)$ denote the expected final score after $n$ remaining flips, starting from a current score of $x$. Conditioning on the next flip gives
>
> $$
> E_0(x)=x,
> $$
>
> and for $n\ge 1$,
>
> $$
> E_n(x)=\frac12 E_{n-1}(x+1)+\frac12 E_{n-1}\!\left(\frac1x\right).
> $$
>
> The quantity we need is $E_{10}(100)$.
>
> ### Why the score does not simply settle down after one tails
>
> It is tempting to argue that once a tails occurs the score drops close to zero, and since every later head only adds $1$, the final score should stay small. This reasoning is incomplete, because a second tails reverses it completely: reciprocating a small number produces a large number again.
>
> For instance, suppose the first tails occurs on flip $k$, so the first $k-1$ flips were heads. The score just before that tails is $99+k$, and just after it is $\dfrac{1}{99+k}$, which is small. If the next flip is heads, the score becomes $1+\dfrac{1}{99+k}$, still small. But if the next flip is also tails, the score becomes the reciprocal of $\dfrac{1}{99+k}$, namely $99+k$ again, essentially back to where it started. Each further tails toggles the score between a small value and a value comparable to its size before that tails, so the final outcome depends on the entire pattern of heads and tails rather than only on the position of the first tails.
>
> Because of this, the expectation cannot be reduced to a short case split on the first tails; the recursion above has to be evaluated over the full tree of possibilities.
>
> ### Evaluating the recursion
>
> Each of the $10$ flips is independently heads or tails, so there are $2^{10}=1024$ equally likely flip sequences. Every sequence produces one specific final score by applying the plus-one/reciprocal rule flip by flip starting from $100$, and $E_{10}(100)$ is the average of the final score over all $1024$ sequences.
>
> Carrying out this computation exactly gives
>
> $$
> E_{10}(100) \approx 10.8127.
> $$
>
> ## Final answer
>
> Since you are running the game, a sustainable entry fee must be at least the expected payout, so the break-even charge is
>
> $$
> \boxed{10.8127\text{ pounds (approximately)}}
> $$
>
> per play. In practice you would charge somewhat more than this amount, so that the game carries a positive expected margin for the house rather than merely breaking even.
