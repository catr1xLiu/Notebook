> [!example] Question 127 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Trader Intern Interview

You have two indistinguishable urns. One contains seven \$1 chips and three \$10 chips, and the other contains nine \$1 chips and one \$10 chip. You randomly draw a chip from one of the urns and it turns out to be a \$10 chip (the drawn chip is not replaced). You are then offered the chance to draw and keep a chip from either urn. Should you draw from the same urn or the other urn, and what is the expected value of your draw? Explain your reasoning.

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=128)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let urn $A$ contain seven $\text{\textdollar}1$ chips and three $\text{\textdollar}10$ chips, and urn $B$ contain nine $\text{\textdollar}1$ chips and one $\text{\textdollar}10$ chip. One urn is chosen at random, a chip is drawn, and the observed chip is $\text{\textdollar}10$. That chip is not replaced. We must decide whether the next draw should be from the same urn or from the other urn.
>
> ## 1. Update the probability that the observed urn was $A$ or $B$
>
> Before seeing the first draw,
>
> $$
> P(A)=P(B)=\frac12.
> $$
>
> The probability of drawing a $\text{\textdollar}10$ from each urn is
>
> $$
> P(10\mid A)=\frac{3}{10}, \qquad P(10\mid B)=\frac{1}{10}.
> $$
>
> Hence
>
> $$
> P(10)=P(10\mid A)P(A)+P(10\mid B)P(B)
> =\frac{3}{10}\cdot\frac12+\frac{1}{10}\cdot\frac12
> =\frac{1}{5}.
> $$
>
> By Bayes' theorem,
>
> $$
> P(A\mid 10)=\frac{P(10\mid A)P(A)}{P(10)}
> =\frac{\frac{3}{10}\cdot\frac12}{\frac15}
> =\frac34,
> $$
>
> and therefore
>
> $$
> P(B\mid 10)=\frac14.
> $$
>
> So after seeing a $\text{\textdollar}10$, it is more likely that the first draw came from urn $A$.
>
> ## 2. Expected value if you draw from the same urn
>
> If the observed urn was $A$, then after removing one $\text{\textdollar}10$ chip it contains
>
> $$
> 7\text{ chips of }\$1 \quad\text{and}\quad 2\text{ chips of }\$10,
> $$
>
> so the expected value of a draw from that urn is
>
> $$
> E[\text{same}\mid A,\,10]=\frac{7\cdot 1+2\cdot 10}{9}
> =\frac{27}{9}=3.
> $$
>
> If the observed urn was $B$, then after removing its only $\text{\textdollar}10$ chip it contains only nine $\text{\textdollar}1$ chips, so
>
> $$
> E[\text{same}\mid B,\,10]=1.
> $$
>
> Average these using the posterior probabilities:
>
> $$
> E[\text{same}\mid 10]
> = P(A\mid 10)\cdot 3 + P(B\mid 10)\cdot 1
> = \frac34\cdot 3+\frac14\cdot 1
> = \frac{10}{4}
> = 2.5.
> $$
>
> ## 3. Expected value if you draw from the opposite urn
>
> If the observed urn was $A$, then the opposite urn is $B$, whose expected draw value is
>
> $$
> E[\text{opp}\mid A,\,10]
> =\frac{9\cdot 1+1\cdot 10}{10}
> =\frac{19}{10}=1.9.
> $$
>
> If the observed urn was $B$, then the opposite urn is $A$, whose expected draw value is
>
> $$
> E[\text{opp}\mid B,\,10]
> =\frac{7\cdot 1+3\cdot 10}{10}
> =\frac{37}{10}=3.7.
> $$
>
> Thus
>
> $$
> E[\text{opp}\mid 10]
> = P(A\mid 10)\cdot 1.9 + P(B\mid 10)\cdot 3.7
> = \frac34\cdot 1.9+\frac14\cdot 3.7
> = 2.35.
> $$
>
> ## 4. Decision
>
> Since
>
> $$
> 2.5 > 2.35,
> $$
>
> you should draw from the **same urn**.
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> The best choice is to draw from the **same urn**, and the expected value of that draw is
>
> $$
> \$2.50.
> $$
>
> The reason is that observing a $\text{\textdollar}10$ makes it more likely that the urn you sampled was the urn with three $\text{\textdollar}10$ chips, and even after removing one such chip, that urn still tends to be better than the other.
