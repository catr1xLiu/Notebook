> [!example] Question 311 · Jane Street
> ## Trader Interview

You have 100 coins. How can you split them into piles such that the product of the number of coins in each pile is maximized? In the general setting, what is the optimal strategy?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=312)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> To maximize the product of the pile sizes subject to a fixed total number of coins, one should split the coins into parts that are as close to $e$ as possible. Since pile sizes must be positive integers, this leads to using mostly $3$'s, with a small adjustment when a remainder of $1$ occurs.
>
> ## Example: $100$ coins
>
> Let the pile sizes be positive integers $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_k$ such that
>
> $$
> a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_k=100.
> $$
>
> We seek to maximize
>
> $$
> P=a_1a_2\cdots a_k.
> $$
>
> ### Step 1: Eliminate large parts
>
> If some pile has size at least $5$, say $m\ge 5$, then replacing it by two piles $3$ and $m-3$ increases the product, because
>
> $$
> 3(m-3)>m
> \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
> 2m>9,
> $$
>
> which is true for all $m\ge 5$. Hence, in an optimal decomposition, no part is $\ge 5$.
>
> ### Step 2: Eliminate $1$'s
>
> A pile of size $1$ is never useful, since
>
> $$
> 1\cdot (m-1)<m
> $$
>
> for any $m\ge 2$. Thus optimal decompositions use no $1$'s, except possibly temporarily before adjustment.
>
> ### Step 3: Compare $2$'s and $3$'s
>
> Since
>
> $$
> 4=2+2
> \quad\text{and}\quad
> 2\cdot 2=4,
> $$
>
> a $4$ may be replaced by two $2$'s without changing the product. Also,
>
> $$
> 3\cdot 3=9>2\cdot 2\cdot 2=8,
> $$
>
> so, for a fixed sum of $6$, two $3$'s are better than three $2$'s. Thus we want as many $3$'s as possible.
>
> The only exception is when this leaves a remainder of $1$, because
>
> $$
> 3+1=4,
> \quad
> 3\cdot 1=3<2\cdot 2=4.
> $$
>
> So whenever a remainder $1$ would occur, replace one $3+1$ by $2+2$.
>
> ## Apply to $100$
>
> Divide $100$ by $3$:
>
> $$
> 100=3\cdot 33+1.
> $$
>
> Here $q=33$ and the remainder is $r=1$. By the rule above, this remainder is handled by using $q-1=32$ piles of $3$ together with two piles of $2$:
>
> $$
> 100=32\cdot 3+2+2.
> $$
>
> Thus the maximum product is
>
> $$
> P_{\max}=3^{32}\cdot 2^2=4\cdot 3^{32}.
> $$
>
> ## General strategy for $n$ coins
>
> Let $n$ be a positive integer. Write
>
> $$
> n=3q+r,
> \qquad r\in\{0,1,2\}.
> $$
>
> Then the product is maximized as follows:
>
> - If $r=0$, use all $3$'s:
>   $$
>   n=3+\cdots+3
>   \quad\Rightarrow\quad
>   P_{\max}=3^q.
>   $$
>
> - If $r=1$, use $q-1$ piles of $3$ and two piles of $2$:
>   $$
>   n=3(q-1)+2+2
>   \quad\Rightarrow\quad
>   P_{\max}=3^{q-1}\cdot 4.
>   $$
>
> - If $r=2$, use $q$ piles of $3$ and one pile of $2$:
>   $$
>   n=3q+2
>   \quad\Rightarrow\quad
>   P_{\max}=3^q\cdot 2.
>   $$
>
> ## Final answer
>
> For $100$ coins, the optimal split is
>
> $$
> 100=3+\cdots+3+2+2
> $$
>
> with $32$ piles of $3$ and $2$ piles of $2$, giving maximum product
>
> $$
> \boxed{4\cdot 3^{32}}.
> $$
>
> In general, to maximize the product of positive integer pile sizes summing to $n$, use as many $3$'s as possible, except that a remainder of $1$ should be replaced by $2+2$.
