> [!example] Question 47 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

You repeatedly play rock, paper, scissors against an opponent who cannot choose 'rock.' The game continues if there is a draw, and ends when one person loses. What strategy should you use?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=48)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let the opponent be restricted to the actions $\{\text{paper}, \text{scissors}\}$.  
> You may choose among $\{\text{rock}, \text{paper}, \text{scissors}\}$ each round, and if the round is a draw, the game repeats until a non-draw occurs.
>
> The objective is to choose a strategy maximizing the probability of eventually winning.
>
> ## Step 1: Compare the possible pure strategies
>
> Suppose you always choose one fixed action.
>
> ### If you always play rock
> - Against paper, you lose.
> - Against scissors, you win.
> - There is never a draw.
>
> Thus your probability of winning is exactly the probability that the opponent throws scissors.
>
> ### If you always play paper
> - Against paper, there is a draw.
> - Against scissors, you lose.
>
> So you can never win: eventually the first non-draw is a loss.  
> Hence the probability of winning is $0$.
>
> ### If you always play scissors
> - Against paper, you win.
> - Against scissors, there is a draw.
>
> So you win exactly when the first non-draw occurs and the opponent has played paper.  
> Hence your probability of winning is the probability that the opponent eventually throws paper on a decisive round.
>
> ## Step 2: Use the fact that draws simply repeat the game
>
> Because a draw restarts the same situation, only the outcomes on non-draw rounds matter.
>
> - Playing paper is strictly bad, since it cannot produce a win.
> - Playing rock gives:
>   - win versus scissors,
>   - loss versus paper.
> - Playing scissors gives:
>   - win versus paper,
>   - draw versus scissors.
>
> Thus:
> - if the opponent is more likely to play scissors, rock is better;
> - if the opponent is more likely to play paper, scissors is better.
>
> ## Step 3: Worst-case reasoning
>
> If you do not know how often the opponent uses paper or scissors, then choosing scissors is safest.
>
> Why? Because:
> - rock can lose immediately to paper;
> - scissors never loses to paper, and against scissors it only draws and tries again.
>
> In particular, if the opponent is free to choose any strategy using only paper and scissors, then:
> - by playing rock, your worst-case winning probability is $0$ (the opponent can always play paper);
> - by playing scissors, your worst-case winning probability is at least $0$, and if the opponent ever plays paper, you eventually win on that round.
>
> So the optimal strategy is to **always play scissors**.
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> The best strategy is:
>
> $$
> \boxed{\text{Always play scissors.}}
> $$
>
> Since the opponent cannot throw rock, scissors can never be beaten by rock; it either wins against paper or draws against scissors and repeats. Thus it is the optimal choice.
