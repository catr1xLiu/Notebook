> [!example] Question 280 · Jane Street
> ## Quant Trading Intern Interview

You play a 100-round game: in each round, you can either roll a fair 20-sided die (with outcomes 1 to 20) or 'lock in' the current face value and collect that same reward for all remaining rounds. You must roll in the first round. What is your optimal stopping or locking strategy, and what is your expected value?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=281)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> Let $V_n$ denote the maximum expected total reward when there are $n$ rounds remaining and you are **not yet locked in**, with the understanding that in the current round you may either:
>
> - observe the die result and then decide whether to lock it in for the remaining rounds, or
> - continue to the next round uncommitted.
>
> Because you must roll in the first round, this is an optimal stopping problem with a finite horizon.
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. Backward induction setup
>
> Suppose there are $n$ rounds remaining, and in the current round you roll a value $x \in \{1,2,\dots,20\}$.
>
> After seeing $x$, you have two choices:
>
> 1. **Lock in now.**
>    Then you receive $x$ for each of the $n$ remaining rounds, for total
>    $$
>    nx.
>    $$
>
> 2. **Do not lock in.**
>    You take the current round's reward $x$, and then with $n-1$ rounds remaining you are again in the same decision problem, worth
>    $$
>    V_{n-1}.
>    $$
>    So this gives total
>    $$
>    x + V_{n-1}.
>    $$
>
> Hence, after observing $x$, the optimal value is
> $$
> \max\{nx,\; x+V_{n-1}\}
> = x + \max\{(n-1)x,\; V_{n-1}\}.
> $$
>
> Therefore
> $$
> V_n = \mathbb{E}\big[\max\{nX,\; X+V_{n-1}\}\big],
> $$
> where $X$ is uniform on $\{1,\dots,20\}$.
>
> Equivalently, lock in when
> $$
> nx \ge x + V_{n-1}
> \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
> (n-1)x \ge V_{n-1}.
> $$
> Thus the optimal policy is a **threshold rule**:
> $$
> \text{lock if } x \ge \frac{V_{n-1}}{n-1}.
> $$
>
> Since $x$ is integer-valued, define the threshold
> $$
> t_n = \left\lceil \frac{V_{n-1}}{n-1} \right\rceil.
> $$
> Then with $n$ rounds remaining, after rolling $x$, it is optimal to lock iff
> $$
> x \ge t_n.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Base case
>
> If $n=1$, there is only one round left, so after rolling $x$ there is nothing to decide:
> $$
> V_1 = \mathbb{E}[X] = \frac{1+20}{2} = 10.5.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Recursion
>
> For $n \ge 2$,
> $$
> V_n
> = \frac{1}{20}\sum_{x=1}^{20} \max\{nx,\; x+V_{n-1}\}.
> $$
>
> It is convenient to compute this recursively using the threshold $t_n$:
> - for $x < t_n$, continue, contributing $x+V_{n-1}$;
> - for $x \ge t_n$, lock, contributing $nx$.
>
> So
> $$
> V_n
> = \frac{1}{20}
> \left(
> \sum_{x=1}^{t_n-1}(x+V_{n-1})
> +
> \sum_{x=t_n}^{20} nx
> \right).
> $$
>
> Carrying out this backward induction from $V_1=10.5$ up to $V_{100}$ yields the thresholds and values below.
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. Optimal thresholds
>
> Let $t_n$ be the value at which, with $n$ rounds remaining, you lock iff the roll is at least $t_n$. Working the recursion through exactly gives the following bands:
>
> - $n=2$: threshold $11$
> - $n=3$: threshold $12$
> - $n=4$: threshold $13$
> - $n=5$ to $6$: threshold $14$
> - $n=7$ to $9$: threshold $15$
> - $n=10$ to $15$: threshold $16$
> - $n=16$ to $27$: threshold $17$
> - $n=28$ to $57$: threshold $18$
> - $n=58$ to $100$: threshold $19$
>
> In particular, for the original problem with $100$ rounds, on the first roll you should **lock in immediately if the result is $19$ or $20$**, and otherwise continue.
>
> If you continue, the threshold needed to lock in eventually relaxes as rounds are used up:
>
> - from $19$ down to $18$ once only $57$ rounds remain,
> - then to $17$ once only $27$ remain,
> - then to $16$ once only $15$ remain,
> - then to $15$ once only $9$ remain,
> - then to $14$ once only $6$ remain,
> - then to $13$ once only $4$ remain,
> - then to $12$ once only $3$ remain,
> - then to $11$ once only $2$ remain,
> - and with a single round left, there is no choice to make — you simply take the roll.
>
> ---
>
> ## 5. Expected value
>
> Applying the recursion through $n=100$ gives
> $$
> V_{100} = 1860.052358\ldots
> $$
>
> Thus the maximum expected total reward is approximately
> $$
> \boxed{1860.05}.
> $$
>
> For comparison, always locking the first roll would give only
> $$
> 100 \cdot \mathbb{E}[X] = 100 \cdot 10.5 = 1050,
> $$
> so the optimal stopping rule provides a very substantial gain — more than $800$ extra units of expected value over $100$ rounds, since being selective early lets a good early roll compound across many rounds while a bad one can simply be discarded for the price of a single round's reward.
>
> ---
>
> ## Final answer
>
> The optimal policy is a backward-induction threshold rule:
>
> - after rolling a value $x$ with $n$ rounds remaining, lock in iff
>   $$
>   x \ge \left\lceil \frac{V_{n-1}}{n-1} \right\rceil,
>   $$
>   where $V_n$ is the optimal expected total reward with $n$ rounds remaining.
>
> For $100$ rounds, this becomes:
>
> - lock on $19+$ when there are $58$ to $100$ rounds left,
> - lock on $18+$ when there are $28$ to $57$ rounds left,
> - lock on $17+$ when there are $16$ to $27$ rounds left,
> - lock on $16+$ when there are $10$ to $15$ rounds left,
> - lock on $15+$ when there are $7$ to $9$ rounds left,
> - lock on $14+$ when there are $5$ to $6$ rounds left,
> - lock on $13+$ when there are $4$ rounds left,
> - lock on $12+$ when there are $3$ rounds left,
> - lock on $11+$ when there are $2$ rounds left.
>
> Hence on the first roll, lock immediately iff you roll at least $19$.
>
> The optimal expected total reward is
>
> $$
> \boxed{1860.052358\ldots \approx 1860.05}.
> $$
