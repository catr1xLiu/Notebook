> [!example] Question 594 · Jane Street
> ## Assistant Trader Interview

You and a friend are playing a game where a random number X between 1 and 20 is chosen, and each of you picks a different number. The person whose chosen number is closer to X wins X dollars. Should you choose to pick first, and what number should you pick if you do?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=595)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let the hidden value be $X \in \{1,2,\dots,20\}$, chosen uniformly at random. Two players pick distinct integers from $\{1,2,\dots,20\}$, and the player whose guess is closer to $X$ wins $X$ dollars. The question is whether it is better to choose first or second, and what the first player should choose.
>
> ## 1. Reformulating the objective
>
> Since the payoff is $X$ dollars, the goal is to maximize the expected value of the amount won.
>
> Suppose your guess is $a$ and your opponent's guess is $b$, with $a \neq b$. For each possible hidden value $x$:
>
> - you win $x$ dollars if $|x-a|<|x-b|$,
> - you lose $x$ dollars if $|x-a|>|x-b|$,
> - if $|x-a|=|x-b|$, neither player is closer, so this contributes $0$ to the expected advantage.
>
> Thus your expected advantage over the opponent is
> $$
> \frac1{20}\sum_{x=1}^{20} x \cdot s_x,
> $$
> where
> $$
> s_x=\begin{cases}1,& |x-a|<|x-b|,\\ -1,& |x-a|>|x-b|,\\ 0,& |x-a|=|x-b|.\end{cases}
> $$
>
> Because the payoff is weighted by $x$, larger values of $X$ matter more, so the game is not symmetric about the midpoint in the usual way.
>
> ## 2. Best response to a fixed first choice
>
> Assume the first player chooses $a$, and the second player chooses $b$.
>
> Without loss of generality, suppose $b>a$. Then the cutoff between the two guesses is their midpoint. Values of $x$ less than the midpoint are closer to $a$, and values greater than the midpoint are closer to $b$.
>
> So if the second player chooses a number larger than $a$, they are capturing the larger values of $X$, which are more valuable. This suggests that the second player will generally want to choose a number just above the first player's choice.
>
> We now compute this exactly.
>
> ## 3. Compare adjacent choices
>
> Suppose the first player chooses $a$, and the second player responds with $a+1$.
>
> Then:
>
> - $x \le a$ is closer to $a$,
> - $x \ge a+1$ is closer to $a+1$,
>
> since there is no tie between adjacent integers.
>
> So the first player's expected gain is proportional to
> $$
> \sum_{x=1}^{a} x,
> $$
> while the second player's expected gain is proportional to
> $$
> \sum_{x=a+1}^{20} x.
> $$
>
> These sums are
> $$
> \sum_{x=1}^{a} x = \frac{a(a+1)}{2},
> $$
> and
> $$
> \sum_{x=a+1}^{20} x = \sum_{x=1}^{20}x-\sum_{x=1}^{a}x=210-\frac{a(a+1)}{2}.
> $$
>
> Thus if the second player chooses $a+1$, the second player does strictly better exactly when
> $$
> 210-\frac{a(a+1)}{2}>\frac{a(a+1)}{2},
> $$
> that is,
> $$
> 210>a(a+1).
> $$
>
> Now
> $$
> 14\cdot 15=210.
> $$
> Hence:
>
> - if $a \le 13$, then $a(a+1)<210$, so choosing $a+1$ gives the second player a strict advantage;
> - if $a = 14$, then $a(a+1)=210$ exactly, so choosing $a+1=15$ leaves the two players exactly tied;
> - if $a \ge 15$, then $a(a+1)>210$, so the first player does better than a second player who chooses $a+1$.
>
> Similarly, if the first player chooses $a$, the second player could choose $a-1$, in which case the first player gets the upper part $\{a,\dots,20\}$ and the second player gets the lower part $\{1,\dots,a-1\}$.
>
> The first player's weighted total would then be
> $$
> \sum_{x=a}^{20}x = 210-\frac{(a-1)a}{2},
> $$
> and the second player's weighted total would be
> $$
> \sum_{x=1}^{a-1}x=\frac{(a-1)a}{2}.
> $$
>
> The second player prefers $a-1$ strictly when
> $$
> \frac{(a-1)a}{2}>210-\frac{(a-1)a}{2},
> $$
> that is,
> $$
> a(a-1)>210.
> $$
>
> Again using $14\cdot 15=210$:
>
> - if $a \ge 16$, then $a(a-1)>210$, so choosing $a-1$ gives the second player a strict advantage;
> - if $a=15$, then $a(a-1)=210$ exactly, so choosing $a-1=14$ leaves the two players exactly tied;
> - if $a \le 14$, choosing $a-1$ does not help the second player.
>
> Therefore the first-player choices that cannot be **strictly** beaten by an adjacent response are exactly $a=14$ (matched only by a tie from $b=15$) and $a=15$ (matched only by a tie from $b=14$). Every other choice of $a$ admits an adjacent response that strictly wins for the second player.
>
> ## 4. Check the choices $14$ and $15$
>
> ### First player chooses $15$
>
> The second player's natural nearby options are $14$ or $16$.
>
> If the second player chooses $14$: $x \le 14$ goes to $14$ and $x\ge15$ goes to $15$, so the first player gets
> $$
> \sum_{x=15}^{20}x = 15+16+17+18+19+20 = 105,
> $$
> and the second player gets
> $$
> \sum_{x=1}^{14}x = 105.
> $$
> This is an exact tie.
>
> If the second player chooses $16$: $x\le15$ goes to $15$ and $x\ge16$ goes to $16$, so the first player gets
> $$
> \sum_{x=1}^{15}x = 120,
> $$
> and the second player gets
> $$
> \sum_{x=16}^{20}x = 90.
> $$
> This is worse for the second player than choosing $14$.
>
> Any choice farther from $15$ is even worse for the second player: moving the response one step further away hands an additional block of $x$-values to the first player while turning what would have been the tie point into a strict loss. So the best response to $15$ is $14$, yielding a tie.
>
> ### First player chooses $14$
>
> By the mirror-image argument, the second player's natural options are $13$ or $15$.
>
> If the second player chooses $13$: the second player gets $\sum_{x=1}^{13}x=91$ and the first player gets $\sum_{x=14}^{20}x=119$, so the first player wins outright.
>
> If the second player chooses $15$: $x\le14$ goes to $14$ and $x\ge15$ goes to $15$, giving the first player $\sum_{x=1}^{14}x=105$ and the second player $\sum_{x=15}^{20}x=105$ — again an exact tie.
>
> So the best response to $14$ is $15$, again yielding a tie, and no adjacent or more distant response does better for the second player.
>
> ## 5. First versus second
>
> - If you go first and choose any number other than $14$ or $15$, the second player can respond in a way that gives them a strict positive expected advantage.
> - If you go first and choose $14$, the second player's best response is $15$, and the game is tied in expected value.
> - If you go first and choose $15$, the second player's best response is $14$, and the game is again tied.
> - If instead you go second, and your opponent chooses anything other than $14$ or $15$, you can gain a strict positive expected advantage by choosing the appropriate adjacent number.
> - If your opponent chooses $14$ or $15$, you can respond with the other of the two and force a tie.
>
> Thus going second is at least as good as going first in every case, and strictly better whenever the first player's choice is not $14$ or $15$.
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> You should **choose to go second**.
>
> If you are forced to go first, there are exactly two optimal choices,
> $$
> \boxed{14 \text{ or } 15},
> $$
> each of which guarantees at least a tie in expected value, and no other first choice does that.
