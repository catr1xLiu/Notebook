> [!example] Question 37 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

There are 200 one-dollar coins, each with an equal probability of going into a pot. You can bid for the pot (the winner gets all the coins, but does not know exactly how many coins are in it). The person who offers the highest bid wins the auction. What would your optimal bid be if there is 1 competitor? What about with 10 competitors? Now, suppose only two people are bidding and both are using their best strategies, but I have the advantage of knowing how many of the first 10 coins are in the pot. What bidding strategies should we each use, how much should you bid, and what is your expected payoff?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=38)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> Let $V$ denote the number of one-dollar coins in the pot. Since each of the $200$ coins independently goes into the pot with probability $\tfrac12$,
>
> $$
> V \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(200,\tfrac12).
> $$
>
> Hence
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[V]=200\cdot \tfrac12=100,
> \qquad
> \operatorname{Var}(V)=200\cdot \tfrac12\cdot \tfrac12=50.
> $$
>
> The auction is a **first-price sealed-bid auction**: the highest bidder pays his own bid and receives the pot, so the winner's payoff is
>
> $$
> V-b,
> $$
>
> where $b$ is his bid.
>
> We assume bidders are risk-neutral and use symmetric equilibrium bidding when they have the same information.
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. Optimal bid with identical uninformed bidders
>
> ### General principle
>
> Suppose there are $n$ bidders total. Each bidder has the same valuation distribution as the others, and here all bidders value the object at the same random amount $V$, but nobody observes $V$ before bidding. Since no bidder has any private information, everyone is strategically identical.
>
> If all other bidders use the deterministic bid $b$, then:
>
> - bidding below $b$ loses for sure;
> - bidding above $b$ wins for sure but gives expected payoff $\mathbb{E}[V]-b'$, where $b'$ is your own bid.
>
> Thus in equilibrium the common bid must be such that no one wants to raise slightly above it. That requires
>
> $$
> b=\mathbb{E}[V]=100.
> $$
>
> Indeed:
>
> - if the common bid were less than $100$, a bidder could outbid by a tiny amount and obtain positive expected profit;
> - if the common bid were greater than $100$, winning would give negative expected profit.
>
> So with any number of symmetric uninformed bidders, the equilibrium bid is
>
> $$
> b^*=100.
> $$
>
> At this bid, expected profit is $0$.
>
> ---
>
> ### Case: 1 competitor
>
> "1 competitor" means $2$ bidders total. Therefore the equilibrium bid is $100$.
>
> ### Case: 10 competitors
>
> "10 competitors" means $11$ bidders total. The same logic applies, so the equilibrium bid is again $100$.
>
> Thus:
>
> - with $1$ competitor: bid $100$;
> - with $10$ competitors: bid $100$.
>
> In both cases the expected payoff is $0$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Two bidders, one knows the first 10 coins
>
> Now there are only two bidders.
>
> - Player $A$ knows how many of the first $10$ coins are in the pot.
> - Player $B$ knows nothing beyond the prior distribution.
>
> Let $K$ be the number of the first $10$ coins that are in the pot. Then
>
> $$
> K \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(10,\tfrac12).
> $$
>
> Let $R$ be the number among the remaining $190$ coins that are in the pot. Then
>
> $$
> R \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(190,\tfrac12),
> \qquad
> \mathbb{E}[R]=95.
> $$
>
> Also,
>
> $$
> V=K+R.
> $$
>
> So if Player $A$ observes $K=k$, his conditional expected valuation is
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[V\mid K=k]=k+\mathbb{E}[R]=k+95.
> $$
>
> Thus Player $A$'s possible expected values, as $k$ ranges over $0,1,\dots,10$, are
>
> $$
> 95,96,\dots,105.
> $$
>
> Player $B$ only knows
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[V]=100.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Why Player B cannot safely bid the unconditional mean
>
> If Player $B$ bids some amount $b$, Player $A$ will outbid him whenever $A$'s conditional value exceeds $b$, that is, whenever $95+k>b$, and will let $B$ win otherwise. So whenever Player $B$ wins, it is because Player $A$'s private information told him the pot was not attractive enough at price $b$ — precisely when the pot tends to be *smaller* than average. This is the winner's curse: winning itself is bad news about $V$.
>
> Concretely, suppose Player $B$ bids high enough that Player $A$ only outbids him when $K$ exceeds some cutoff $m\ge1$, so $B$ wins whenever $K\le m$. Player $B$'s conditional expected value upon winning is then
>
> $$
> 95+\mathbb{E}[K\mid K\le m].
> $$
>
> Because the binomial distribution of $K$ places positive probability on every value below $m$ as well as at $m$, this conditional mean is always **strictly less than** $m$ whenever $m\ge1$. So a bid corresponding to any cutoff $m\ge1$ can never break even: the pot Player $B$ actually wins is, on average, worth less than what such a bid would require him to pay.
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. Player B's equilibrium bid
>
> Bidding high enough to win whenever $K\le m$ for any $m\ge1$ exposes Player $B$ to a losing winner's curse, by Step 3. So the only bid that avoids this trap is the one corresponding to the cutoff $m=0$: Player $B$ bids exactly
>
> $$
> b_B=95,
> $$
>
> which is $\mathbb{E}[V\mid K=0]$, the pot's value in the single worst-case scenario for Player $A$'s private information.
>
> At this bid:
>
> - if $K=0$ (probability $2^{-10}$), Player $A$'s conditional value is exactly $95$, so he is indifferent to outbidding, and Player $B$'s expected value conditional on winning is exactly $95$, matching his bid;
> - if $K\ge1$, Player $A$'s conditional value $95+K$ strictly exceeds $95$, so Player $A$ strictly prefers to outbid, and wins the auction.
>
> Any bid strictly above $95$ would instead let Player $A$ win only above some higher cutoff $m\ge1$, and Step 3 shows that would leave Player $B$ losing money whenever he wins. So $b_B=95$ is the unique equilibrium bid at which Player $B$ neither forfeits value nor exposes himself to the winner's curse.
>
> ---
>
> ## 5. Player A's strategy and expected profit
>
> Player $A$'s strategy is simple: outbid Player $B$'s $95$ whenever $K\ge1$, and let $B$ win when $K=0$.
>
> Since $K=0$ occurs with probability $2^{-10}=\tfrac1{1024}$, Player $A$ wins the auction with probability
>
> $$
> 1-\frac1{1024}=\frac{1023}{1024},
> $$
>
> paying just above $95$ each time, and receiving a pot worth $95+K$ in expectation given his signal.
>
> Player $A$'s expected profit is therefore
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}\bigl[(95+K-95)\cdot\mathbf 1_{\{K\ge1\}}\bigr]
> =\mathbb{E}\bigl[K\cdot\mathbf 1_{\{K\ge1\}}\bigr]
> =\mathbb{E}[K]
> =10\cdot\frac12=5,
> $$
>
> since including or excluding the $K=0$ term makes no difference to $\mathbb{E}[K\cdot\mathbf 1_{\{K\ge1\}}]$, as that term contributes zero either way.
>
> ---
>
> ## Final answers
>
> ### Without extra information
>
> - With $1$ competitor, the equilibrium bid is $100$, and the expected payoff is $0$.
> - With $10$ competitors, the equilibrium bid is $100$, and the expected payoff is $0$.
>
> ### Two bidders, one knows the number among the first 10 coins
>
> - Let $K$ be the number of first-$10$ coins in the pot, so $K\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(10,\tfrac12)$.
> - The informed bidder's conditional expected value given his signal is $95+K$.
> - The uninformed bidder's equilibrium bid is $95$, the pot's value in the worst case for the informed bidder's signal; any higher bid falls victim to the winner's curse.
> - The informed bidder outbids whenever $K\ge1$, and lets the uninformed bidder win only when $K=0$.
> - The uninformed bidder's expected payoff is $0$.
> - The informed bidder's expected payoff is $\mathbb{E}[K]=5$.
>
> So the informational advantage is worth exactly $5$ dollars in expected profit to the informed bidder.
