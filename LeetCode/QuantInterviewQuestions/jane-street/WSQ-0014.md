> [!example] Question 14 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

Compare the expected value of (1) the square of the outcome when rolling one die, (2) the product of the outcomes when rolling two dice, and (3) the square of the median outcome when rolling five dice.

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=15)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let:
>
> - $X$ be the result of rolling one fair six-sided die.
> - $Y_1, Y_2$ be the results of rolling two independent fair six-sided dice.
> - $Z_1,\dots,Z_5$ be the results of rolling five independent fair six-sided dice, and let $M$ be their median.
>
> We compare:
>
> $$E[X^2], \qquad E[Y_1Y_2], \qquad E[M^2].$$
>
> ## 1. Expectation of the square of one die
>
> Since $X$ is uniform on $\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$,
>
> $$
> E[X^2] = \frac{1^2+2^2+3^2+4^2+5^2+6^2}{6}.
> $$
>
> Compute:
>
> $$
> 1+4+9+16+25+36 = 91,
> $$
>
> so
>
> $$
> E[X^2] = \frac{91}{6}.
> $$
>
> ## 2. Expectation of the product of two dice
>
> Because $Y_1$ and $Y_2$ are independent,
>
> $$
> E[Y_1Y_2] = E[Y_1]E[Y_2].
> $$
>
> Now
>
> $$
> E[Y_1] = E[Y_2] = \frac{1+2+3+4+5+6}{6} = \frac{21}{6} = \frac{7}{2}.
> $$
>
> Hence
>
> $$
> E[Y_1Y_2] = \left(\frac{7}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{49}{4}.
> $$
>
> ## 3. Expectation of the square of the median of five dice
>
> Let $M$ be the median of five i.i.d. fair dice rolls. We first find the distribution of $M$.
>
> For $k \in \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$, the event $M \le k$ means at least $3$ of the $5$ dice are at most $k$. Since for each die,
>
> $$
> P(Z_i \le k) = \frac{k}{6},
> $$
>
> we have
>
> $$
> P(M \le k) = \sum_{j=3}^{5} \binom{5}{j}\left(\frac{k}{6}\right)^j\left(1-\frac{k}{6}\right)^{5-j}.
> $$
>
> Then
>
> $$
> P(M=k) = P(M\le k)-P(M\le k-1).
> $$
>
> We compute these cumulative probabilities.
>
> ### For $k=1$
>
> Here $p=\frac16$, $q=\frac56$, and all three terms $j=3,4,5$ must be included:
>
> $$
> P(M\le 1)=\binom{5}{3}\left(\frac16\right)^3\left(\frac56\right)^2+\binom{5}{4}\left(\frac16\right)^4\left(\frac56\right)^1+\binom{5}{5}\left(\frac16\right)^5.
> $$
>
> In terms of a common denominator $6^5=7776$, the three terms are $250$, $25$, and $1$, so
>
> $$
> P(M\le 1)=\frac{250+25+1}{7776}=\frac{276}{7776}.
> $$
>
> Thus
>
> $$
> P(M=1)=\frac{276}{7776}=\frac{23}{648}.
> $$
>
> ### For $k=2$
>
> Here $p=\frac13$, $q=\frac23$:
>
> $$
> P(M\le 2)=\binom{5}{3}\left(\frac13\right)^3\left(\frac23\right)^2+\binom{5}{4}\left(\frac13\right)^4\left(\frac23\right)^1+\binom{5}{5}\left(\frac13\right)^5
> =\frac{1632}{7776}=\frac{17}{81}.
> $$
>
> So
>
> $$
> P(M=2)=P(M\le2)-P(M\le1)=\frac{1632-276}{7776}=\frac{1356}{7776}=\frac{113}{648}.
> $$
>
> ### For $k=3$
>
> By symmetry,
>
> $$
> P(M\le3)=\frac12,
> $$
>
> so
>
> $$
> P(M=3)=\frac12-\frac{1632}{7776}=\frac{3888-1632}{7776}=\frac{2256}{7776}=\frac{47}{162}.
> $$
>
> ### By symmetry of the die outcomes
>
> The median distribution is symmetric about $3.5$, so
>
> $$
> P(M=6)=P(M=1), \qquad P(M=5)=P(M=2), \qquad P(M=4)=P(M=3).
> $$
>
> Thus
>
> $$
> P(M=1)=P(M=6)=\frac{276}{7776}=\frac{23}{648},
> $$
>
> $$
> P(M=2)=P(M=5)=\frac{1356}{7776}=\frac{113}{648},
> $$
>
> $$
> P(M=3)=P(M=4)=\frac{2256}{7776}=\frac{47}{162}.
> $$
>
> As a check, twice their sum is
>
> $$
> 2(276+1356+2256)=2(3888)=7776,
> $$
>
> which confirms the six probabilities sum to $1$.
>
> Now compute $E[M^2]$:
>
> $$
> E[M^2]
> = \sum_{k=1}^6 k^2 P(M=k)
> = \frac{1^2(276)+2^2(1356)+3^2(2256)+4^2(2256)+5^2(1356)+6^2(276)}{7776}.
> $$
>
> Compute the numerator:
>
> $$
> 276 + 5424 + 20304 + 36096 + 33900 + 9936 = 105936.
> $$
>
> Therefore
>
> $$
> E[M^2] = \frac{105936}{7776}.
> $$
>
> Reducing this fraction (dividing numerator and denominator by $48$) gives
>
> $$
> E[M^2] = \frac{2207}{162} \approx 13.6235.
> $$
>
> ## 4. Comparison
>
> Now compare:
>
> $$
> E[X^2] = \frac{91}{6} \approx 15.167,
> $$
>
> $$
> E[Y_1Y_2] = \frac{49}{4} = 12.25,
> $$
>
> $$
> E[M^2] = \frac{2207}{162} \approx 13.6235.
> $$
>
> Therefore,
>
> $$
> E[Y_1Y_2] < E[M^2] < E[X^2].
> $$
>
> ## Final answer
>
> $$
> E[\text{(one die)}^2] = \frac{91}{6}, \qquad
> E[\text{(product of two dice)}] = \frac{49}{4}, \qquad
> E[\text{(median of five dice)}^2] = \frac{2207}{162}.
> $$
>
> Thus,
>
> $$
> \frac{49}{4} < \frac{2207}{162} < \frac{91}{6}.
> $$
>
> So the expectation of the product of two dice is smallest, the expectation of the square of the median of five dice is in the middle, and the expectation of the square of one die is largest.
