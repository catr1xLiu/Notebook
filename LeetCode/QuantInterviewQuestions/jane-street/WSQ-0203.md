> [!example] Question 203 · Jane Street
> ## Trading Intern Interview

Four 50-sided dice are rolled so that the numbers are all different, then assigned randomly to players A, B, C, and D. The players with the highest and lowest numbers pair up, as do the two with the middle numbers. The team with the higher total pays the team with the lower total the difference. You are one of the players. Someone offers you a way to fix the game so that you start with a given number from 1 to 50. Question 1: What is the best number to pick? Question 2: Assuming you choose X as the best number, and there is an auction to sell the hack that lets you start with X, how should you bid? (Is this just asking for the expected value of the hack, or is there more to consider about bidding strategies?)

> ## Solution
>
> Let the four distinct rolled numbers, in increasing order, be
> $$
> a<b<c<d.
> $$
> After random assignment to players, the rules pair the lowest with the highest and the two middle numbers together. Thus the two team totals are
> $$
> a+d \qquad \text{and} \qquad b+c.
> $$
> Since
> $$
> (a+d)-(b+c)=(d-c)-(b-a),
> $$
> the payment is
> $$
> \bigl|(a+d)-(b+c)\bigr|=\bigl|(d-c)-(b-a)\bigr|.
> $$
> If you are one of the four players and your assigned number is one of $a,b,c,d$, then:
>
> - if you hold $a$ or $d$, you are on team $a+d$;
> - if you hold $b$ or $c$, you are on team $b+c$.
>
> So your payoff is $+\Delta$ if your team has the larger sum, and $-\Delta$ if your team has the smaller sum, where
> $$
> \Delta=\bigl|(a+d)-(b+c)\bigr|.
> $$
>
> We are allowed to fix our own number to some value $x\in\{1,\dots,50\}$, with the other three numbers chosen uniformly from the remaining $49$ values.
>
> The task is to maximize the expected payoff as a function of $x$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. Express the payoff given $x$
>
> Fix $x$. Let the other three distinct numbers be
> $$
> y_1<y_2<y_3.
> $$
> There are four possible ranks for $x$ among the four numbers.
>
> ### Case 1: $x<y_1<y_2<y_3$
>
> Then the ordered quadruple is
> $$
> a=x,\quad b=y_1,\quad c=y_2,\quad d=y_3.
> $$
> You are on team $a+d$, and your payoff is
> $$
> (x+y_3)-(y_1+y_2)=x+y_3-y_1-y_2.
> $$
>
> ### Case 2: $y_1<x<y_2<y_3$
>
> Then
> $$
> a=y_1,\quad b=x,\quad c=y_2,\quad d=y_3.
> $$
> You are on team $b+c$, so your payoff is
> $$
> (x+y_2)-(y_1+y_3).
> $$
>
> ### Case 3: $y_1<y_2<x<y_3$
>
> Then
> $$
> a=y_1,\quad b=y_2,\quad c=x,\quad d=y_3,
> $$
> and your payoff is
> $$
> (y_2+x)-(y_1+y_3).
> $$
>
> ### Case 4: $y_1<y_2<y_3<x$
>
> Then
> $$
> a=y_1,\quad b=y_2,\quad c=y_3,\quad d=x,
> $$
> and your payoff is
> $$
> (y_1+x)-(y_2+y_3).
> $$
>
> In every case the payoff has the same shape: you receive $x$ plus the two numbers on your own team's side of the pairing, minus the two numbers on the opposing side. Rather than track ranks symbolically, it is simplest to condition on how many of the other three numbers fall below $x$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Compute the expected payoff
>
> Let
>
> - $m=x-1$ be the count of numbers below $x$,
> - $n=50-x$ be the count of numbers above $x$.
>
> The other three numbers are a uniformly random $3$-subset of the remaining $49$ numbers, so the probability of having exactly $k$ numbers below $x$ is
> $$
> \frac{\binom{m}{k}\binom{n}{3-k}}{\binom{49}{3}}, \qquad k=0,1,2,3.
> $$
>
> We now compute the conditional expected payoff in each case.
>
> ---
>
> ### Case $k=0$: all three other numbers are above $x$
>
> Let the chosen numbers above $x$ be $u_1<u_2<u_3$. From Case 1 above, the payoff is
> $$
> x+u_3-u_1-u_2.
> $$
> Hence
> $$
> \mathbb E[\pi\mid k=0]
> =
> x+\mathbb E[u_3-u_1-u_2].
> $$
>
> For a uniform $3$-subset of $\{x+1,\dots,50\}$, write $N=n=50-x$. The expected order statistics from a uniform $3$-subset of $\{1,\dots,N\}$ are
> $$
> \mathbb E[U_{(1)}]=\frac{N+1}{4},\quad
> \mathbb E[U_{(2)}]=\frac{N+1}{2},\quad
> \mathbb E[U_{(3)}]=\frac{3(N+1)}{4}.
> $$
> After shifting by $x$, this gives
> $$
> \mathbb E[u_1]=x+\frac{n+1}{4},\quad
> \mathbb E[u_2]=x+\frac{n+1}{2},\quad
> \mathbb E[u_3]=x+\frac{3(n+1)}{4}.
> $$
> Therefore
> $$
> \mathbb E[\pi\mid k=0]
> =
> x+\left(x+\frac{3(n+1)}{4}\right)-\left(x+\frac{n+1}{4}\right)-\left(x+\frac{n+1}{2}\right)=0.
> $$
>
> So
> $$
> \mathbb E[\pi\mid k=0]=0.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### Case $k=3$: all three other numbers are below $x$
>
> By symmetry, if the three chosen numbers below $x$ are $v_1<v_2<v_3$, the payoff is
> $$
> x+v_1-v_2-v_3,
> $$
> and the same order-statistic calculation gives
> $$
> \mathbb E[\pi\mid k=3]=0.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### Case $k=1$: one number below, two above
>
> Let the chosen numbers be
> $$
> \ell<x<u_1<u_2.
> $$
> From Case 2, the payoff is
> $$
> x+u_1-\ell-u_2=x-\ell-(u_2-u_1).
> $$
> Now $\ell$ is uniformly distributed on $\{1,\dots,x-1\}$, so
> $$
> \mathbb E[\ell]=\frac{x}{2}.
> $$
> Also, for a uniform $2$-subset $u_1<u_2$ of $\{x+1,\dots,50\}$, with $n=50-x$, the expected gap is
> $$
> \mathbb E[u_2-u_1]=\frac{n+1}{3}.
> $$
> Therefore
> $$
> \mathbb E[\pi\mid k=1]
> =
> x-\frac{x}{2}-\frac{n+1}{3}
> =
> \frac{x}{2}-\frac{51-x}{3}
> =
> \frac{5x-102}{6}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### Case $k=2$: two numbers below, one above
>
> Let
> $$
> \ell_1<\ell_2<x<u.
> $$
> From Case 3, the ordered quadruple is $a=\ell_1,\ b=\ell_2,\ c=x,\ d=u$. You are on team $b+c$, whose sum is $\ell_2+x$, against $\ell_1+u$. So the payoff is
> $$
> (\ell_2+x)-(\ell_1+u)=x+\ell_2-\ell_1-u.
> $$
>
> For a uniform $2$-subset $\ell_1<\ell_2$ of $\{1,\dots,m\}$ with $m=x-1$,
> $$
> \mathbb E[\ell_1]=\frac{m+1}{3}=\frac{x}{3},\qquad
> \mathbb E[\ell_2]=\frac{2(m+1)}{3}=\frac{2x}{3},
> $$
> so
> $$
> \mathbb E[\ell_2-\ell_1]=\frac{x}{3}.
> $$
> Also $u$ is uniform on $\{x+1,\dots,50\}$, so
> $$
> \mathbb E[u]=\frac{x+51}{2}.
> $$
> Hence
> $$
> \mathbb E[\pi\mid k=2]
> =
> x+\frac{x}{3}-\frac{x+51}{2}
> =
> \frac{4x}{3}-\frac{x+51}{2}
> =
> \frac{8x-3x-153}{6}
> =
> \frac{5x-153}{6}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Assemble the expectation
>
> Therefore
> $$
> \mathbb E[\pi(x)]
> =
> \frac{\binom{m}{1}\binom{n}{2}}{\binom{49}{3}}\cdot \frac{5x-102}{6}
> +
> \frac{\binom{m}{2}\binom{n}{1}}{\binom{49}{3}}\cdot \frac{5x-153}{6},
> $$
> where
> $$
> m=x-1,\qquad n=50-x.
> $$
> Since the $k=0$ and $k=3$ terms contribute $0$, this is
> $$
> \mathbb E[\pi(x)]
> =
> \frac{1}{6\binom{49}{3}}
> \left[
> (x-1)\binom{50-x}{2}(5x-102)
> +
> \binom{x-1}{2}(50-x)(5x-153)
> \right].
> $$
>
> This simplifies substantially. Using
> $$
> \binom{50-x}{2}=\frac{(50-x)(49-x)}{2},\qquad
> \binom{x-1}{2}=\frac{(x-1)(x-2)}{2},
> $$
> we obtain
> $$
> \mathbb E[\pi(x)]
> =
> \frac{(x-1)(50-x)}{12\binom{49}{3}}
> \left[
> (49-x)(5x-102)+(x-2)(5x-153)
> \right].
> $$
> Now expand:
> $$
> (49-x)(5x-102)=-5x^2+347x-4998,
> $$
> and
> $$
> (x-2)(5x-153)=5x^2-163x+306.
> $$
> Adding gives
> $$
> 184x-4692=184(x-25.5)=92(2x-51).
> $$
> Since
> $$
> 12\binom{49}{3}=12\cdot 18424=221088,
> $$
> we get
> $$
> \mathbb E[\pi(x)]
> =
> \frac{92(x-1)(50-x)(2x-51)}{221088}
> =
> \frac{23(x-1)(50-x)(2x-51)}{55272}.
> $$
>
> So we must maximize
> $$
> f(x)=(x-1)(50-x)(2x-51)
> $$
> over integers $x=1,\dots,50$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. Maximize over $x$
>
> Note first:
>
> - for $x<25.5$, we have $2x-51<0$, so the expectation is negative;
> - for $x>25.5$, it is positive.
>
> Thus the optimal $x$ must be above the middle.
>
> Evaluate near the top. Since
> $$
> f(50)=0,\qquad f(49)=48\cdot 1\cdot 47=2256,
> $$
> and
> $$
> f(48)=47\cdot 2\cdot 45=4230,
> $$
> $$
> f(47)=46\cdot 3\cdot 43=5934,
> $$
> $$
> f(46)=45\cdot 4\cdot 41=7380,
> $$
> $$
> f(45)=44\cdot 5\cdot 39=8580,
> $$
> $$
> f(44)=43\cdot 6\cdot 37=9546,
> $$
> $$
> f(43)=42\cdot 7\cdot 35=10290,
> $$
> $$
> f(42)=41\cdot 8\cdot 33=10824,
> $$
> $$
> f(41)=40\cdot 9\cdot 31=11160,
> $$
> $$
> f(40)=39\cdot 10\cdot 29=11310,
> $$
> $$
> f(39)=38\cdot 11\cdot 27=11286.
> $$
> So the maximum occurs at
> $$
> x=40.
> $$
>
> Thus the best number to fix is
> $$
> \boxed{40}.
> $$
>
> The expected payoff there is
> $$
> \mathbb E[\pi(40)]
> =
> \frac{23(39)(10)(29)}{55272}
> =
> \frac{260130}{55272}
> =
> \frac{43355}{9212}
> \approx 4.7064.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 5. Interpretation for the auction
>
> The hack gives you an expected gain of
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{43355}{9212}\approx 4.7064}
> $$
> relative to playing without the hack.
>
> This raises the question of what to bid, and the answer is not purely "quote the expected value." A single-item auction for a hack like this is a strategic setting, not a simple valuation exercise:
>
> - if it is a first-price sealed-bid auction against other bidders who also value the hack near its expected worth, bidding the full expected value forgoes any surplus — a rational bidder shades the bid below $4.7064$ to leave room for profit if the bid wins;
> - if it is a second-price (Vickrey) auction, bidding your true value $4.7064$ is the dominant strategy, since the price paid is set by the next-highest bid rather than your own;
> - if you are the only bidder or the seller will accept any nonnegative offer, you should bid only marginally above $0$, not your full valuation.
>
> So the expected value $43355/9212\approx 4.7064$ is the **ceiling** on any sensible bid — you should never bid above it, since that would make the hack a losing proposition on average — but the actual bid also depends on the auction format and the competition, not on the expected value alone.
>
> ---
>
> ## Final answer
>
> 1. The optimal fixed starting number is
> $$
> \boxed{40}.
> $$
>
> 2. Its expected value is
> $$
> \boxed{\mathbb E[\text{payoff with }40]=\frac{43355}{9212}\approx 4.7064}.
> $$
> This is the maximum sensible bid for the hack; the actual bid should be shaded below this ceiling according to the auction format (first-price versus second-price) and the expected competition, rather than simply bidding the expected value itself.
