> [!example] Question 408 · Jane Street
> ## Trader Interview

You and a friend are betting on individual games of the World Series. For each game, if your team wins, you win a certain positive amount of money, and if your team loses, you lose that same amount. You and your friend want to construct a betting scheme so that you will win \$1000 if your team wins the series and lose \$1000 if your team loses the series, regardless of the final game score (e.g., 4-3, 2-4, etc.). How much should you bet on the first game?

> ## Solution
>
> Let a series state be $(w,\ell)$: your team has $w$ wins and $\ell$ losses, and the series ends at four. Let $f(w,\ell)$ be the net amount you must be up at state $(w,\ell)$ so that the scheme finishes at exactly $+1000$ dollars if your team wins the series and $-1000$ dollars if it loses.
>
> The boundary conditions are
>
> $$
> f(4,\ell)=+1000, \qquad f(w,4)=-1000.
> $$
>
> If you bet $b(w,\ell)$ on the next game, winning must move you to $f(w+1,\ell)$ and losing to $f(w,\ell+1)$:
>
> $$
> f(w+1,\ell)=f(w,\ell)+b(w,\ell), \qquad f(w,\ell+1)=f(w,\ell)-b(w,\ell).
> $$
>
> Adding and subtracting these equations gives
>
> $$
> f(w,\ell)=\frac{f(w+1,\ell)+f(w,\ell+1)}{2}, \qquad b(w,\ell)=\frac{f(w+1,\ell)-f(w,\ell+1)}{2}.
> $$
>
> So every interior value of $f$ is the average of its two successors, and $f$ is determined by backward induction from the boundary. A function satisfying this averaging recursion with boundary values $\pm 1000$ is
>
> $$
> f(w,\ell)=1000\bigl(2p(w,\ell)-1\bigr),
> $$
>
> where $p(w,\ell)$ is the probability that a fair coin produces four team wins before four losses starting from $(w,\ell)$ — because $p$ satisfies the same averaging recursion with boundary values $1$ and $0$.
>
> ## The first bet
>
> At the start, symmetry gives $f(0,0)=0$ and $f(0,1)=-f(1,0)$, so
>
> $$
> b(0,0)=\frac{f(1,0)-f(0,1)}{2}=f(1,0).
> $$
>
> To find $f(1,0)$: from state $(1,0)$ your team needs $3$ more wins before suffering $4$ losses. Imagine the next six games are all played regardless; your team takes the series exactly when at least $3$ of those $6$ are wins:
>
> $$
> p(1,0)=\frac{\binom{6}{3}+\binom{6}{4}+\binom{6}{5}+\binom{6}{6}}{2^6}=\frac{20+15+6+1}{64}=\frac{42}{64}=\frac{21}{32}.
> $$
>
> Therefore
>
> $$
> f(1,0)=1000\left(2\cdot\frac{21}{32}-1\right)=1000\cdot\frac{10}{32}=312.50,
> $$
>
> and the amount to bet on the first game is
>
> $$
> b(0,0)=\boxed{312.50}
> $$
>
> dollars — equivalently, $1000\cdot\binom{6}{3}/2^{6}$.
>
> ## Consistency check
>
> The scheme stays feasible all the way down the bracket: each later bet is again half the gap between neighboring values of $f$, growing as the series tightens. At a deciding seventh game the state is $(3,3)$, where $f(4,3)=+1000$ and $f(3,4)=-1000$, so the bet is the full $1000$ dollars — exactly as it must be, since that single game then decides the series.
