> [!example] Question 32 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

Given cards numbered from 1 to 9 placed face up and two dice, you repeatedly throw the dice. If the sum is greater than 9, you throw again. If the sum is less than or equal to 9, you have two options: flip down the card with the number equal to the sum, or flip down the two cards corresponding to each die value. For example, if you roll a 3 and a 4, you can flip down the 7, or both the 3 and 4. If the card for the sum is already flipped, you must flip down each die's card if possible; if one of those is already flipped, you must flip the sum (if possible). The game ends when no move is possible, and your score is the number of flipped cards. What is the best strategy?

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> This is a finite-state dynamic programming problem.
>
> At every stage, the game state is determined by the set of cards already flipped down. Since the cards are numbered $1,\dots,9$, a state is any subset of $\{1,\dots,9\}$. For each state, one wants the strategy that maximizes the expected final score.
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. Reformulating the game
>
> Let $S \subseteq \{1,\dots,9\}$ be the set of cards already flipped down.
>
> A dice throw produces ordered values $(a,b)$ with $a,b \in \{1,\dots,6\}$, each equally likely. Let $s=a+b$.
>
> - If $s>9$, the throw is ignored and one throws again.
> - If $s\le 9$, then one must make a move if possible:
>   - flip down card $s$, provided $s\notin S$;
>   - or flip down both cards $a$ and $b$, provided $a\ne b$ and both $a,b\notin S$.
>
> If no legal move exists, the game ends immediately, and the score is $|S|$.
>
> The objective is to maximize the expected final score.
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Effective probabilities of relevant throws
>
> Because all throws with sum $>9$ are discarded and rerolled, the only relevant outcomes are those with sum at most $9$.
>
> Among the $36$ ordered outcomes of two fair dice, the numbers of outcomes with sums $2,\dots,9$ are
>
> $$
> 1,2,3,4,5,6,5,4.
> $$
>
> Hence the total number of relevant outcomes is
>
> $$
> 1+2+3+4+5+6+5+4 = 30.
> $$
>
> Therefore, conditional on obtaining a usable throw $(a,b)$ with $a+b\le 9$, the probabilities are proportional to these counts. Equivalently, each ordered pair $(a,b)$ with $a+b\le 9$ occurs with conditional probability
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{30}.
> $$
>
> So the process may be viewed as repeatedly drawing uniformly from the $30$ ordered pairs $(a,b)$ satisfying $a+b\le 9$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Dynamic programming recurrence
>
> Let $V(S)$ denote the maximum expected final score starting from state $S$.
>
> If a relevant roll $(a,b)$ occurs, let $s=a+b$. The legal actions are:
>
> - **Take the sum card:** possible if $s\notin S$, giving new state $S\cup\{s\}$.
> - **Take the two dice cards:** possible if $a\ne b$ and $a,b\notin S$, giving new state $S\cup\{a,b\}$.
>
> If both are possible, choose the one giving larger future value. If neither is possible, the game stops with score $|S|$.
>
> Thus
>
> $$
> V(S)=\frac{1}{30}\sum_{\substack{1\le a,b\le 6\\a+b\le 9}} W(S;a,b),
> $$
>
> where
>
> $$
> W(S;a,b)=
> \begin{cases}
> \max\!\bigl(V(S\cup\{a+b\}),\,V(S\cup\{a,b\})\bigr), & \text{if both moves are legal},\\[4pt]
> V(S\cup\{a+b\}), & \text{if only the sum move is legal},\\[4pt]
> V(S\cup\{a,b\}), & \text{if only the two-card move is legal},\\[4pt]
> |S|, & \text{if no move is legal}.
> \end{cases}
> $$
>
> Here $S\cup\{a,b\}$ means adding one card if $a=b$, but in fact the two-card move is legal only when $a\ne b$.
>
> Since there are only $2^9=512$ states, this recurrence can be solved exactly by backward induction.
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. Structure of the optimal strategy
>
> The full table of $V(S)$ has $512$ entries, but the optimal decisions have a clear pattern once the recurrence is solved.
>
> From the empty starting state, taking the **sum card** is the better choice at most rolls, including every roll where the two-card move is not even legal (the doubles $(1,1)$, $(2,2)$, $(3,3)$, $(4,4)$, which force taking the sum). Taking **both dice cards** is strictly better only for four specific rolls: $(1,2)$, $(1,3)$, $(1,5)$, and $(2,4)$.
>
> This is somewhat counterintuitive at first glance, since taking two cards raises the score by $2$ immediately instead of $1$. But cards $7$, $8$, and $9$ can only ever be obtained by taking a sum — they can never appear as an individual die value — so keeping small cards available to help land on $7$, $8$, or $9$ later is frequently worth more than the extra card gained right away. The balance between these two effects depends on the current state, so the optimal move is genuinely state-dependent, and there is no single universal rule such as "always take two cards" or "always take the sum."
>
> ---
>
> ## 5. Initial-state decisions
>
> Let the initial state be $S=\varnothing$.
>
> For each unordered roll with sum at most $9$, comparing the two options using the solved recurrence gives the following optimal initial choices:
>
> - $(1,1)$: must flip $2$ (no two-card move is legal).
> - $(1,2)$: flip $1$ and $2$.
> - $(1,3)$: flip $1$ and $3$.
> - $(1,4)$: flip $5$.
> - $(1,5)$: flip $1$ and $5$.
> - $(1,6)$: flip $7$.
> - $(2,2)$: must flip $4$ (no two-card move is legal).
> - $(2,3)$: flip $5$.
> - $(2,4)$: flip $2$ and $4$.
> - $(2,5)$: flip $7$.
> - $(2,6)$: flip $8$.
> - $(3,3)$: must flip $6$ (no two-card move is legal).
> - $(3,4)$: flip $7$.
> - $(3,5)$: flip $8$.
> - $(3,6)$: flip $9$.
> - $(4,4)$: must flip $8$ (no two-card move is legal).
> - $(4,5)$: flip $9$.
>
> So, from the starting position, taking both dice cards is optimal only for the rolls $(1,2)$, $(1,3)$, $(1,5)$, and $(2,4)$; every other reachable roll is better served by taking the sum card, or is forced to by the rules when the two dice match.
>
> ---
>
> ## 6. Best expected score
>
> Solving the recurrence over all $512$ states by backward induction gives the optimal expected score from the empty state:
>
> $$
> V(\varnothing) \approx 5.14.
> $$
>
> So with best play, the expected number of cards flipped down is about
>
> $$
> \boxed{5.14}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 7. Final answer: optimal strategy
>
> The best strategy is obtained by dynamic programming on the set of flipped cards. The optimal move after a roll depends on the current state, so there is no universal one-line rule.
>
> However, the optimal policy is characterized as follows:
>
> - For each state $S$ and relevant roll $(a,b)$ with $a+b\le 9$, choose the legal move that maximizes $V(S\cup\{a+b\})$ or $V(S\cup\{a,b\})$, whichever is legal and larger.
> - From the initial state, the optimal choices are exactly the ones listed above: take the sum card at every roll except $(1,2)$, $(1,3)$, $(1,5)$, and $(2,4)$, where taking both dice cards is better.
> - The maximum expected final score is approximately $5.14$ cards.
>
> So the "best strategy" is the state-dependent dynamic-programming policy, with expected score
>
> $$
> \boxed{5.14\text{ cards (approximately)}}.
> $$
