> [!example] Question 115 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Trader Intern Interview

You and your friend play a betting game where you start with \$1 and your friend starts with N dollars, where N is a natural number. Each round, you 'flip a fair coin for the shortest current stack' (i.e., you win the shortest stack amount from your friend if it lands Heads, and your friend wins the shortest stack amount from you if it lands Tails). You buy back in for an extra \$1 every time you lose your current stack to your friend and the game continues, but if your friend loses all his stack to you, he doesn't buy back in and the game ends. (a) What is the expected number of rounds that this game will last? (b) What is the expected amount of profit that you walk away with? (c) What is the expected number of times you expect to buy back in for an iteration of the game for very large N? (d) In the real world, a U.S. penny has about a 51% chance of landing the same side up as before it was flipped, and about an 80% chance of landing Tails if spun on edge. Now, you may choose to use your real U.S. penny in the game: flip it with Heads up (51% Heads), Tails up (49% Heads), or spin it (20% Heads). Alternatively, you can use the perfectly fair coin (50% Heads). Your goal is always to maximize your expected profit. What is your optimal strategy and the expected profit? (e) The game also ends if you lose N dollars (i.e., you are down N dollars from your original \$1 buy-in), in which case your friend wins. What is the minimum probability of landing Heads the coin must have for you and your friend to have equal chances of winning the game?

> ## Solution
>
> Let your current stack be $x$ and your friend's current stack be $y$, with $x,y$ positive integers (after any needed buy-in, your stack is always at least $1$). Each round, the amount wagered is the smaller stack, $\min(x,y)$.
>
> Thus, writing $m=\min(x,y)$ for the wager, with heads-probability $p$: with probability $p$ your stack becomes $x+m$ and your friend's becomes $y-m$; with probability $1-p$ your stack becomes $x-m$ and your friend's becomes $y+m$. Whenever your own stack would hit $0$, you immediately buy back in for $\text{\textdollar}1$, resetting it to $1$; whenever your friend's stack hits $0$ instead, the game ends and you win.
>
> A crucial fact drives everything below: **your friend's stack $Y_t$ is a fair-game (martingale) process when $p=\tfrac12$.** Indeed, in either case above the wagered amount is $\min(x,y)$, and one checks directly that
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[\Delta Y] = \min(x,y)\cdot(1-2p),
> $$
>
> which is exactly $0$ when $p=\tfrac12$. Since your friend never buys back in, the game ends exactly when this process $Y_t$ is absorbed at $0$.
>
> ---
>
> ## (a) Expected number of rounds
>
> Because $Y_t$ is a fair-game process absorbed only at $0$ — with no opposing barrier above, since $Y_t$ can grow without bound every time you lose and rebuy — this is exactly the same situation as a simple symmetric random walk started at a positive integer with absorption at $0$. Classically, such a walk is absorbed with probability $1$ (the game ends almost surely), but its expected absorption time is **infinite**: the tail probability that the walk survives past round $T$ decays only like $1/\sqrt{T}$, and $\sum_T 1/\sqrt{T}$ diverges.
>
> The same heavy-tailed behavior occurs here, so
>
> $$
> \boxed{\mathbb{E}[\text{number of rounds}] = \infty.}
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## (b) Expected profit
>
> Let $B$ be the (random) number of times you buy back in over the course of the game. Track the total money physically in play, namely your stack plus your friend's stack. Every round this total is simply transferred between the two of you — except when you buy back in, which injects one brand-new dollar into the game.
>
> At the moment the game ends, your friend has $0$ and you hold everything: your final stack equals the original total in play, $1+N$, plus every dollar you injected via buy-ins:
>
> $$
> \text{final stack} = 1+N+B.
> $$
>
> Your net profit is your final stack minus everything you personally put in (your original $\text{\textdollar}1$ plus $B$ dollars of buy-ins):
>
> $$
> \text{profit} = (1+N+B) - (1+B) = N.
> $$
>
> This holds on *every* path on which the game ends, regardless of how many rounds it took or how many times you rebought. So the profit is not merely $N$ in expectation — it is exactly $N$ with certainty:
>
> $$
> \boxed{\text{profit} = N \text{ with certainty, so } \mathbb{E}[\text{profit}] = N.}
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## (c) Expected number of buy-ins for large $N$
>
> Each buy-in corresponds to a round in which you were the shorter (or equal) stack and lost. Since the number of rounds has infinite expectation by part (a), and buy-ins occur at a rate comparable to the number of rounds played (roughly half of the rounds where you hold the shorter stack), the expected number of buy-ins is likewise infinite:
>
> $$
> \boxed{\mathbb{E}[\text{number of buy-ins}] = \infty,}
> $$
>
> for every $N$, including in the large-$N$ limit.
>
> ---
>
> ## (d) Optimal choice of coin and expected profit
>
> Now each round you may choose a coin with heads-probability among
>
> $$
> p \in \{0.51,\;0.49,\;0.20,\;0.50\}.
> $$
>
> The accounting argument in part (b) never used the value of $p$ — it only used that wagers are zero-sum transfers, that you alone rebuy, and that the game eventually ends. So **whenever the game ends with probability $1$, your profit is exactly $N$ regardless of which coin you used.** The choice of $p$ therefore cannot be judged by comparing different finite profit values; instead, it matters only through whether — and how reliably — the game actually terminates.
>
> Recall $\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y]=\min(x,y)(1-2p)$ for your friend's stack. If $p<\tfrac12$ (the $0.49$ or $0.20$ options), this drift is strictly positive: your friend's stack is biased to grow. By the classical dichotomy for such asymmetric unbounded random walks, this gives a genuine positive probability that your friend's stack never returns to $0$ at all — that you are stuck rebuying forever with no resolution. That catastrophic possibility makes $0.49$ and $0.20$ strictly worse choices, and they should never be used.
>
> For $p\ge \tfrac12$ (the $0.50$ or $0.51$ options), the drift is zero or negative, and the game ends with probability $1$ exactly as in parts (a)–(b), giving profit $N$ with certainty either way. The two are tied in raw expected profit, but they are not equally well-behaved: at $p=0.50$ the process is a knife-edge fair game, ending almost surely but (as in part (a)) only after an infinite expected number of rounds; at $p=0.51$ the drift is strictly negative, which guarantees not only almost-sure termination but a **finite** expected number of rounds.
>
> So the optimal strategy is to always flip the penny heads-up ($p=0.51$) before each round:
>
> $$
> \boxed{\text{Use the heads-up penny, } p=0.51 \text{, every round.}}
> $$
>
> This achieves the same guaranteed profit of $N$ as any other terminating choice, while additionally guaranteeing the game resolves in finite expected time:
>
> $$
> \boxed{\mathbb{E}[\text{profit}] = N, \text{ achieved in finite expected time.}}
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## (e) Minimum heads probability for equal winning chances when you may also lose $N$
>
> Now the game stops in one of two ways:
>
> - you win if your friend's stack reaches $0$;
> - you lose if your cumulative loss reaches $N$ dollars relative to your original buy-in.
>
> Thus there are two absorbing barriers of equal magnitude $N$: one corresponding to winning your friend's entire $N$ dollars, and one corresponding to losing $N$ dollars yourself.
>
> This is exactly a gambler's ruin problem with step probabilities $p$ for heads and $q=1-p$ for tails, with two symmetric absorbing barriers equidistant ($N$) from the start.
>
> For equal winning chances, symmetry requires
>
> $$
> p = q,
> $$
>
> hence
>
> $$
> p = \frac12.
> $$
>
> Indeed, for gambler's ruin with two boundaries placed symmetrically around the start, the probability of hitting one boundary before the other is $\tfrac12$ if and only if $p=\tfrac12$; any $p>\tfrac12$ tips the odds in your favor, so $\tfrac12$ is also the *minimum* heads probability needed to guarantee you are not worse than even.
>
> Therefore the minimum heads probability needed for equal chances is
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac12.}
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Final Answers
>
> $$
> \boxed{(a)\ \mathbb{E}[\text{rounds}] = \infty}
> $$
>
> $$
> \boxed{(b)\ \text{profit} = N \text{ with certainty}}
> $$
>
> $$
> \boxed{(c)\ \mathbb{E}[\text{buy-ins}] = \infty}
> $$
>
> $$
> \boxed{(d)\ \text{Always use the }51\%\text{ heads flip; profit } =N \text{ with certainty, in finite expected time}}
> $$
>
> $$
> \boxed{(e)\ p_{\min} = \frac12}
> $$
