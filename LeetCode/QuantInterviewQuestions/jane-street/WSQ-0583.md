> [!example] Question 583 · Jane Street
> ## Assistant Trader Interview

You roll an n-sided die. You can accept the outcome and get paid that amount, or pay 1/n dollars to reroll. How much would you pay to play this game, and how much would you sell the game for? (Provide approximate numbers. You have five minutes to answer.)

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=584)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> Model the game as an optimal stopping problem.
>
> A fair $n$-sided die is rolled, with outcomes $1,2,\dots,n$. After seeing the outcome $k$, you may either
>
> - **stop** and take payoff $k$, or
> - **continue** by paying $\frac{1}{n}$ dollars and rolling again.
>
> We seek the value of the game under optimal play.
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. Value equation
>
> Let $V$ be the expected value of the game before a roll, assuming optimal decisions thereafter.
>
> After observing outcome $k$, the optimal action is:
>
> - take $k$, or
> - reroll, whose net value is $V-\frac{1}{n}$.
>
> Thus after seeing $k$, the value is
>
> $$
> \max\left(k,\; V-\frac{1}{n}\right).
> $$
>
> Averaging over all equally likely outcomes gives
>
> $$
> V=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^n \max\left(k,\; V-\frac{1}{n}\right).
> $$
>
> This implies a **threshold rule**: reroll whenever $k$ is below some cutoff, and stop when $k$ is at least that cutoff.
>
> Let
>
> $$
> t = V-\frac{1}{n}.
> $$
>
> Then you reroll if $k<t$ and keep the roll if $k\ge t$.
>
> Since outcomes are integers, suppose the cutoff is an integer $m$ such that
>
> - reroll on $1,2,\dots,m-1$,
> - keep on $m,m+1,\dots,n$.
>
> Then consistency requires
>
> $$
> m-1 < V-\frac{1}{n} \le m.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Compute $V$ for a given cutoff
>
> If the threshold is $m$, then
>
> $$
> V=\frac{1}{n}\left((m-1)\left(V-\frac{1}{n}\right)+\sum_{k=m}^n k\right).
> $$
>
> Now
>
> $$
> \sum_{k=m}^n k = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}-\frac{(m-1)m}{2}.
> $$
>
> So
>
> $$
> nV=(m-1)\left(V-\frac{1}{n}\right)+\frac{n(n+1)}{2}-\frac{(m-1)m}{2}.
> $$
>
> Hence
>
> $$
> (n-m+1)V
> =
> -\frac{m-1}{n}
> +\frac{n(n+1)}{2}
> -\frac{(m-1)m}{2}.
> $$
>
> Therefore
>
> $$
> V
> =
> \frac{
> \frac{n(n+1)}{2}
> -\frac{(m-1)m}{2}
> -\frac{m-1}{n}
> }{
> n-m+1
> }.
> $$
>
> This is exact once the correct $m$ is known.
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Rough asymptotics for large $n$
>
> Because the reroll cost is only $\frac{1}{n}$, the value should be very close to the maximum possible payoff $n$.
>
> Let
>
> $$
> V=n-\delta,
> $$
>
> where $\delta$ is small. Then
>
> $$
> V-\frac{1}{n}=n-\delta-\frac{1}{n}.
> $$
>
> So you only reroll the very top few misses near $n$. Let
>
> $$
> r=n-m+1,
> $$
>
> so that you accept the top $r$ outcomes
>
> $$
> n-r+1,\dots,n.
> $$
>
> Then the average of accepted outcomes is
>
> $$
> \frac{(n-r+1)+n}{2}=n-\frac{r-1}{2}.
> $$
>
> The probability of acceptance on any roll is $\frac{r}{n}$, so the expected number of rerolls before acceptance is about
>
> $$
> \frac{n-r}{r}.
> $$
>
> Since each reroll costs $\frac{1}{n}$, the expected total reroll cost is about
>
> $$
> \frac{n-r}{rn}.
> $$
>
> Thus the value is approximately
>
> $$
> V \approx n-\frac{r-1}{2}-\frac{n-r}{rn}.
> $$
>
> For large $n$, this becomes
>
> $$
> V \approx n-\frac{r}{2}-\frac{1}{r}.
> $$
>
> So we choose $r$ to minimize
>
> $$
> \frac{r}{2}+\frac{1}{r}.
> $$
>
> Differentiate:
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{r}{2}+\frac{1}{r}\right)=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r^2}.
> $$
>
> Set to $0$:
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{r^2}
> \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
> r^2=2
> \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
> r\approx \sqrt{2}.
> $$
>
> Since $r$ must be an integer, the optimum is essentially $r=1$ or $r=2$.
>
> - If $r=1$, accept only $n$.
> - If $r=2$, accept $n-1$ or $n$.
>
> Compute the two rough values:
>
> $$
> r=1:\quad V\approx n-\frac{0}{2}-1=n-1,
> $$
>
> $$
> r=2:\quad V\approx n-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}=n-1.
> $$
>
> So the value is roughly
>
> $$
> V\approx n-1.
> $$
>
> More careful calculation shows the optimal cutoff is indeed to accept only the very top one or two faces, and the value differs from $n-1$ only by a tiny amount of order $\frac{1}{n}$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. Exact comparison of the top two plausible strategies
>
> ### Strategy A: accept only $n$
>
> Then each failed roll costs $\frac{1}{n}$ and you repeat until getting $n$.
>
> The chance of success each roll is $\frac{1}{n}$, so the expected number of failures before success is
>
> $$
> \frac{1-\frac{1}{n}}{\frac{1}{n}}=n-1.
> $$
>
> Expected reroll cost:
>
> $$
> (n-1)\cdot \frac{1}{n}=1-\frac{1}{n}.
> $$
>
> Final payoff is $n$, so
>
> $$
> V_A = n-\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)=n-1+\frac{1}{n}.
> $$
>
> ### Strategy B: accept $n-1$ or $n$
>
> Success probability each roll is $\frac{2}{n}$, so expected failures before success are
>
> $$
> \frac{1-\frac{2}{n}}{\frac{2}{n}}=\frac{n-2}{2}.
> $$
>
> Expected reroll cost:
>
> $$
> \frac{n-2}{2}\cdot \frac{1}{n}=\frac{n-2}{2n}.
> $$
>
> Conditional accepted payoff is the average of $n-1$ and $n$:
>
> $$
> n-\frac{1}{2}.
> $$
>
> Hence
>
> $$
> V_B = \left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)-\frac{n-2}{2n}
> = n-1+\frac{1}{n}.
> $$
>
> So these two strategies give the same value:
>
> $$
> V = n-1+\frac{1}{n}.
> $$
>
> In fact this is the optimal value.
>
> ---
>
> ## 5. Interpretation: buy price and sell price
>
> A risk-neutral fair price for the game is its value:
>
> $$
> \boxed{V=n-1+\frac{1}{n}}.
> $$
>
> So, roughly,
>
> $$
> \boxed{V\approx n-1}.
> $$
>
> Thus:
>
> - **How much would you pay to play?** About $n-1$ dollars.
> - **How much would you sell the game for?** Also about $n-1$ dollars.
>
> If one wants a practical “buy low, sell high” spread, one might say:
>
> - buy around $n-1$ or a bit less,
> - sell around $n-1$ or a bit more,
>
> depending on risk preference and bargaining. But the mathematical fair value is
>
> $$
> \boxed{n-1+\frac{1}{n}}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Final answer
>
> The game is worth
>
> $$
> \boxed{n-1+\frac{1}{n}}
> $$
>
> under optimal play, which is roughly
>
> $$
> \boxed{n-1}.
> $$
>
> An optimal strategy is to keep only the top one or two faces:
>
> - accept only $n$, or
> - accept $n-1$ and $n$,
>
> both of which give the same expected value.
>
> So the rough answer is:
>
> $$
> \boxed{\text{Pay about } n-1 \text{ dollars to play, and sell it for about } n-1.}
> $$
