> [!example] Question 27 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

A coin is flipped 100 times, and you may ask one yes/no question about the sequence of results. You must then guess the entire sequence. You earn one dollar for each correct guess and lose one dollar for each incorrect guess. Find a good strategy and compute its expected return.

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=28)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let the unknown sequence of $100$ coin flips be
> $$
> X=(X_1,\dots,X_{100})\in\{H,T\}^{100},
> $$
> with all $2^{100}$ sequences equally likely.
>
> After asking one yes/no question, you must announce a full sequence
> $$
> G=(G_1,\dots,G_{100})\in\{H,T\}^{100}.
> $$
> If $G_i=X_i$, you gain $1$, and otherwise you lose $1$. Thus the total payoff is
> $$
> \sum_{i=1}^{100} \mathbf{1}_{\{G_i=X_i\}}-\mathbf{1}_{\{G_i\neq X_i\}}
> = (\#\text{correct})-(\#\text{incorrect}).
> $$
> Since there are $100$ flips, this equals
> $$
> 2(\#\text{correct})-100.
> $$
> So maximizing expected payoff is equivalent to maximizing the expected number of correct guesses.
>
> ## 1. A good yes/no question: ask about the majority
>
> Let $N=\#\{i:X_i=H\}$ be the number of heads in the true sequence, so
> $$
> N\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(100,\tfrac12),
> $$
> and the number of tails is $100-N$.
>
> Ask the question:
>
> "Are there at least $50$ heads among the $100$ flips?"
>
> - If the answer is yes ($N\ge 50$), guess that every single flip was heads.
> - If the answer is no ($N<50$), guess that every single flip was tails.
>
> ## 2. Why this works
>
> If you guess "all heads," the number of correct guesses equals exactly the number of positions where the true sequence really is heads, namely $N$. If you guess "all tails," the number of correct guesses equals exactly $100-N$.
>
> Since you always guess the side that the answer tells you is the majority, the number of correct guesses under this strategy is
>
> $$
> \#\text{correct}=\max(N,\,100-N).
> $$
>
> This single yes/no question is powerful precisely because it lets you commit to the correct constant guess at *every* one of the $100$ positions simultaneously, rather than gaining information about only one or two positions.
>
> ## 3. Expected number of correct guesses
>
> We need
> $$
> E\bigl[\max(N,100-N)\bigr] = 50+E\bigl[\,|N-50|\,\bigr].
> $$
>
> For $N\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(100,\tfrac12)$, the mean absolute deviation from the center has the closed form
> $$
> E\bigl[\,|N-50|\,\bigr]=\frac{50\binom{100}{50}}{2^{100}}.
> $$
>
> Numerically,
> $$
> E\bigl[\,|N-50|\,\bigr]\approx 3.98,
> $$
> so
> $$
> E\bigl[\max(N,100-N)\bigr]\approx 50+3.98=53.98.
> $$
>
> ## 4. Expected payoff
>
> Since the payoff is $2(\#\text{correct})-100$,
> $$
> E[\text{payoff}]=2(53.98)-100\approx 7.96.
> $$
>
> Equivalently, in closed form,
> $$
> E[\text{payoff}]=\frac{100\binom{100}{50}}{2^{100}}\approx 7.96.
> $$
>
> ## 5. Why pairing each sequence with its complement does not work
>
> A natural-looking alternative is to pair every sequence $x$ with its bitwise complement $\bar x$ (flip every $H$ to $T$ and vice versa), ask a single yes/no question meant to reveal which member of the pair is the true sequence, and then guess accordingly. This idea does not actually work.
>
> The trouble is that a single yes/no answer carries only one bit of information, while there are $2^{99}$ complementary pairs $\{x,\bar x\}$ to distinguish between. A pre-committed strategy needs to name, in advance, one representative sequence for each of these $2^{99}$ pairs, and the one bit of information you receive can only ever tell you which side of a single, fixed partition the truth lies on — it cannot also tell you which of the $2^{99}$ pairs you are in, since that would require far more than one bit. So the "chosen representative" that you guess after hearing the answer is effectively just one fixed sequence (or its complement), decided before you know anything about $X$. By symmetry, such a guess is correct in exactly $50$ positions in expectation, no better than making a guess without asking any question at all.
>
> The majority question above avoids this trap entirely: it does not try to pin down individual positions, but instead reveals a single aggregate fact (whether heads or tails is more common), and that one fact is enough to determine the best constant guess across all $100$ positions simultaneously.
>
> ## Final answer
>
> A good strategy: ask whether there are at least $50$ heads among the $100$ flips.
>
> - If yes, guess that every flip landed heads.
> - If no, guess that every flip landed tails.
>
> This achieves $\max(N,100-N)$ correct guesses, where $N$ is the true number of heads, so the expected number of correct guesses is
>
> $$
> E[\max(N,100-N)]=50+\frac{50\binom{100}{50}}{2^{100}}\approx 53.98.
> $$
>
> The expected payoff is therefore
>
> $$
> 2(53.98)-100\approx \boxed{7.96\text{ dollars}}.
> $$
