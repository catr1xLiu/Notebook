> [!example] Question 557 · Jane Street
> ## Assistant Trader Interview

Person A has a 30-sided die and person B has a 20-sided die. Both players roll, and the person with the highest roll wins (on a draw, B wins). The loser pays the winner the value shown on the winner's die. 
1. Calculate the expected value of this game for player A.
2. How does this value change if player B can re-roll, and when should B re-roll?
3. Now, how much is it worth for player A to get a re-roll option in this scenario?
4. If player A cannot re-roll, how many re-rolls would player B need in order to be favored in the game?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=558)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## 1. Expected value for player $A$ with no re-rolls
>
> Let $A$ roll a fair $30$-sided die and $B$ roll a fair $20$-sided die.
>
> - If $A>B$, then $A$ wins and receives $30$.
> - If $A\le B$, then $B$ wins and $A$ pays $20$.
>
> Thus the payoff to $A$ is
> $$
> X=
> \begin{cases}
> 30, & A>B,\\ -20, & A\le B.
> \end{cases}
> $$
>
> So we first compute
> $$
> \mathbb{P}(A>B).
> $$
>
> For each $b\in\{1,\dots,20\}$, the number of $a\in\{1,\dots,30\}$ with $a>b$ is $30-b$. Hence
> $$
> \#\{(a,b):a>b\}=\sum_{b=1}^{20}(30-b)=20\cdot 30-\sum_{b=1}^{20}b.
> $$
> Since
> $$
> \sum_{b=1}^{20}b=\frac{20\cdot 21}{2}=210,
> $$
> we get
> $$
> \#\{(a,b):a>b\}=600-210=390.
> $$
> Out of $30\cdot 20=600$ equally likely outcomes,
> $$
> \mathbb{P}(A>B)=\frac{390}{600}=\frac{13}{20}.
> $$
> Therefore
> $$
> \mathbb{P}(A\le B)=1-\frac{13}{20}=\frac{7}{20}.
> $$
>
> Hence
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[X]
> =30\cdot \frac{13}{20}-20\cdot \frac{7}{20}
> =\frac{390-140}{20}
> =\frac{250}{20}
> =\frac{25}{2}.
> $$
>
> Thus the expected value for player $A$ is
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{25}{2}=12.5}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Player $B$ may re-roll once: when should he re-roll, and what is the new value for $A$?
>
> Assume $B$ sees his first roll and may either keep it or discard it and roll once more, with the second result forced.
>
> Let $b$ be $B$'s first roll.
>
> If $B$ keeps $b$, then $A$ wins exactly when $A>b$, so
> $$
> \mathbb{P}(A\text{ wins}\mid B=b)=\frac{30-b}{30}.
> $$
> Therefore $A$'s expected payoff given that $B$ keeps $b$ is
> $$
> 30\cdot \frac{30-b}{30}-20\cdot \frac{b}{30}
> =(30-b)-\frac{2b}{3}
> =30-\frac{5b}{3}.
> $$
>
> If $B$ re-rolls, then his expected action-value is the same as in the original game with no re-roll option used afterward, namely $A$'s expectation becomes
> $$
> \frac{25}{2}.
> $$
>
> So $B$ should re-roll exactly when doing so lowers $A$'s expected payoff, i.e. when
> $$
> \frac{25}{2}<30-\frac{5b}{3}.
> $$
> Solve:
> $$
> \frac{25}{2}<30-\frac{5b}{3}
> \iff \frac{5b}{3}<30-\frac{25}{2}=\frac{35}{2}
> \iff b<\frac{35}{2}\cdot \frac{3}{5}=\frac{21}{2}=10.5.
> $$
> Thus $B$ should re-roll for
> $$
> b\le 10,
> $$
> and keep for
> $$
> b\ge 11.
> $$
>
> So the optimal strategy is:
>
> $$
> \boxed{\text{$B$ re-rolls on }1,2,\dots,10\text{ and keeps }11,\dots,20.}
> $$
>
> Now compute $A$'s expected value under this strategy.
>
> ### Case 1: $B$ first rolls $1,\dots,10$
> This occurs with probability $\frac{10}{20}=\frac12$. Then $B$ re-rolls, and the expected value to $A$ is the original
> $$
> \frac{25}{2}.
> $$
>
> ### Case 2: $B$ first rolls $11,\dots,20$
> Each such value occurs with probability $\frac1{20}$ and is kept. For fixed $b$,
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[X\mid B=b]=30-\frac{5b}{3}.
> $$
> Therefore
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[X\mid B\in\{11,\dots,20\},\text{ kept part}]
> =\frac1{20}\sum_{b=11}^{20}\left(30-\frac{5b}{3}\right).
> $$
> Compute:
> $$
> \sum_{b=11}^{20}30=10\cdot 30=300,
> $$
> and
> $$
> \sum_{b=11}^{20} b=\frac{(11+20)\cdot 10}{2}=155.
> $$
> Hence
> $$
> \frac1{20}\left(300-\frac{5}{3}\cdot 155\right)
> =\frac1{20}\left(300-\frac{775}{3}\right)
> =\frac1{20}\cdot \frac{125}{3}
> =\frac{25}{12}.
> $$
>
> Adding the two parts:
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[X]
> =\frac12\cdot \frac{25}{2}+\frac{25}{12}
> =\frac{25}{4}+\frac{25}{12}
> =\frac{75+25}{12}
> =\frac{100}{12}
> =\frac{25}{3}.
> $$
>
> Therefore the new expected value for $A$ is
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{25}{3}\approx 8.333}.
> $$
>
> So compared with part $1$, $A$ loses
> $$
> \frac{25}{2}-\frac{25}{3}=\frac{25}{6}.
> $$
>

> ## 3. Now allow player $A$ also one re-roll option. How much is this worth to $A$?
>
> Now both players may use one re-roll, and, exactly as in part $2$, each player must decide whether to re-roll based only on his own first roll, without seeing the other's result. This calls for a pair of threshold strategies that are mutual best responses: $A$ re-rolls when $a\le t_A$ and keeps when $a>t_A$; $B$ re-rolls when $b\le t_B$ and keeps when $b>t_B$.
>
> ### Final distributions under threshold strategies
>
> Under a threshold $t_A$ for $A$, a first roll $a\le t_A$ is replaced by a fresh uniform roll on $\{1,\dots,30\}$, while $a>t_A$ is kept. So $A$'s final value carries an extra weight of $\frac{t_A}{30}\cdot\frac1{30}$ spread over every outcome, on top of $\frac1{30}$ for the kept outcomes. The analogous statement holds for $B$ with threshold $t_B$ on $\{1,\dots,20\}$.
>
> Testing $t_A=15$ and $t_B=11$: $A$'s final value carries weight $\frac1{60}$ on each of $1,\dots,15$ and weight $\frac1{20}$ on each of $16,\dots,30$; $B$'s final value carries weight $\frac{11}{400}$ on each of $1,\dots,11$ and weight $\frac{31}{400}$ on each of $12,\dots,20$.
>
> ### Checking that these thresholds are mutual best responses
>
> If $A$ keeps a roll of $a$, his payoff is $G(a)=30\,\mathbb{P}(B<a)-20\,\mathbb{P}(B\ge a)$, using $B$'s distribution above. If $A$ re-rolls instead, he faces a fresh roll against that same distribution; call this continuation value $R_A$.
>
> Since $B$'s mean is
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[B]=\frac{11\cdot 66+31\cdot 144}{400}=\frac{519}{40}=12.975,
> $$
> and a fresh roll $A'$ uniform on $\{1,\dots,30\}$ satisfies $\mathbb{P}(A'>B)=1-\frac{\mathbb{E}[B]}{30}$,
> $$
> R_A=50\left(1-\frac{12.975}{30}\right)-20=50(0.5675)-20=8.375.
> $$
>
> Direct computation gives $G(15)=6.75$ and $G(16)=10.625$. Since $G(15)<R_A<G(16)$, it is indeed optimal for $A$ to re-roll on $15$ and keep on $16$, confirming $t_A=15$.
>
> Symmetrically, if $B$ keeps $b$, his effect on $A$'s payoff is $H(b)=30\,\mathbb{P}(A>b)-20\,\mathbb{P}(A\le b)$, using $A$'s distribution above; if $B$ re-rolls, the continuation value is $R_B$. Summing $\mathbb{P}(A>u)$ against $A$'s distribution for $u=1,\dots,20$ gives a total of $16$, so a fresh roll $B'$ satisfies $\mathbb{P}(A>B')=\frac{16}{20}=\frac45$, and
> $$
> R_B=50\left(\frac45\right)-20=20.
> $$
>
> Direct computation gives $H(11)=\frac{125}{6}\approx 20.83$ and $H(12)=20$. Since $H(11)>R_B$, $B$ prefers to re-roll at $11$; since $H(12)=R_B$ exactly, $b=12$ is the indifference point, and keeping is (weakly) optimal there. This confirms $t_B=11$.
>
> ### Computing the value of the game
>
> Split each player's range into a low and a high block: for $A$, low $=\{1,\dots,15\}$ (weight $\frac1{60}$ each) and high $=\{16,\dots,30\}$ (weight $\frac1{20}$ each); for $B$, low $=\{1,\dots,11\}$ (weight $\frac{11}{400}$ each) and high $=\{12,\dots,20\}$ (weight $\frac{31}{400}$ each).
>
> - $A$-high always beats $B$-low, since every value in the first block exceeds every value in the second.
> - Among $A$-low versus $B$-high, only $6$ of the $15\times 9$ pairs satisfy $a>b$.
> - Among $A$-low versus $B$-low, $99$ of the $15\times 11$ pairs satisfy $a>b$.
> - Among $A$-high versus $B$-high, $120$ of the $15\times 9$ pairs satisfy $a>b$.
>
> Adding the weighted contributions of all four blocks,
> $$
> \mathbb{P}(A>B)=\frac34\cdot\frac{121}{400}+6\cdot\frac1{60}\cdot\frac{31}{400}+99\cdot\frac1{60}\cdot\frac{11}{400}+120\cdot\frac1{20}\cdot\frac{31}{400}=\frac{149}{200}.
> $$
>
> So
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[X]=50\cdot\frac{149}{200}-20=\frac{149}{4}-20=\frac{69}{4}=17.25.
> $$
>
> ### Worth of the re-roll option to $A$
>
> From part $2$, when only $B$ had a re-roll, $A$'s value was $\frac{25}{3}$. With $A$ also getting one re-roll, the value becomes $17.25$. So the extra value to $A$ is
> $$
> \frac{69}{4}-\frac{25}{3}=\frac{207-100}{12}=\frac{107}{12}\approx 8.92.
> $$
>
> ### Summary for part 3
>
> $$
> \boxed{\text{$A$ re-rolls on }1,\dots,15\text{ and keeps }16,\dots,30;\quad \text{$B$ re-rolls on }1,\dots,11\text{ and keeps }12,\dots,20.}
> $$
>
> The value of the game to $A$ becomes
>
> $$
> \boxed{17.25}
> $$
>
> so the re-roll option is worth approximately
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{107}{12}\approx 8.92}
> $$
>
> to player $A$ in this scenario.
>

> ## 4. Remove $A$'s re-roll. How many re-rolls does $B$ need to be a favorite?
>
> Now only $B$ has re-rolls, up to $n$ of them, and $B$ still cannot observe $A$'s value. We seek the least $n$ such that $A$'s expected value becomes negative.
>
> Let $V_n$ denote $A$'s expected payoff when $B$ has $n$ re-rolls remaining and plays optimally. As in part 2, if $B$ keeps a roll $b$, then $A$'s conditional expectation is
>
> $$
> 30-\frac{5b}{3},
> $$
>
> while if $B$ re-rolls, $A$'s expectation becomes $V_{n-1}$. Since $B$ wants $A$'s expectation as small as possible, $B$ keeps $b$ exactly when
>
> $$
> 30-\frac{5b}{3}\le V_{n-1}
> \iff
> b\ge 18-\frac{3}{5}V_{n-1}.
> $$
>
> Writing $k_n$ for the number of faces $B$ re-rolls, the recursion is
>
> $$
> V_n=\frac{k_n}{20}V_{n-1}+\frac{1}{20}\sum_{b\,\text{kept}}\left(30-\frac{5b}{3}\right),
> \qquad V_0=\frac{25}{2}.
> $$
>
> Evaluating successively (each threshold recomputed from the previous value):
>
> - $V_1=\frac{25}{3}\approx 8.333$, with $B$ keeping $11$ through $20$;
> - $V_2=6$, keeping $13$ through $20$;
> - $V_3=4.45$, keeping $15$ through $20$;
> - $V_4\approx 3.338$, keeping $16$ through $20$;
> - $V_5\approx 2.503$, and $V_6\approx 1.836$, then keeping $17$ through $20$;
> - $V_7\approx 1.302$, $V_8\approx 0.857$, then keeping $18$ through $20$;
> - $V_9\approx 0.478$, $V_{10}\approx 0.157$;
> - $V_{11}\approx -0.117$.
>
> The value first becomes negative at $n=11$. Note that a shortcut which lets $B$ keep re-rolling "until reaching at least $a$" for each fixed value $a$ of $A$ would overstate $B$'s power: it implicitly lets $B$ react to a roll he never observes. The recursion above uses only what $B$ actually knows, and it reproduces the verified answers of parts 1 and 2 as its first two steps.
>
> Thus the smallest $n$ for which $B$ is a favorite is
>
> $$
> \boxed{11}.
> $$
>
> So $B$ needs
>
> $$
> \boxed{11\text{ re-rolls}}
> $$
>
> to make the expected value negative for $A$.
>
> ---
>
> ## Final answers
>
> With no re-rolls, the expected value for $A$ is $\frac{25}{2}=12.5$.
>
> If $B$ gets one re-roll, the optimal strategy is to re-roll on $1,\dots,10$ and keep on $11,\dots,20$, giving $A$ a new expected value of $\frac{25}{3}\approx 8.333$.
>
> If $B$ has that re-roll and $A$ also gets one re-roll, $A$ re-rolls on $1,\dots,15$ and keeps $16,\dots,30$, while $B$ re-rolls on $1,\dots,11$ and keeps $12,\dots,20$. The value to $A$ becomes $17.25$, so the re-roll option is worth $17.25-\frac{25}{3}=\frac{107}{12}\approx 8.92$ to $A$.
>
> With only $B$ allowed re-rolls, the minimum number of re-rolls needed for $B$ to become the favorite is $11$.
