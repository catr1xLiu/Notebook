> [!example] Question 451 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Trader Interview

There are 52 cards: 26 red and 26 black. You draw cards one at a time, indefinitely, with replacement. For each red card drawn, you lose 1 point; for each black card, you gain 1 point. What strategy maximizes your total points?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=452)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let $S_n$ denote the cumulative score after $n$ cards have been drawn, with replacement, where each black card drawn contributes $+1$ and each red card contributes $-1$. Because the draws are with replacement, every draw independently shows black or red with probability $\frac12$ each — there are always $26$ of each color among the $52$ cards, regardless of what has already been drawn. Thus $(S_n)_{n\ge0}$ is a simple symmetric random walk on the integers, and you may keep drawing indefinitely, choosing when (if ever) to stop.
>
> ## Why this differs from the finite without-replacement version
>
> If the cards were drawn without replacement, the process would necessarily end after at most $52$ draws, since the deck would be exhausted with $S_{52}=0$ deterministically, and the best strategy would simply be to stop at the running maximum of $S_n$ over that fixed, finite horizon. Here, however, the draws are with replacement, so there is no finite horizon: the walk can continue forever, and $S_n$ is a genuine unbounded random walk with no terminal constraint.
>
> ## 1. Any strategy with bounded expected duration has expected payoff 0
>
> Since each draw contributes $+1$ or $-1$ with equal probability, $(S_n)$ is a martingale:
> $$
> E[S_{n+1}\mid S_0,\dots,S_n]=S_n.
> $$
> By the optional stopping theorem, for any stopping rule $\tau$ with bounded expected duration (for instance $E[\tau]<\infty$ together with bounded increments),
> $$
> E[S_\tau]=E[S_0]=0.
> $$
> So any strategy guaranteed to stop reasonably quickly in expectation — including a rule based on a capped number of draws — has expected payoff exactly $0$. The "stop at the running maximum" idea that works for the finite without-replacement version does not carry over here, since there is no final draw at which to look back and no upper bound on how long play can continue.
>
> ## 2. Recurrence allows guaranteed, but slow, positive scores
>
> A simple symmetric random walk on the integers is recurrent: starting from $0$, it visits every integer level with probability $1$ if given unlimited time. In particular, fix any target $k>0$ and consider the strategy of drawing until $S_n=k$ for the first time, then stopping. This strategy succeeds with probability $1$ — the running score is guaranteed, eventually, to hit exactly $k$ — so its payoff is $k$ with probability $1$, giving
> $$
> E[\text{payoff}]=k.
> $$
>
> This does not contradict Step 1: although this strategy stops in finite time almost surely, its expected number of draws is infinite. This is a classical fact about simple random walks — they are recurrent, but only *null* recurrent, so hitting times to a fixed level have infinite expectation. The optional stopping theorem's hypotheses therefore fail for this strategy, and $E[S_\tau]=k\neq0$ is possible.
>
> ## 3. No optimal strategy exists
>
> Because $k$ can be chosen to be any positive integer, the expected payoff achievable this way is unbounded: targeting $k=1{,}000{,}000$ succeeds with probability $1$ and has expected payoff $1{,}000{,}000$, just as targeting any larger number does, at the cost of a still almost-surely-finite but ever-longer wait. There is therefore no strategy that maximizes expected total points — for any candidate strategy, a more patient player can always do better simply by aiming for a larger target.
>
> ## Final answer
>
> Because the cards are drawn with replacement, the running score is an unbounded, recurrent, fair random walk, not the finite $52$-draw process of the classical without-replacement puzzle. Any strategy with a bounded (in expectation) number of draws has expected payoff exactly $0$, by the optional stopping theorem. But recurrence lets a patient player guarantee, with probability $1$ given unlimited time, reaching any fixed target score $k$, so expected payoffs of $k$ for arbitrarily large $k$ are all achievable.
>
> Consequently, **no strategy maximizes the expected total points**: the achievable expected payoff is unbounded above, and every finite target is beaten by aiming for a larger one. Bounded-time strategies break even at an expected payoff of $0$; unboundedly patient strategies can achieve any expected payoff $k$, but none of them is optimal.
