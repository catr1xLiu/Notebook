> ## Math

> ### 1. Fair coin flipped $8$ times: probability the number of heads is a multiple of $3$
>
> Let $X \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(8, \tfrac12)$. We want
> $$
> \mathbb{P}(X \in \{0,3,6\}),
> $$
> since the possible multiples of $3$ between $0$ and $8$ are $0,3,6$.
>
> Thus
> $$
> \mathbb{P}(X \in \{0,3,6\})
> = \frac{\binom{8}{0}+\binom{8}{3}+\binom{8}{6}}{2^8}.
> $$
>
> Compute:
> $$
> \binom{8}{0}=1,\qquad \binom{8}{3}=56,\qquad \binom{8}{6}=\binom{8}{2}=28.
> $$
>
> So
> $$
> \mathbb{P}(X \in \{0,3,6\})
> = \frac{1+56+28}{256}
> = \frac{85}{256}.
> $$
>
> Therefore, the probability is
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{85}{256}}.
> $$

> ### 2. Estimate the number of copies of *Infinite Jest* that have been read cover to cover
>
> This is a Fermi estimate, so the goal is a reasonable order-of-magnitude answer.
>
> #### Step 1: Estimate total copies sold
>
> A famous literary novel by a highly regarded author, continuously discussed for decades, likely has cumulative worldwide sales on the order of a few million copies. A plausible estimate is
> $$
> 2 \text{ million to } 4 \text{ million copies sold}.
> $$
>
> Take the midpoint:
> $$
> 3 \times 10^6 \text{ copies sold}.
> $$
>
> #### Step 2: Estimate fraction actually read cover to cover
>
> This book is unusually long and difficult. Many buyers are aspirational purchasers; some start but do not finish. For a normal popular novel, completion might be high, but for *Infinite Jest* it is likely much lower. A plausible completion fraction is perhaps
> $$
> 10\% \text{ to } 30\%.
> $$
>
> Take
> $$
> 20\%.
> $$
>
> #### Step 3: Multiply
>
> Then the number read cover to cover is approximately
> $$
> (3 \times 10^6)(0.2)=6 \times 10^5.
> $$
>
> So a reasonable estimate is
> $$
> \boxed{\text{about } 600{,}000}.
> $$
>
> Given uncertainty, any answer in the rough range
> $$
> \boxed{300{,}000 \text{ to } 1{,}000{,}000}
> $$
> would be defensible if supported clearly.

> ### 3. Maximum surface distance on a $2 \times 1 \times 1$ brick
>
> A shortest path constrained to the surface of a rectangular box can be found by unfolding faces into a plane. The maximum distance between two surface points will occur between two vertices, and specifically between opposite vertices.
>
> Take opposite corners of the $2 \times 1 \times 1$ box. To find the shortest surface path between them, unfold adjacent faces and compute straight-line distances in the net.
>
> There are three relevant unfoldings:
>
> #### Unfolding $1$
> Combine dimensions as $(2+1)$ by $1$:
> $$
> d_1=\sqrt{(2+1)^2+1^2}=\sqrt{10}.
> $$
>
> #### Unfolding $2$
> Combine dimensions as $(2+1)$ by $1$ again (symmetry):
> $$
> d_2=\sqrt{(2+1)^2+1^2}=\sqrt{10}.
> $$
>
> #### Unfolding $3$
> Combine dimensions as $(1+1)$ by $2$:
> $$
> d_3=\sqrt{(1+1)^2+2^2}=\sqrt{8}=2\sqrt{2}.
> $$
>
> The shortest surface path between opposite vertices is therefore
> $$
> \min\{\sqrt{10},\sqrt{10},2\sqrt{2}\}=2\sqrt{2}.
> $$
>
> It remains to justify that this is the maximum over all pairs of surface points. Any pair of points lies on the boundary, and when searching for the largest possible geodesic distance on a convex polyhedron of this type, the extremal pair occurs at opposite vertices; moving either point away from a vertex can only decrease the maximal attainable unfolded straight-line separation. Hence the maximum surface distance is attained by opposite corners.
>
> Therefore, the maximum possible distance is
> $$
> \boxed{2\sqrt{2}}.
> $$
