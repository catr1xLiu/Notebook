> [!example] Question 663 · Jane Street
> ## Quant Trader Intern Interview

Suppose you are playing a dice game with a six-sided die. You may roll the die as many times as you like, and after each roll, you may choose to stop and take the sum of the numbers rolled as your score. However, if you roll a 6 at any time, you must stop and your total score becomes 0. What is the optimal strategy to maximize your expected score?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=664)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> Let $V(s)$ denote the maximum expected score attainable once your current accumulated total is $s$, under optimal play. At each point you may either **stop** and keep $s$, or **roll once more**: with probability $\frac16$ you roll a $6$ and immediately bust, ending the game with a score of $0$; with probability $\frac16$ each, you roll a $1,2,3,4,$ or $5$, adding that amount to your total and continuing.
>
> So
> $$
> V(s)=\max\left\{\,s,\ \frac16\sum_{k=1}^{5}V(s+k)\,\right\},
> $$
> since rolling a $6$ contributes $0$ to the roll-value and the other five outcomes contribute $\tfrac16V(s+k)$ each.
>
> ## 1. Guessing a threshold, and checking it
>
> Since busting wipes out your entire score, the risk of continuing becomes relatively less attractive as your accumulated total $s$ grows, while the potential gain from one more roll is capped at $+5$. This suggests the optimal policy is a single threshold: roll below some cutoff, stop at or above it.
>
> Suppose, for $s$ at or above some threshold $s^\ast$, stopping is optimal, so $V(t)=t$ for all $t\ge s^\ast$. Then for any $s\ge s^\ast$, using $V(s+k)=s+k$ for $k=1,\dots,5$,
> $$
> \frac16\sum_{k=1}^5 V(s+k)=\frac16\bigl((s+1)+(s+2)+(s+3)+(s+4)+(s+5)\bigr)=\frac{5s+15}{6}.
> $$
> Stopping remains at least as good as rolling exactly when $s\ge\frac{5s+15}6$, that is, when
> $$
> 6s\ge5s+15\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad s\ge15.
> $$
>
> So the threshold is self-consistent at exactly $s^\ast=15$: for every $s\ge15$, stopping gives $V(s)=s$, matching what was assumed. At $s=15$ the two options are exactly tied, since $\tfrac{5(15)+15}{6}=15$, and for $s>15$ stopping is strictly better.
>
> ## 2. Working backward below the threshold
>
> For $s\le14$, we use $V(s)=\dfrac{V(s+1)+V(s+2)+V(s+3)+V(s+4)+V(s+5)}{6}$, with $V(t)=t$ known for $t\ge15$.
>
> $$
> V(14)=\frac{15+16+17+18+19}{6}=\frac{85}{6}\approx14.1667.
> $$
> Since this exceeds $14$, rolling is optimal at $14$, exactly as the threshold at $15$ requires.
>
> Continuing backward, folding in the values just computed:
> $$
> V(13)=\frac{V(14)+15+16+17+18}{6}=\frac{481}{36}\approx13.3611,
> $$
> $$
> V(12)=\frac{V(13)+V(14)+15+16+17}{6}=\frac{2719}{216}\approx12.5880,
> $$
> $$
> V(11)=\frac{V(12)+V(13)+V(14)+15+16}{6}=\frac{15361}{1296}\approx11.8526,
> $$
> $$
> V(10)=\frac{V(11)+V(12)+V(13)+V(14)+15}{6}=\frac{86791}{7776}\approx11.1614.
> $$
>
> From $s=9$ downward, all five successors $s+1,\dots,s+5$ are themselves below the threshold, so every remaining step uses only previously computed values:
> $$
> V(9)=\frac{V(10)+V(11)+V(12)+V(13)+V(14)}{6}=\frac{490897}{46656}\approx10.5216,
> $$
> $$
> V(8)\approx9.9141,\quad V(7)\approx9.3396,\quad V(6)\approx8.7982,\quad V(5)\approx8.2892,
> $$
> $$
> V(4)\approx7.8105,\quad V(3)\approx7.3586,\quad V(2)\approx6.9327,\quad V(1)\approx6.5315,\quad V(0)\approx6.1537.
> $$
>
> At every step from $14$ down to $0$, the roll value exceeds the current total, confirming that rolling is strictly optimal throughout $s=0,\dots,14$ — exactly as the threshold at $15$ requires.
>
> ## 3. Final answer
>
> The optimal strategy is a clean threshold rule:
> $$
> \boxed{\text{roll whenever } s\le14,\ \text{ stop whenever } s\ge15.}
> $$
>
> Starting from $s=0$, the expected score under optimal play is
> $$
> V(0)=\frac{2893395172951}{470184984576}\approx6.154.
> $$
>
> $$
> \boxed{V(0)\approx6.154}
> $$
