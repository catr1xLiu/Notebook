> [!example] Question 187 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Trading Intern Interview

In a game, you have a standard six-faced die, starting with the \$1 face up. Each turn, you may either roll the die (randomly changing the upface) or 'take' by cashing out the current upface for its dollar amount. The game does not end when you take, and you may take as many times as you want, including multiple times in a row. For example, you could take 100 times on the initial \$1 upface for \$100 total. Your strategy is to roll until you see a face of at least n for the first time, then 'take' on that face and continue taking as much as you like. Assuming you choose n optimally, what is your expected payout in this game?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=188)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let the threshold strategy be: choose an integer $n \in \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$ at the start, and then repeatedly roll until the first time the die shows a value at least $n$. Once that happens, stop rolling and repeatedly take forever.
>
> ## 1. Why taking forever matters
>
> If at any point the current upface is $k$, then each take pays $k$ and does not change the die. Hence after reaching an upface $k$, one may perform arbitrarily many takes and obtain arbitrarily large total payout.
>
> In particular, under the threshold strategy:
>
> - if $n=1$, the initial upface is already at least $1$, so one can immediately take forever and obtain unbounded payout;
> - if $n\ge 2$, one keeps rolling until a face in $\{n,n+1,\dots,6\}$ appears, and then takes forever at that face value.
>
> Since for any $n\le 6$, the probability of eventually rolling a value at least $n$ is $1$, the strategy reaches a cash-out face almost surely.
>
> ## 2. Expected payout for any threshold
>
> Fix any threshold $n \in \{1,\dots,6\}$.
>
> Let $T$ be the first time the die shows a value at least $n$, and let $X_T$ be that upface. Then $X_T \ge n$ almost surely. After time $T$, if one performs $m$ takes, the total payout from those takes is at least
>
> $$
> m n.
> $$
>
> Since this holds for every positive integer $m$, the total achievable payout is unbounded. Therefore the expected payout is not finite; it diverges to infinity.
>
> Formally, if $P_m$ denotes the payout obtained by taking exactly $m$ times after the threshold is reached, then
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[P_m] \ge m n.
> $$
>
> Letting $m \to \infty$ gives
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[\text{payout}] = \infty.
> $$
>
> ## 3. Rational choice of threshold
>
> Because every threshold $n \in \{1,\dots,6\}$ yields infinite expected payout, there is no unique optimizing threshold in the usual finite sense. Any threshold is optimal, and the value of the game is
>
> $$
> \boxed{\infty}.
> $$
>
> Thus the expected payout under rational play is **infinite**.
