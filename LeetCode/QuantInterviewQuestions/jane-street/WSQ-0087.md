> [!example] Question 87 · Jane Street
> ## Trading Interview

Game 1: You roll a 100-faced die labeled from 1 to 100. 1. You roll once and receive the amount in dollars that appears. How much would you pay for this roll? 2. How much would you pay if you can roll twice and keep the higher result? 3. If you can roll the die an infinite number of times, but each additional roll after the first costs \$1, what is your optimal strategy?

Game 2: You are competing with two others and a 21-faced die labeled 1 to 21. Each player picks a number, then the die is rolled. The player whose number is closest to the result wins. What is your optimal strategy? How does your strategy change if all players can communicate?

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Game 1
>
> A fair $100$-sided die has outcomes $1,2,\dots,100$, each with probability $\frac{1}{100}$.
>
> ### 1. One roll
>
> If you roll once and receive the number shown in dollars, the value of the game is its expected payoff:
>
> $$
> E[X] = \frac{1}{100}\sum_{k=1}^{100} k
> = \frac{1}{100}\cdot \frac{100\cdot 101}{2}
> = 50.5.
> $$
>
> So the fair price is
>
> $$
> \boxed{\text{\textdollar}50.50}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### 2. Two rolls, with the option to reroll once
>
> You roll once, observe the result, and may either keep it or discard it and take one final second roll.
>
> Let the first roll be $x$. If you keep it, your payoff is $x$. If you reroll, the expected payoff from the second roll is
>
> $$
> E[X] = 50.5.
> $$
>
> Therefore the optimal rule is:
>
> - keep the first roll if $x \ge 50.5$,
> - reroll if $x < 50.5$.
>
> Since $x$ is an integer, this means:
>
> - keep if $x \ge 51$,
> - reroll if $x \le 50$.
>
> Now compute the expected value:
>
> $$
> V = \frac{1}{100}\left(\sum_{x=51}^{100} x + \sum_{x=1}^{50} 50.5\right).
> $$
>
> First,
>
> $$
> \sum_{x=51}^{100} x
> = \sum_{x=1}^{100} x - \sum_{x=1}^{50} x
> = 5050 - 1275
> = 3775.
> $$
>
> Also,
>
> $$
> \sum_{x=1}^{50} 50.5 = 50 \cdot 50.5 = 2525.
> $$
>
> Hence
>
> $$
> V = \frac{3775 + 2525}{100} = \frac{6300}{100} = 63.
> $$
>
> So the fair price is
>
> $$
> \boxed{\text{\textdollar}63}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### 3. Infinitely many rolls, each additional roll costs one dollar
>
> You may roll as many times as you like. The first roll is free; each later roll costs $\text{\textdollar}1$. After any roll, you may stop and take the current face value, or pay $\text{\textdollar}1$ to roll again.
>
> This is an optimal stopping problem.
>
> Let $V$ be the expected net value of the game from the start of any decision point just after observing a roll. Suppose the current observed value is $x$.
>
> - If you stop, your payoff is $x$.
> - If you continue, you pay $\text{\textdollar}1$ and then face the same situation again, so the expected value is $V-1$.
>
> Thus the optimal strategy is:
>
> - stop if $x \ge V-1$,
> - continue if $x < V-1$.
>
> Since $x$ is integer-valued, there is some cutoff $c$ such that you stop for $x \ge c$ and reroll for $x < c$.
>
> Let $W$ denote the value of the game immediately after a roll is seen. Then
>
> $$
> W = \frac{1}{100}\left(\sum_{x=c}^{100} x + \sum_{x=1}^{c-1} (W-1)\right).
> $$
>
> Equivalently,
>
> $$
> 100W = \sum_{x=c}^{100} x + (c-1)(W-1).
> $$
>
> Rearrange:
>
> $$
> (101-c)W = \sum_{x=c}^{100} x - (c-1).
> $$
>
> Now determine the correct cutoff. If the continuation value is $W-1$, then stopping is optimal when
>
> $$
> x \ge W-1.
> $$
>
> We test a natural candidate. Suppose the cutoff is $c=91$, meaning:
>
> - reroll on $1,\dots,90$,
> - stop on $91,\dots,100$.
>
> Then
>
> $$
> \sum_{x=91}^{100} x = \frac{(91+100)\cdot 10}{2} = 955.
> $$
>
> So
>
> $$
> 100W = 955 + 90(W-1),
> $$
>
> hence
>
> $$
> 100W = 955 + 90W - 90,
> $$
>
> $$
> 10W = 865,
> $$
>
> $$
> W = 86.5.
> $$
>
> Then the continuation value is
>
> $$
> W-1 = 85.5.
> $$
>
> So you should stop when $x \ge 86$, not $91$; thus $c=91$ is not correct.
>
> Now suppose the cutoff is $c=86$, meaning:
>
> - reroll on $1,\dots,85$,
> - stop on $86,\dots,100$.
>
> Then
>
> $$
> \sum_{x=86}^{100} x = \frac{(86+100)\cdot 15}{2} = 1395.
> $$
>
> So
>
> $$
> 100W = 1395 + 85(W-1),
> $$
>
> $$
> 100W = 1395 + 85W - 85,
> $$
>
> $$
> 15W = 1310,
> $$
>
> $$
> W = \frac{1310}{15} = 87.\overline{3}.
> $$
>
> Then
>
> $$
> W-1 = 86.\overline{3}.
> $$
>
> Thus stopping is optimal for $x \ge 87$ and rerolling for $x \le 86$.
>
> So the correct cutoff is $87$.
>
> Now compute the corresponding value directly:
>
> $$
> \sum_{x=87}^{100} x = \frac{(87+100)\cdot 14}{2} = 1309.
> $$
>
> Then
>
> $$
> 100W = 1309 + 86(W-1),
> $$
>
> $$
> 100W = 1309 + 86W - 86,
> $$
>
> $$
> 14W = 1223,
> $$
>
> $$
> W = \frac{1223}{14} \approx 87.3571.
> $$
>
> Check consistency:
>
> $$
> W-1 = \frac{1209}{14} \approx 86.3571,
> $$
>
> so stopping for $x \ge 87$ is indeed optimal.
>
> Therefore the optimal strategy is:
>
> - **keep any roll of $87$ or more**,
> - **reroll any roll of $86$ or less**.
>
> The value of the game is
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{1223}{14} \approx \text{\textdollar}87.36}.
> $$
>

> ## Game 2
>
> A fair $21$-sided die has outcomes $1,2,\dots,21$. Each of three players chooses a number, then the die is rolled. The player whose chosen number is closest to the outcome wins.
>
> Assume ties are split equally among tied players.
>
> ### Best strategy when others are choosing independently
>
> Suppose the three chosen numbers, in increasing order, are
>
> $$
> a < b < c.
> $$
>
> Then $a$ wins the outcomes closest to it, $c$ wins the outcomes closest to it, and $b$ wins whatever lies in between — each player's winning region is the Voronoi cell generated by the three chosen points on $\{1,\dots,21\}$.
>
> Unlike the two-player version of this game, the three-player simultaneous game does not admit a single number that is provably optimal against arbitrary opponents: whatever triple is on the table, a player can typically improve their own region either by nudging toward a neighbor (crowding out part of that neighbor's territory) or by jumping to the far side of the whole group to claim an entire flank, so no clean pure-strategy equilibrium describes "the" answer here.
>
> What is true without any assumptions on the opponents is that extreme numbers are weak, since a pick near either end of $\{1,\dots,21\}$ can only ever win outcomes on one side, whereas a central pick can win on both sides. This makes the central numbers
>
> $$
> 10,\ 11,\ 12
> $$
>
> the most robust choices when nothing is known about how the other two players will choose, and among these the true center,
>
> $$
> \boxed{11},
> $$
>
> is the natural single best guess: it minimizes the worst-case distance to any outcome and is the least exploitable choice available without coordination.
>
> ---
>
> ### If all three players can communicate
>
> If the three players can coordinate and are trying to maximize their combined chances (equivalently, to split the win probability as evenly as possible among themselves), they should partition the $21$ outcomes as evenly as they can — into three groups of $7$.
>
> To achieve this, choose numbers spaced evenly across the line:
>
> $$
> \boxed{4,\ 11,\ 18}.
> $$
>
> Check the regions:
>
> - $4$ covers outcomes $1$ through $7$,
> - $11$ covers outcomes $8$ through $14$,
> - $18$ covers outcomes $15$ through $21$.
>
> Each player wins on exactly $7$ outcomes, so each has probability
>
> $$
> \frac{7}{21} = \frac{1}{3}.
> $$
>
> Thus with communication and cooperation, the optimal coordinated strategy is
>
> $$
> \boxed{4,\ 11,\ 18}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Final Answers
>
> ### Game 1
> 1. One roll: value $\boxed{\text{\textdollar}50.50}$.
> 2. Two rolls with an optional reroll: reroll if the first roll is at most $50$, otherwise keep it; value $\boxed{\text{\textdollar}63}$.
> 3. Infinite rerolls at $\text{\textdollar}1$ each after the first: keep any roll of $87$ or more, otherwise reroll; value $\boxed{\frac{1223}{14} \approx \text{\textdollar}87.36}$.
>
> ### Game 2
> - Without coordination: the most robust single choice, absent any clean equilibrium, is the center, $\boxed{11}$.
> - With full coordination among all three players: choose $\boxed{4,\ 11,\ 18}$, so that each player covers exactly one-third of the outcomes.
