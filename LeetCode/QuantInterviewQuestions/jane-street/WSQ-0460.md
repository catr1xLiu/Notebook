> [!example] Question 460 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Trader Interview

Suppose in a market making context, you are asked to make a market (set buy and sell prices, as well as quantities) on the sum or the product of two rolls of fair 6-sided dice. How would you determine appropriate prices and quantities in this scenario?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=461)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Market making on the sum or product of two fair six-sided dice
>
> A standard first-round market-making question is to quote a two-sided market on a random variable defined from rolling two fair six-sided dice. The interviewer may ask for:
>
> - a **bid** and an **ask** price,
> - and the **size** at which you are willing to buy or sell,
>
> for either the **sum** or the **product** of the two dice.
>
> The objective is not only to know the expected value, but to make a sensible market that reflects uncertainty, inventory risk, and the possibility that the counterparty may trade only when your quote is favorable to them.
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. General market-making framework
>
> Let $X$ be the payoff of the game.
>
> A natural starting point is the **fair value**
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[X].
> $$
>
> If there were no risk and no adverse selection, one might quote exactly this value. In practice, a market maker quotes a spread around fair value, for example
> $$
> \text{bid} < \mathbb{E}[X] < \text{ask},
> $$
> so that buying at the bid and selling at the ask earns edge on average.
>
> A reasonable interview answer should include:
>
> 1. the fair value,
> 2. some comment on dispersion or tail risk,
> 3. a bid-ask spread,
> 4. a size, typically smaller if the payoff distribution is more volatile.
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Sum of two dice
>
> Let $D_1, D_2$ be independent and uniformly distributed on $\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$, and define
> $$
> S = D_1 + D_2.
> $$
>
> ### Fair value
>
> Since
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[D_1] = \mathbb{E}[D_2] = \frac{1+2+3+4+5+6}{6} = 3.5,
> $$
> we have
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[S] = \mathbb{E}[D_1] + \mathbb{E}[D_2] = 7.
> $$
>
> Thus the fair value of the sum is
> $$
> 7.
> $$
>
> ### Distribution
>
> The possible sums are $2,3,\dots,12$, with probabilities:
>
> - **Sum $2$** — Ways: $1$; Probability: $\frac{1}{36}$
> - **Sum $3$** — Ways: $2$; Probability: $\frac{2}{36}$
> - **Sum $4$** — Ways: $3$; Probability: $\frac{3}{36}$
> - **Sum $5$** — Ways: $4$; Probability: $\frac{4}{36}$
> - **Sum $6$** — Ways: $5$; Probability: $\frac{5}{36}$
> - **Sum $7$** — Ways: $6$; Probability: $\frac{6}{36}$
> - **Sum $8$** — Ways: $5$; Probability: $\frac{5}{36}$
> - **Sum $9$** — Ways: $4$; Probability: $\frac{4}{36}$
> - **Sum $10$** — Ways: $3$; Probability: $\frac{3}{36}$
> - **Sum $11$** — Ways: $2$; Probability: $\frac{2}{36}$
> - **Sum $12$** — Ways: $1$; Probability: $\frac{1}{36}$
>
> This distribution is symmetric around $7$ and relatively concentrated.
>
> ### Risk measure
>
> For one die,
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[D^2] = \frac{1^2+2^2+3^2+4^2+5^2+6^2}{6} = \frac{91}{6},
> $$
> so
> $$
> \operatorname{Var}(D) = \frac{91}{6} - (3.5)^2 = \frac{35}{12}.
> $$
>
> Therefore
> $$
> \operatorname{Var}(S) = 2\cdot \frac{35}{12} = \frac{35}{6},
> $$
> and
> $$
> \operatorname{SD}(S) = \sqrt{\frac{35}{6}} \approx 2.42.
> $$
>
> So the sum is fairly low-risk.
>
> ### Example market
>
> A reasonable market could be
> $$
> 6.5 \text{ bid at } 10,\quad 7.5 \text{ ask at } 10.
> $$
>
> This says:
>
> - willing to **buy** up to $10$ units at $6.5$,
> - willing to **sell** up to $10$ units at $7.5$.
>
> Because the fair value is $7$, this is a symmetric $1$-point spread.
>
> A tighter market such as
> $$
> 6.8 \text{ bid},\quad 7.2 \text{ ask}
> $$
> might also be defensible if one wants to appear competitive. A wider market might be justified if the counterparty seems informed or if inventory matters.
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Product of two dice
>
> Now define
> $$
> P = D_1 D_2.
> $$
>
> ### Fair value
>
> Using independence,
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[P] = \mathbb{E}[D_1]\mathbb{E}[D_2] = 3.5 \cdot 3.5 = 12.25.
> $$
>
> Thus the fair value of the product is
> $$
> 12.25.
> $$
>
> ### Distribution
>
> The product takes values
> $$
> 1,2,3,4,5,6,8,9,10,12,15,16,18,20,24,25,30,36,
> $$
> with varying multiplicities among the $36$ equally likely outcomes.
>
> For example:
>
> - $1$ occurs only via $(1,1)$,
> - $6$ occurs via $(1,6),(6,1),(2,3),(3,2)$,
> - $12$ occurs via $(2,6),(6,2),(3,4),(4,3)$,
> - $36$ occurs only via $(6,6)$.
>
> The distribution is much less symmetric than for the sum and has more mass in the upper tail.
>
> ### Risk measure
>
> Because $P = D_1D_2$ and the dice are independent,
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[P^2] = \mathbb{E}[D_1^2]\mathbb{E}[D_2^2] = \left(\frac{91}{6}\right)^2.
> $$
>
> Hence
> $$
> \operatorname{Var}(P) = \left(\frac{91}{6}\right)^2 - (12.25)^2.
> $$
>
> Compute:
> $$
> \left(\frac{91}{6}\right)^2 = \frac{8281}{36} \approx 230.028,
> $$
> and
> $$
> (12.25)^2 = 150.0625.
> $$
>
> So
> $$
> \operatorname{Var}(P) \approx 79.965,
> $$
> and
> $$
> \operatorname{SD}(P) \approx 8.94.
> $$
>
> Thus the product is substantially more volatile than the sum.
>
> ### Example market
>
> A reasonable market could be
> $$
> 11 \text{ bid at } 5,\quad 13.5 \text{ ask at } 5.
> $$
>
> This is wider and smaller than for the sum because:
>
> - fair value is $12.25$,
> - the distribution is more dispersed,
> - extreme outcomes such as $25,30,36$ create more risk.
>
> One might also quote
> $$
> 10.5 \text{ bid at } 10,\quad 14 \text{ ask at } 10
> $$
> if one prefers a rounder and safer market.
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. How to justify the size
>
> If asked how many units you would trade, a simple rule is:
>
> - for the **sum**, trade **larger size**,
> - for the **product**, trade **smaller size**.
>
> This is because the product has much higher variance. A concise interview-style justification is:
>
> “I would quote around fair value, but with a wider spread and smaller size on the product than on the sum, since the product has significantly more dispersion and fatter upside outcomes.”
>
> For example:
>
> - **sum**: $10$ or $20$ units,
> - **product**: $5$ or $10$ units.
>
> ---
>
> ## 5. What the interviewer is looking for
>
> A strong response demonstrates the following:
>
> ### For the sum
> - fair value:
>   $$
>   7
>   $$
> - relatively tight market, e.g.
>   $$
>   6.5 \text{ bid},\ 7.5 \text{ ask}
>   $$
>
> ### For the product
> - fair value:
>   $$
>   12.25
>   $$
> - wider market, e.g.
>   $$
>   11 \text{ bid},\ 13.5 \text{ ask}
>   $$
>
> ### Additional good practice
> Mention that your quoted price depends on:
>
> - whether the other side chooses buy or sell after hearing your price,
> - your current inventory,
> - whether you must quote size before or after price,
> - whether repeated rounds allow you to manage inventory.
>
> If the counterparty can choose whether to buy or sell after hearing your market, then you should be especially careful not to quote too tightly.
>
> ---
>
> ## Final answer
>
> For two fair six-sided dice:
>
> - If the payoff is the **sum**, then
>   $$
>   \mathbb{E}[D_1 + D_2] = 7.
>   $$
>   A reasonable market is
>   $$
>   6.5 \text{ bid at } 10,\quad 7.5 \text{ ask at } 10.
>   $$
>
> - If the payoff is the **product**, then
>   $$
>   \mathbb{E}[D_1D_2] = 12.25.
>   $$
>   A reasonable market is
>   $$
>   11 \text{ bid at } 5,\quad 13.5 \text{ ask at } 5.
>   $$
>
> The key idea is that the product has much greater variance than the sum, so it warrants a wider spread and smaller size.
