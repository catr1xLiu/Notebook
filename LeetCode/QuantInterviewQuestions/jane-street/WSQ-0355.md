> [!example] Question 355 · Jane Street
> ## Trader Interview

Is Simpson's paradox possible? If so, provide an example illustrating the paradox.

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=356)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Example exhibiting Simpson’s paradox
>
> Yes, it is possible. Simpson’s paradox occurs when a comparison that holds within each of several groups reverses when the groups are combined.
>
> A standard way to construct such an example is with two treatments, $A$ and $B$, tested on two subgroups.
>
> ### Step 1: Choose subgroup data
>
> Suppose the success counts are as follows.
>
> #### Group 1
> - Treatment $A$: $81$ successes out of $90$
> - Treatment $B$: $8$ successes out of $10$
>
> So the success rates are
>
> $$
> \frac{81}{90}=0.90, \qquad \frac{8}{10}=0.80.
> $$
>
> Thus, in Group $1$, treatment $A$ is better than treatment $B$.
>
> #### Group 2
> - Treatment $A$: $1$ success out of $10$
> - Treatment $B$: $72$ successes out of $90$
>
> So the success rates are
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{10}=0.10, \qquad \frac{72}{90}=0.80.
> $$
>
> Again, in Group $2$, treatment $A$ is worse than $B$ as written, so this does **not** yet show Simpson’s paradox. We want $A$ to be better in each group but worse overall. Adjust the data slightly.
>
> Take instead:
>
> #### Group 1
> - Treatment $A$: $19$ successes out of $20$
> - Treatment $B$: $180$ successes out of $200$
>
> Then
>
> $$
> \frac{19}{20}=0.95, \qquad \frac{180}{200}=0.90,
> $$
>
> so $A$ is better than $B$ in Group $1$.
>
> #### Group 2
> - Treatment $A$: $1$ success out of $10$
> - Treatment $B$: $8$ successes out of $100$
>
> Then
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{10}=0.10, \qquad \frac{8}{100}=0.08,
> $$
>
> so $A$ is also better than $B$ in Group $2$.
>
> ### Step 2: Combine the groups
>
> Now total the results.
>
> #### Treatment $A$
> Total successes:
>
> $$
> 19+1=20
> $$
>
> Total trials:
>
> $$
> 20+10=30
> $$
>
> Overall success rate:
>
> $$
> \frac{20}{30}=\frac{2}{3}\approx 0.667
> $$
>
> #### Treatment $B$
> Total successes:
>
> $$
> 180+8=188
> $$
>
> Total trials:
>
> $$
> 200+100=300
> $$
>
> Overall success rate:
>
> $$
> \frac{188}{300}\approx 0.627
> $$
>
> This still gives $A>B$ overall, so let us choose a better-known example where the reversal actually occurs.
>
> ---
>
> ## Correct example
>
> Consider the following table.
>
> ### Group 1
> - $A$: $81/90$
> - $B$: $234/270$
>
> Then
>
> $$
> \frac{81}{90}=0.90, \qquad \frac{234}{270}\approx 0.867,
> $$
>
> so $A$ is better in Group $1$.
>
> ### Group 2
> - $A$: $19/210$
> - $B$: $6/10$
>
> Then
>
> $$
> \frac{19}{210}\approx 0.0905, \qquad \frac{6}{10}=0.60,
> $$
>
> this again does not work. So let us construct one systematically.
>
> ---
>
> ## A clean construction
>
> Take two groups.
>
> ### Group 1
> - $A$: $9$ successes out of $10$
> - $B$: $80$ successes out of $100$
>
> Then
>
> $$
> \frac{9}{10}=0.90, \qquad \frac{80}{100}=0.80,
> $$
>
> so $A$ is better.
>
> ### Group 2
> - $A$: $18$ successes out of $90$
> - $B$: $1$ success out of $10$
>
> Then
>
> $$
> \frac{18}{90}=0.20, \qquad \frac{1}{10}=0.10,
> $$
>
> so $A$ is again better.
>
> Now combine them.
>
> For $A$:
>
> $$
> \frac{9+18}{10+90}=\frac{27}{100}=0.27
> $$
>
> For $B$:
>
> $$
> \frac{80+1}{100+10}=\frac{81}{110}\approx 0.736
> $$
>
> Thus,
>
> $$
> 0.27 < 0.736.
> $$
>
> So although treatment $A$ has a higher success rate in **each group separately**, treatment $B$ has the higher success rate **overall**.
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> This is Simpson’s paradox:
>
> - In Group $1$, $A>B$
> - In Group $2$, $A>B$
> - But overall, $A<B$
>
> Hence, it is indeed possible. A concrete example is:
>
> $$
> \begin{array}{c|cc}
> & \text{Successes} & \text{Trials} \\
> \hline
> A \text{ in Group 1} & 9 & 10 \\
> B \text{ in Group 1} & 80 & 100 \\
> A \text{ in Group 2} & 18 & 90 \\
> B \text{ in Group 2} & 1 & 10
> \end{array}
> $$
>
> with subgroup comparisons
>
> $$
> \frac{9}{10}>\frac{80}{100}, \qquad \frac{18}{90}>\frac{1}{10},
> $$
>
> but overall comparison
>
> $$
> \frac{27}{100}<\frac{81}{110}.
> $$
>
> This is an explicit example of Simpson’s paradox.
