> [!example] Question 108 · Jane Street
> ## Trading Interview

Flip a coin. If it lands heads, I win 1 point; if tails, you win 1 point. The first person to reach 2 points wins the game, and the loser pays the winner \$1. However, I have an option to increase the stake to \$2 per game. What is the value of this option?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=109)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> Model the game by the score difference
> $$
> D = (\text{your points}) - (\text{my points}).
> $$
> Initially,
> $$
> D=0.
> $$
> Each coin flip changes $D$ by $\pm 1$ with probability $1/2$ each, and the game ends when one player reaches $2$ points. Thus the possible nonterminal states are
> $$
> D \in \{-1,0,1\},
> $$
> and the absorbing winning states are
> $$
> D=2 \quad \text{(you win)}, \qquad D=-2 \quad \text{(I win)}.
> $$
>
> Without any option, the game is fair by symmetry, so the expected payoff is
> $$
> 0.
> $$
>
> The option allows you, at some point during the game, to increase the final stake from $\text{\textdollar}1$ to $\text{\textdollar}2$. Since this benefits the player who is more likely to win from the current state, the optimal strategy is to exercise the option only when the position is favorable to you.
>
> Let
> $$
> V(d)
> $$
> be the value of the game to you when the current score difference is $d$ and you still retain the option.
>
> Also let
> $$
> W(d)
> $$
> be the expected payoff to you if the stake has already been fixed at $\text{\textdollar}2$ from state $d$ onward.
>
> Then $W(d)$ is just $\text{\textdollar}2$ times the probability of your eventual win minus $\text{\textdollar}2$ times the probability of your eventual loss.
>
> ### Step 1: Compute the win probabilities
>
> Let
> $$
> p_d = \Pr(\text{you eventually win} \mid D=d).
> $$
> Then:
> - From $D=1$, one more head wins immediately, while a tail moves to $D=0$:
>   $$
>   p_1 = \frac12 \cdot 1 + \frac12 p_0.
>   $$
> - From $D=-1$, one more tail loses immediately, while a head moves to $D=0$:
>   $$
>   p_{-1} = \frac12 p_0 + \frac12 \cdot 0 = \frac12 p_0.
>   $$
> - From $D=0$:
>   $$
>   p_0 = \frac12 p_1 + \frac12 p_{-1}.
>   $$
>
> Substitute the first two equations into the third:
> $$
> p_0 = \frac12\left(\frac12 + \frac12 p_0\right) + \frac12\left(\frac12 p_0\right)
> = \frac14 + \frac12 p_0.
> $$
> Hence
> $$
> p_0 = \frac12.
> $$
> Then
> $$
> p_1 = \frac12 + \frac14 = \frac34, \qquad p_{-1} = \frac14.
> $$
>
> Therefore the expected payoff with stake $\text{\textdollar}2$ is
> $$
> W(d) = 2\bigl(p_d - (1-p_d)\bigr) = 2(2p_d-1).
> $$
> So:
> $$
> W(1) = 2\left(2\cdot \frac34 -1\right)=1,
> $$
> $$
> W(0) = 2\left(2\cdot \frac12 -1\right)=0,
> $$
> $$
> W(-1) = 2\left(2\cdot \frac14 -1\right)=-1.
> $$
>
> Thus if you can choose whether to double:
> - at $D=1$, doubling gives expected value $\text{\textdollar}1$;
> - at $D=0$, doubling gives expected value $\text{\textdollar}0$;
> - at $D=-1$, doubling gives expected value $-\text{\textdollar}1$, so you would not do it.
>
> ### Step 2: Optimal stopping recursion
>
> Now compute $V(d)$.
>
> At $D=1$, you should exercise immediately, since without exercising the stake remains $\text{\textdollar}1$, whose value from $D=1$ is only
> $$
> 2p_1-1 = \frac12,
> $$
> whereas exercising gives value
> $$
> W(1)=1.
> $$
> So
> $$
> V(1)=1.
> $$
>
> At $D=-1$, you should not exercise, since doubling would hurt you. If you wait one flip:
> - with probability $1/2$, a head moves to $D=0$;
> - with probability $1/2$, a tail ends the game with your loss of $\text{\textdollar}1$.
>
> Hence
> $$
> V(-1)=\frac12 V(0)+\frac12(-1).
> $$
>
> At $D=0$, after one flip:
> - with probability $1/2$, the state becomes $D=1$, worth $V(1)=1$;
> - with probability $1/2$, the state becomes $D=-1$, worth $V(-1)$.
>
> Thus
> $$
> V(0)=\frac12\cdot 1+\frac12 V(-1).
> $$
>
> Substitute the formula for $V(-1)$:
> $$
> V(0)=\frac12+\frac12\left(\frac12 V(0)-\frac12\right)
> = \frac12 + \frac14 V(0)-\frac14
> = \frac14 + \frac14 V(0).
> $$
> Therefore
> $$
> \frac34 V(0)=\frac14,
> $$
> so
> $$
> V(0)=\frac13.
> $$
>
> Since the game without the option has value $0$, the value of the option itself is
> $$
> \boxed{\$ \frac13 }.
> $$
>
> ### Optimal strategy
> Do not exercise at the start.  
> Exercise only if the first flip is a head, putting you ahead $1$-$0$.  
> If the first flip is a tail, never exercise.
>
> Hence the option to increase the stake to $\text{\textdollar}2$ is worth
> $$
> \boxed{\$ \frac13 }.
> $$
