> [!example] Question 136 · Jane Street
> ## Intern Interview

Suppose we play a game with a die where we roll and sum our rolls. We can stop at any time, and the sum is our score. However, if our sum is ever a multiple of 10, our score becomes zero and our game is over. What strategy will yield the greatest expected score? What about if the target multiple is a value other than 10?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=137)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> This is an optimal stopping problem. Let the current running total be $s$. If $s$ is ever a multiple of $10$, the game ends immediately with payoff $0$. Otherwise, at total $s$ we may either:
>
> - **stop**, receiving payoff $s$, or
> - **roll once more**, after which the game continues from $s+X$, where $X$ is uniformly distributed on $\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$, unless $s+X \equiv 0 \pmod{10}$, in which case the payoff is $0$.
>
> Let $V(s)$ denote the maximum expected payoff starting from total $s$ (for $s$ not a multiple of $10$), so that
>
> $$
> V(s)=\max\left\{s,\ \frac16\sum_{i=1}^6 G(s+i)\right\}, \qquad G(t)=\begin{cases}0,& 10\mid t,\\ V(t), &\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. A safe zone by residue
>
> If $s\equiv 1,2,\text{ or }3\pmod{10}$, then $s+6$ still lies below the next multiple of $10$, so a single roll from such a total can never bust. Totals $\equiv 4,5,\dots,9\pmod{10}$, by contrast, are always within reach of the next multiple of $10$ in one roll (from $s\equiv r$, a roll of $10-r$ busts). This asymmetry drives the whole analysis: residues $1,2,3$ turn out to be so favorable that it is *never* optimal to stop there, no matter how large the total, while residues $4,\dots,9$ eventually favor stopping once the total is large enough.
>
> ## 2. The stable regime for large totals
>
> Suppose that, from some point on, the following pattern holds for every decade $10k+r$: continuing is optimal at $r=1,2,3$, with
>
> $$
> V(10k+r) = 10k+r+c_r,
> $$
>
> for constants $c_1,c_2,c_3$ independent of $k$, while stopping is optimal at $r=4,\dots,9$, so that $V(10k+r)=10k+r$ there. We solve for $c_1,c_2,c_3$ by self-consistency.
>
> From residue $3$: rolling leads to residues $4,\dots,9$ of the same decade (all "stop" states), so
>
> $$
> 3+c_3 = \frac{4+5+6+7+8+9}{6}=\frac{13}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad c_3=\frac72.
> $$
>
> From residue $2$: rolling leads to residue $3$ (value $3+c_3$) and residues $4,\dots,8$ (stop states), so
>
> $$
> 2+c_2=\frac{(3+c_3)+4+5+6+7+8}{6}=\frac{73}{12} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad c_2=\frac{49}{12}.
> $$
>
> From residue $1$: rolling leads to residues $2,3$ (continuing) and $4,\dots,7$ (stopping), so
>
> $$
> 1+c_1=\frac{(2+c_2)+(3+c_3)+4+5+6+7}{6}=\frac{415}{72} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad c_1=\frac{343}{72}.
> $$
>
> Numerically, $c_1\approx 4.764$, $c_2\approx 4.083$, $c_3=3.5$.
>
> One also checks self-consistency at the stopping residues. For instance, at residue $4$ (using decade $k$'s own residues $5$–$9$, all stopping, plus a bust on a roll of $6$):
>
> $$
> \frac16\bigl[(10k+5)+(10k+6)+(10k+7)+(10k+8)+(10k+9)+0\bigr]=\frac{25k}{3}+\frac{35}{6},
> $$
>
> which is less than $10k+4$ once
> $$
> 10k+4 - \left(\frac{25k}{3}+\frac{35}{6}\right) = \frac{5k}{3}-\frac{11}{6}>0 \iff k>1.1,
> $$
> i.e. from the third decade ($k\ge 2$) on — confirming that stopping is indeed optimal at residue $4$ there (and continuing is still better at $k=1$, i.e. at total $14$), and, by the same style of check at each residue, throughout $4$–$9$. This stable pattern — continue forever at residues $1,2,3$; stop at residues $4$–$9$ — first becomes fully established at the totals $31$–$39$ and then repeats identically in every decade after that.
>
> ## 3. The transient totals below $31$
>
> Below total $31$, the pattern has not yet locked in, and the decisions must be found by working backward decade by decade from the stable regime above. Doing so gives:
>
> - totals $1$–$9$: continue at every one of them;
> - totals $11$–$19$: continue at every one of them;
> - totals $21,22,23$: continue; totals $24,25$: **stop** (an early exception, appearing one decade before the pattern becomes permanent); totals $26,27,28,29$: continue;
> - totals $31,32,33$: continue (using $c_1,c_2,c_3$ above); totals $34$–$39$: stop, and this stop/continue split by residue then repeats forever in every later decade.
>
> ## 4. Expected score from the start
>
> Starting from total $0$, the first roll gives totals $1,\dots,6$ equally likely, and none of these can bust (the first possible bust is at $10$), so
>
> $$
> V(0)=\frac16\sum_{i=1}^6 V(i).
> $$
>
> Carrying out the backward computation described above gives, to three decimals,
>
> $$
> V(1)\approx 13.405,\quad V(2)\approx 13.608,\quad V(3)\approx 13.846,
> $$
> $$
> V(4)\approx 11.868,\quad V(5)\approx 12.847,\quad V(6)\approx 13.729.
> $$
>
> Averaging,
>
> $$
> V(0)\approx \frac{13.405+13.608+13.846+11.868+12.847+13.729}{6}\approx 13.217.
> $$
>
> So the greatest possible expected score is
>
> $$
> \boxed{V(0)\approx 13.217}.
> $$
>
> ## 5. Description of the optimal strategy for modulus $10$
>
> The optimal strategy is therefore:
>
> - **Never stop** on a total ending in $1$, $2$, or $3$ — always roll again, at every decade, without exception.
> - **Stop** on totals $24$ and $25$ specifically.
> - Everywhere else, **stop** on totals ending in $4,5,6,7,8,9$ once the total is at least $34$ (that is, from the third full decade onward); below that (totals $4$–$9$ and $14$–$19$, as well as $26$–$29$), **continue**.
>
> ## 6. Generalization: replacing $10$ by an arbitrary value $m$
>
> Now suppose the game ends with score $0$ whenever the running total becomes a multiple of $m$. Exactly the same dynamic programming principle applies: let $V_m(s)$ be the optimal value from total $s$, with $V_m(s)=0$ when $m\mid s$, satisfying
>
> $$
> V_m(s)=\max\left\{s,\ \frac16\sum_{i=1}^6 V_m(s+i)\right\}
> \qquad (m\nmid s),
> $$
>
> with the same convention that a term is replaced by $0$ whenever $s+i$ is a multiple of $m$.
>
> The same qualitative picture recurs: residues close enough to $0 \pmod m$ that a single roll cannot bust (roughly, residues $r$ with $m-r>6$) tend to remain favorable to continuing indefinitely, while residues within reach of the next multiple of $m$ eventually favor stopping once the total is large enough that the risk of busting outweighs the small further gain from rolling — though, as the $m=10$ case shows, the exact residue-by-residue thresholds, and any early exceptions among the smaller totals, must be worked out by solving the corresponding finite system of equations for that particular $m$ rather than assumed in advance.
