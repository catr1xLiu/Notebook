> [!example] Question 457 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Trader Interview

Three random points are chosen on the circumference of a circle. What is the probability that a table with legs placed at those points would stand (i.e., that all three points lie on the same semicircle)?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=458)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let the three random points on the circumference determine the three legs of the table.
>
> A three-legged table stands on a flat floor if and only if the three legs are not all contained in some semicircle of the circumference. Equivalently, the center of the circle must lie inside the triangle formed by the three points. Thus the problem reduces to finding the probability that three random points on a circle are **not** all contained in a semicircle.
>
> ## Step 1: Compute the complementary probability
>
> Choose the three points independently and uniformly on the circle.
>
> Fix one of the points, say $P_1$. Consider the semicircle of length $180^\circ$ starting at $P_1$ and going clockwise. If both other points $P_2$ and $P_3$ fall into this semicircle, then all three points lie in some semicircle.
>
> For a fixed $P_1$, each of $P_2$ and $P_3$ has probability $\tfrac12$ of lying in that semicircle, so
>
> $$
> \Pr(P_2, P_3 \text{ both in that semicircle}) = \left(\frac12\right)^2 = \frac14.
> $$
>
> Now observe: if three points lie in some semicircle, then there is a unique point among them that is the clockwise endpoint of the smallest arc containing all three points. Hence the event “all three points lie in a semicircle” is partitioned into $3$ disjoint cases, according to which point plays this role.
>
> Therefore,
>
> $$
> \Pr(\text{all three points lie in some semicircle}) = 3 \cdot \frac14 = \frac34.
> $$
>
> ## Step 2: Take the complement
>
> Hence the probability that the three points are **not** all in a semicircle is
>
> $$
> 1 - \frac34 = \frac14.
> $$
>
> Thus the probability that the three-legged table stands is
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac14}.
> $$
