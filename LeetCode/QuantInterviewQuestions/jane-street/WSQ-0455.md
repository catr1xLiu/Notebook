> [!example] Question 455 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Trader Interview

You roll a standard 6-sided die (d6) and a 10-sided die (d10). Before seeing the result, you must bid on the sum of the two dice. If your bid matches the actual sum, you win that amount. What is the optimal sum to bid in order to maximize your expected winnings?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=456)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> The payoff structure is: you choose an integer bid $k$ before the dice are rolled; if the sum equals $k$, you win $k$ dollars, and otherwise you win nothing. To maximize your expected winnings, you must maximize
> $$
> E[\text{winnings}\mid \text{bid }k]=k\cdot P(X+Y=k),
> $$
> not the expected value of the sum itself — matching the expected sum would only be the right target if you were paid according to how close your bid was, which is not the payoff rule here.
>
> ## 1. Distribution of the sum
>
> Let $X$ be the result of the $6$-sided die (uniform on $\{1,\dots,6\}$) and $Y$ the result of the $10$-sided die (uniform on $\{1,\dots,10\}$), independent, so there are $60$ equally likely pairs $(X,Y)$ and the sum $S=X+Y$ ranges from $2$ to $16$.
>
> For each possible sum $k$, count the pairs $(a,b)$ with $a\in\{1,\dots,6\}$, $b\in\{1,\dots,10\}$, $a+b=k$:
>
> - $k=2$: $1$ pair
> - $k=3$: $2$ pairs
> - $k=4$: $3$ pairs
> - $k=5$: $4$ pairs
> - $k=6$: $5$ pairs
> - $k=7$: $6$ pairs
> - $k=8$: $6$ pairs
> - $k=9$: $6$ pairs
> - $k=10$: $6$ pairs
> - $k=11$: $6$ pairs
> - $k=12$: $5$ pairs
> - $k=13$: $4$ pairs
> - $k=14$: $3$ pairs
> - $k=15$: $2$ pairs
> - $k=16$: $1$ pair
>
> These sum to $60$, as required. In particular,
> $$
> P(S=k)=\frac{1}{10}\qquad\text{for } k=7,8,9,10,11,
> $$
> the widest, flattest part of the distribution.
>
> ## 2. Maximize k times P(S=k)
>
> Since winning requires bidding exactly the realized sum, the expected payoff from bidding $k$ is $k\cdot P(S=k)$. Using the counts above, each divided by $60$:
>
> - $k=7$: $7\cdot\frac{6}{60}=0.70$
> - $k=8$: $8\cdot\frac{6}{60}=0.80$
> - $k=9$: $9\cdot\frac{6}{60}=0.90$
> - $k=10$: $10\cdot\frac{6}{60}=1.00$
> - $k=11$: $11\cdot\frac{6}{60}=1.10$
> - $k=12$: $12\cdot\frac{5}{60}\approx0.83$
> - $k=6$: $6\cdot\frac{5}{60}=0.50$
>
> Among all values of $k$ from $2$ to $16$, the product $k\cdot P(S=k)$ is maximized at $k=11$: this is the largest bid within the flat region $\{7,\dots,11\}$ where $P(S=k)=\frac1{10}$ is as large as it ever gets, so multiplying by the largest such $k$ gives the biggest expected payoff. Once $k$ moves to $12$ or beyond, $P(S=k)$ drops enough to outweigh the larger $k$.
>
> ## 3. Conclusion
>
> The optimal bid is $k=11$, with expected winnings
> $$
> 11\cdot\frac{6}{60}=11\cdot\frac1{10}=1.1
> $$
> dollars.
>
> By contrast, the expected value of the sum itself is $E[X+Y]=3.5+5.5=9$ — but this answers a different question, namely what value the sum tends to take on average, not which bid maximizes expected payout under a "bid must match exactly" rule.
>
> ## Final answer
>
> $$
> \boxed{\text{Optimal bid}=11,\qquad \text{expected winnings}=1.1\text{ dollars}.}
> $$
