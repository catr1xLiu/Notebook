> [!example] Question 669 · Jane Street
> ## Quant Trader Intern Interview

There are two balls in a bag, each either black or white. If you draw a white ball, put it back, then draw again and it's still a white ball, what is the probability of drawing a white ball the next time?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=670)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## 1. Probability the next draw is white
>
> Let the bag contain two balls, each of which is either white or black. Assume, a priori, that the three possible compositions are equally likely:
>
> - $WW$
> - $WB$
> - $BB$
>
> You draw a white ball, replace it, draw again, and again obtain a white ball. We want
>
> $$
> P(\text{next draw is white} \mid \text{first two draws were white}).
> $$
>
> Because draws are with replacement, conditional on the composition:
>
> - If the bag is $WW$, then the probability of two white draws is $1$.
> - If the bag is $WB$, then the probability of two white draws is $\left(\tfrac12\right)^2=\tfrac14$.
> - If the bag is $BB$, then the probability of two white draws is $0$.
>
> By Bayes' rule, the posterior probabilities of the possible bag types after observing two white draws are proportional to
>
> $$
> P(WW)\cdot 1,\quad P(WB)\cdot \frac14,\quad P(BB)\cdot 0.
> $$
>
> Since each prior probability is $\tfrac13$, these are proportional to
>
> $$
> \frac13,\quad \frac1{12},\quad 0.
> $$
>
> Normalizing,
>
> $$
> P(WW\mid WW\text{ observed})=\frac{\frac13}{\frac13+\frac1{12}}=\frac{4}{5},
> $$
>
> and
>
> $$
> P(WB\mid WW\text{ observed})=\frac{\frac1{12}}{\frac13+\frac1{12}}=\frac{1}{5}.
> $$
>
> Now the probability the next draw is white is
>
> $$
> P(\text{white next}\mid \text{two whites observed})
> =
> P(WW\mid \cdot)\cdot 1 + P(WB\mid \cdot)\cdot \frac12.
> $$
>
> Thus
>
> $$
> \frac45\cdot 1 + \frac15\cdot \frac12
> =
> \frac45+\frac1{10}
> =
> \frac9{10}.
> $$
>
> Therefore, the probability is
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{9}{10}}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Optimal stopping rule for the red-black card game
>
> You have a shuffled deck of $52$ cards, with $26$ red and $26$ black. Cards are drawn without replacement. Each red card gives $+1$, each black card gives $-1$. You may stop at any time, and your payoff is the total accumulated score up to that point.
>
> We seek the stopping rule that maximizes expected earnings.
>
> ### Step 1: Reformulate the problem
>
> Suppose at some stage there are $r$ red cards and $b$ black cards remaining in the deck, and your current score is irrelevant for the decision from now onward. Let
>
> $$
> V(r,b)
> $$
>
> denote the maximal expected **additional** gain from this point onward.
>
> At each state $(r,b)$, you have two choices:
>
> 1. **Stop now**, yielding additional gain $0$.
> 2. **Continue**, in which case:
>    - with probability $\frac{r}{r+b}$, the next card is red, giving immediate gain $+1$ and moving to $(r-1,b)$;
>    - with probability $\frac{b}{r+b}$, the next card is black, giving immediate gain $-1$ and moving to $(r,b-1)$.
>
> Hence
>
> $$
> V(r,b)=\max\left\{0,\;
> \frac{r}{r+b}\bigl(1+V(r-1,b)\bigr)+
> \frac{b}{r+b}\bigl(-1+V(r,b-1)\bigr)
> \right\}.
> $$
>
> This is the dynamic programming equation.
>
> ### Step 2: Determine when continuing is favorable
>
> The key observation is that the deck state matters only through the imbalance between remaining red and black cards.
>
> If $r>b$, then the expected value of the **next** card is positive:
>
> $$
> E[\text{next increment}]
> =
> \frac{r-b}{r+b}>0.
> $$
>
> If $r\le b$, then the next card has nonpositive expectation.
>
> Moreover, when $r>b$, there is an advantage in continuing because the remaining deck is favorable on average. When $r\le b$, there is no positive expected gain available from future play, so stopping is optimal.
>
> This leads to the optimal rule:
>
> $$
> \text{Continue if } r>b,\qquad \text{stop if } r\le b.
> $$
>
> ### Step 3: Interpret the rule in terms of cards already seen
>
> Initially, $r=b=26$.
>
> After some cards have been drawn, let $R$ be the number of red cards already seen and $B$ the number of black cards already seen. Then the remaining numbers are
>
> $$
> r=26-R,\qquad b=26-B.
> $$
>
> Thus
>
> $$
> r>b
> \iff
> 26-R>26-B
> \iff
> B>R.
> $$
>
> So the optimal stopping rule may be stated equivalently as:
>
> - **Keep drawing as long as more black than red cards have appeared so far.**
> - **Stop as soon as the number of red cards seen is at least the number of black cards seen.**
>
> Since the current score is $R-B$, this is the same as:
>
> $$
> \text{Stop as soon as your cumulative score is nonnegative.}
> $$
>
> At the very start, the score is $0$, so if stopping immediately is allowed, then immediate stopping yields $0$, and no strategy can guarantee more than the optimal expected value from continuation. The rule above says that whenever the remaining deck is not favorable, one should stop.
>
> ### Final answer
>
> 1. The probability that the next ball is white is
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{9}{10}}.
> $$
>
> 2. The optimal strategy is:
>
> $$
> \boxed{\text{Continue drawing only while the remaining deck has more red than black cards.}}
> $$
>
> Equivalently,
>
> $$
> \boxed{\text{Stop as soon as your running score is } \ge 0.}
> $$
>
> That is, after observing cards, continue exactly when you have seen more black cards than red cards so far.
