> [!example] Question 5 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

Given the probabilities with which your opponent will play Rock, Paper, and Scissors, how should you respond to maximize your expected winnings?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=6)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let the opponent choose
>
> - Rock with probability $p_R$,
> - Paper with probability $p_P$,
> - Scissors with probability $p_S$,
>
> where
>
> $$
> p_R + p_P + p_S = 1.
> $$
>
> We want the optimal response in Rock-Paper-Scissors.
>
> ## Expected payoff of each pure action
>
> Use the standard payoff: win $= +1$, lose $= -1$, tie $= 0$.
>
> ### If you play Rock
> - against Rock: payoff $0$,
> - against Paper: payoff $-1$,
> - against Scissors: payoff $+1$.
>
> So the expected payoff is
>
> $$
> E_R = 0\cdot p_R + (-1)\cdot p_P + 1\cdot p_S = p_S - p_P.
> $$
>
> ### If you play Paper
> - against Rock: payoff $+1$,
> - against Paper: payoff $0$,
> - against Scissors: payoff $-1$.
>
> Thus
>
> $$
> E_P = p_R - p_S.
> $$
>
> ### If you play Scissors
> - against Rock: payoff $-1$,
> - against Paper: payoff $+1$,
> - against Scissors: payoff $0$.
>
> Thus
>
> $$
> E_S = p_P - p_R.
> $$
>
> ## Optimal strategy
>
> Choose the action with the largest expected payoff among
>
> $$
> E_R = p_S - p_P,\qquad
> E_P = p_R - p_S,\qquad
> E_S = p_P - p_R.
> $$
>
> Equivalently:
>
> - play Rock if
>
> $$
> p_S - p_P \ge p_R - p_S
> \quad\text{and}\quad
> p_S - p_P \ge p_P - p_R;
> $$
>
> - play Paper if
>
> $$
> p_R - p_S \ge p_S - p_P
> \quad\text{and}\quad
> p_R - p_S \ge p_P - p_R;
> $$
>
> - play Scissors if
>
> $$
> p_P - p_R \ge p_S - p_P
> \quad\text{and}\quad
> p_P - p_R \ge p_R - p_S.
> $$
>
> ## Interpretation
>
> Notice that the three expected payoffs always sum to zero,
>
> $$
> E_R+E_P+E_S=(p_S-p_P)+(p_R-p_S)+(p_P-p_R)=0,
> $$
>
> so the optimal action is whichever of the three is largest. It is tempting to guess that this always means "play the move that beats the opponent's single most likely action," but that shortcut is not correct in general. The optimal move depends on the *entire* distribution $(p_R,p_P,p_S)$, not just on which probability is largest.
>
> For a counterexample, take
>
> $$
> p_R=0.5,\qquad p_P=0.1,\qquad p_S=0.4.
> $$
>
> The opponent's most likely move is Rock, so the naive heuristic recommends playing Paper. But the actual expected payoffs are
>
> $$
> E_R = p_S-p_P = 0.3,\qquad
> E_P = p_R-p_S = 0.1,\qquad
> E_S = p_P-p_R = -0.4.
> $$
>
> Here $E_R$ is the largest of the three, so the optimal response is Rock, not Paper. Rock ties the opponent's most likely move (Rock) but wins decisively against the opponent's second most likely move (Scissors, played with probability $0.4$), and this outweighs the smaller edge Paper would gain against Rock alone. So the best response must be found by comparing all three expected payoffs directly, not by identifying the single most probable opponent action.
>
> ## Final answer
>
> Given opponent probabilities $(p_R,p_P,p_S)$, compute the three quantities
>
> $$
> p_S-p_P,\qquad p_R-p_S,\qquad p_P-p_R.
> $$
>
> Then choose, respectively,
>
> - Rock if $p_S-p_P$ is largest,
> - Paper if $p_R-p_S$ is largest,
> - Scissors if $p_P-p_R$ is largest,
>
> breaking ties arbitrarily among the maximizers. This maximizes your expected winnings against the given distribution; it is generally *not* the same as simply beating the opponent's single most probable choice.
