> [!example] Question 82 · Jane Street
> ## Trading Interview

On an island, there are 99 lions and one sheep. When a lion eats the sheep, he transforms into a sheep. Every lion's primary objective is survival, and their secondary goal is to eat a sheep. All lions are intelligent and make optimal decisions. How many lions will be on the island after some time?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=83)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Consider the decision faced by a lion.
>
> If a lion eats a sheep, then immediately after eating it, that lion becomes a sheep. Since every lion’s first priority is to survive, a lion will eat a sheep only if becoming a sheep does not lead to certain death.
>
> The key is to analyze smaller cases and identify the pattern.
>
> ## Step 1: Small cases
>
> ### Case $1$: $1$ lion and $1$ sheep
> The single lion can eat the sheep and become a sheep. Then there are no lions left, so the new sheep survives forever. Hence the lion will eat.
>
> Final number of lions: $0$.
>
> ### Case $2$: $2$ lions and $1$ sheep
> If one lion eats the sheep, it becomes a sheep, leaving $1$ lion and $1$ sheep. From Case $1$, that remaining lion would eat the sheep. So the original eater would die.
>
> Since survival is the primary goal, neither lion will eat.
>
> Final number of lions: $2$.
>
> ### Case $3$: $3$ lions and $1$ sheep
> If one lion eats the sheep, it becomes a sheep, leaving $2$ lions and $1$ sheep. From Case $2$, with $2$ lions and $1$ sheep, no lion eats. Thus the transformed lion survives as a sheep.
>
> So a lion will eat.
>
> Final number of lions: after the first move, there remain $2$ lions.
>
> ## Step 2: General pattern
>
> Let $f(n)$ denote whether a lion will eat when there are $n$ lions and $1$ sheep.
>
> - If a lion eats, the state changes from $n$ lions and $1$ sheep to $n-1$ lions and $1$ sheep.
> - Therefore, a lion will choose to eat exactly when the resulting position with $n-1$ lions is one in which no lion will eat.
>
> Thus:
>
> - if lions do **not** eat for $n-1$, then lions **do** eat for $n$;
> - if lions **do** eat for $n-1$, then lions **do not** eat for $n$.
>
> This alternates by parity:
>
> - for odd $n$, a lion eats;
> - for even $n$, no lion eats.
>
> ## Step 3: Apply to $99$ lions
>
> Since $99$ is odd, one lion will eat the sheep. That lion becomes a sheep, leaving $98$ lions and $1$ sheep. Since $98$ is even, no lion will eat after that.
>
> Therefore the process stops with
>
> $$98$$
>
> lions remaining on the island.
