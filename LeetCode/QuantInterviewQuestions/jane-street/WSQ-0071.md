> [!example] Question 71 · Jane Street
> ## Trading Interview

There are 100 seats on a fully booked train. The first passenger is blind and takes a seat at random. Each subsequent passenger takes their assigned seat if it is free; if not, they choose one of the remaining empty seats at random. What is the probability that the last passenger sits in their assigned seat?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=72)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let the passengers and seats be numbered $1,2,\dots,100$, where passenger $i$ is assigned seat $i$.
>
> We seek the probability that passenger $100$ ends up in seat $100$.
>
> ## Key observation
>
> After passenger $1$ sits down randomly, each subsequent passenger behaves as follows:
>
> - If their own seat is free, they take it.
> - If not, they choose uniformly among the remaining empty seats.
>
> At any stage, the only seats that can matter to the eventual fate of passenger $100$ are:
>
> - seat $1$,
> - seat $100$,
> - and possibly some occupied seats in between.
>
> More precisely, whenever a passenger finds their own seat occupied and must choose randomly, the set of available seats includes seat $1$, seat $100$, and some higher-numbered seats. The process continues until one of two things happens:
>
> 1. someone chooses seat $1$, in which case every later passenger can sit in their own seat, so passenger $100$ gets seat $100$;
> 2. someone chooses seat $100$, in which case passenger $100$ loses their seat.
>
> Thus the process ends when either seat $1$ or seat $100$ is chosen at random.
>
> ## Symmetry argument
>
> At every random-choice step, seats $1$ and $100$ are completely symmetric:
>
> - neither belongs to the passenger currently choosing,
> - both are just available seats among the empty ones.
>
> Therefore, when the process finally selects one of these two special seats first, each is equally likely to be the one chosen.
>
> Hence,
> $$
> \mathbb{P}(\text{passenger }100\text{ gets seat }100)=\frac{1}{2}.
> $$
>
> ## Final answer
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{1}{2}}
> $$
