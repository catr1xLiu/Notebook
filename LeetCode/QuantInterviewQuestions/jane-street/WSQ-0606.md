> ## Assistant Trader Interview

What quantitative characteristics would you use to qualitatively assess the difficulty of a maze?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=607)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Quantitative characteristics for assessing maze difficulty
>
> A maze’s *difficulty* is qualitative, but it can be estimated from measurable structural features. A useful approach is to describe the maze as a graph and then evaluate quantities that affect how hard it is to navigate.
>
> ### 1. Basic size measures
>
> Let the maze be represented by a graph with:
>
> - $V$ = number of reachable cells or junctions,
> - $E$ = number of passages,
> - $L$ = length of the solution path from entrance to exit.
>
> Larger mazes are usually harder, but size alone is not sufficient. Two mazes with the same number of cells can differ greatly in difficulty.
>
> ### 2. Branching structure
>
> A major source of difficulty is the number of decisions the solver must make.
>
> - **Number of junctions:** count vertices with degree at least $3$.
> - **Average branching factor:**  
>   $$
>   b = \frac{1}{|J|}\sum_{v \in J} \deg(v),
>   $$
>   where $J$ is the set of junctions.
>
> More branching generally means more opportunities to choose incorrectly.
>
> ### 3. Dead ends
>
> Dead ends waste time and increase uncertainty.
>
> - **Number of dead ends:** count vertices of degree $1$, excluding entrance and exit if desired.
> - **Average dead-end depth:** average distance from a dead end back to the nearest junction.
> - **Maximum dead-end depth:** the deepest false path.
>
> A maze with many deep dead ends is usually harder than one with many shallow ones.
>
> ### 4. Correct-path obscurity
>
> Difficulty depends strongly on how hidden the correct route is.
>
> - **Solution-path decision count:** number of junctions on the true path.
> - **Wrong-turn opportunities:** total number of branches leaving the solution path that do not immediately continue toward the exit.
> - **Penalty for wrong choices:** expected extra path length caused by taking an incorrect branch.
>
> If wrong turns lead to long detours, the maze feels much harder.
>
> ### 5. Path-length ratios
>
> Compare the correct path to the overall maze.
>
> - **Solution length:** $L$.
> - **Total corridor length:** $T$.
> - **Relative solution length:**  
>   $$
>   \frac{L}{T}.
>   $$
> A small ratio means the true path is buried among many irrelevant corridors, which tends to increase difficulty.
>
> ### 6. Cycles versus tree structure
>
> A simply connected maze has no loops; in graph terms, it is a tree. Loops substantially affect difficulty.
>
> - **Cyclomatic number:**  
>   $$
>   \mu = E - V + C,
>   $$
>   where $C$ is the number of connected components; for a connected maze,  
>   $$
>   \mu = E - V + 1.
>   $$
>
> If $\mu = 0$, the maze is a tree. Larger $\mu$ means more loops, which makes local strategies such as “backtrack at dead ends” less informative and often increases difficulty.
>
> ### 7. Local visual ambiguity
>
> A maze is harder when local geometry gives poor clues about the correct direction.
>
> Possible measurable proxies include:
>
> - fraction of junctions where two or more outgoing branches look similar in length or orientation,
> - average angular symmetry at intersections,
> - similarity of corridor widths or visual patterns.
>
> These are harder to formalize from a drawing alone, but they matter for human solvers.
>
> ### 8. Distance to goal under uncertainty
>
> One can model a naive solver who chooses randomly at junctions. Then difficulty can be estimated by:
>
> - **Expected time to reach the exit,**
> - **Expected number of wrong turns,**
> - **Expected backtracking distance.**
>
> These can be computed from the maze graph under a specified navigation model. This is one of the most meaningful quantitative summaries because it directly captures solver effort.
>
> ### 9. Information-theoretic view
>
> At each junction, a solver must choose among alternatives. This suggests measuring uncertainty.
>
> If at a junction the solver perceives $k$ plausible choices, the local uncertainty is roughly
> $$
> \log_2 k
> $$
> bits.
>
> Summing this over important decision points gives a rough estimate of the information required to solve the maze without mistakes.
>
> ### 10. Spatial complexity
>
> Additional geometric features may affect perceived difficulty:
>
> - **Twistiness:** average turning angle along corridors,
> - **Line-of-sight limitation:** how far ahead one can see,
> - **Density/compactness:** how tightly packed the maze is,
> - **Crossing proximity:** how often corridors pass near each other without connecting.
>
> These can make a maze more confusing even if the graph structure is unchanged.
>
> ## Practical set of metrics
>
> For an interview or quick qualitative judgment, the most useful quantitative characteristics are:
>
> 1. **Number of junctions,**
> 2. **Average branching factor,**
> 3. **Number and average depth of dead ends,**
> 4. **Solution path length,**
> 5. **Number of wrong branches off the solution path,**
> 6. **Presence and number of loops,**
> 7. **Expected wrong-turn cost or expected solving time for a simple solver.**
>
> ## A concise difficulty heuristic
>
> A reasonable qualitative rule is:
>
> - A maze is **easy** if it has few junctions, shallow dead ends, short solution path, and no loops.
> - It is **moderate** if it has many choices but errors are cheap to recover from.
> - It is **hard** if it has many branching points, deep false paths, substantial loops, and a long, visually ambiguous solution path.
>
> Thus, the most important quantitative characteristics are not just the maze’s size, but the combination of **decision complexity**, **error cost**, and **structural ambiguity**.
