> [!example] Question 23 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

A deck of cards is shuffled and placed face down on a table. The cards are turned over one by one. If a black card is turned up, you win \$1.00; if a red card is turned up, you lose \$1.00. What is your strategy, and what are the expected winnings of your strategy?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=24)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> At any stage of the game, suppose there are
>
> - $b$ black cards remaining,
> - $r$ red cards remaining.
>
> Let $V(b,r)$ denote the maximum expected future gain from this position.
>
> The next card is black with probability $\dfrac{b}{b+r}$ and red with probability $\dfrac{r}{b+r}$. Since after seeing the next card you may decide whether to continue, the optimality equation is
>
> $$
> V(b,r)=\max\left\{0,\; \frac{b}{b+r}\bigl(1+V(b-1,r)\bigr)+\frac{r}{b+r}\bigl(-1+V(b,r-1)\bigr)\right\}.
> $$
>
> Here, the $0$ corresponds to stopping immediately, and $V(0,0)=0$.
>
> We now determine the optimal strategy and value.
>
> ---
>
> ### Step 1: A natural but incomplete guess
>
> If there are at least as many red cards as black, one might guess there is no advantage to continuing, and that whenever black leads, the value is exactly the size of that lead:
>
> $$
> V(b,r)\ \overset{?}{=}\ \max\{b-r,\,0\}.
> $$
>
> We test this guess directly, starting from the simplest possible tied state.
>
> ---
>
> ### Step 2: The guess fails already at $b=r=1$
>
> Take $b=1,r=1$. The guessed formula gives $V(1,1)=0$, meaning it should not matter whether you continue.
>
> Compute the continuation value directly. The next card is black or red, each with probability $\tfrac12$.
>
> - If it is black (probability $\tfrac12$), you gain $1$ and move to state $(0,1)$: no black cards remain, so continuing further can only lose money, and the best choice is to stop. Hence the value from here is $1+0=1$.
> - If it is red (probability $\tfrac12$), you lose $1$ and move to state $(1,0)$: only a black card remains, so continuing is guaranteed to gain $1$. Hence the value from here is $-1+1=0$.
>
> So the continuation value at $(1,1)$ is
>
> $$
> \frac12(1)+\frac12(0)=\frac12.
> $$
>
> Since $\frac12>0$, continuing is strictly better than stopping, so
>
> $$
> V(1,1)=\frac12\neq 0.
> $$
>
> This refutes the guessed formula: even at a tied state, continuing has strictly positive value.
>
> ---
>
> ### Step 3: Why the naive martingale argument does not settle the question
>
> Conditional on $b$ black and $r$ red cards remaining, the expected gain from the *next single card* is
>
> $$
> \frac{b}{b+r}(1)+\frac{r}{b+r}(-1)=\frac{b-r}{b+r},
> $$
>
> which is exactly $0$ when $b=r$. It is tempting to conclude from this that there is no benefit to continuing once the deck is tied, and hence that the optimal strategy is to stop as soon as $b\le r$.
>
> That conclusion does not follow. The one-step calculation only shows that being *forced* to reveal one more card, without the option to stop afterward, is a fair bet when $b=r$. It says nothing about the value of the *option* to keep watching and quit later, after the remaining cards have had a chance to move in your favor. That option always has non-negative value, and Step 2 shows it is strictly positive already at $(1,1)$. In fact, solving the recursion shows the region where continuing is optimal extends even a little beyond the tie line: for instance, with $b=2,r=3$ or $b=3,r=4$ (red strictly ahead), the continuation value is still positive, because enough cards remain for the situation to plausibly turn around. This is the same phenomenon that gives early exercise of an American option its time value: a locally unfavorable or neutral expected step does not make the right to stop later worthless.
>
> Consequently $V(b,r)$ cannot be guessed from the local drift; it must be computed by backward induction from the recursion above, starting at $b+r=0$ and working up to $b+r=52$.
>
> ---
>
> ### Step 4: Solving the recursion
>
> Since $0\le b,r\le 26$, there are only $27\times27$ states. Two boundary facts anchor the induction:
>
> - $V(b,0)=b$ for all $b$: with no red cards left, every remaining card is black, so you should always continue and collect all of them.
> - $V(0,r)=0$ for all $r$: with no black cards left, every remaining card is red, so you should stop immediately.
>
> Filling in the table by increasing $b+r$, using the optimality equation at each step, gives the value for the full deck:
>
> $$
> V(26,26)\approx 2.6245.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Final answer
>
> The optimal strategy is *not* simply "stop as soon as red is at least as numerous as black." Instead, at every state $(b,r)$ you should continue turning cards whenever the recursively computed continuation value
>
> $$
> \frac{b}{b+r}\bigl(1+V(b-1,r)\bigr)+\frac{r}{b+r}\bigl(-1+V(b,r-1)\bigr)
> $$
>
> is positive, and stop otherwise; this region extends somewhat beyond the tie line $b=r$, since the option to quit later after favorable cards have appeared has real value. For the full $52$-card deck, starting at $b=r=26$, this continuation value is positive, so you should not stop at the outset.
>
> Solving the recursion by backward induction over all states gives the value of the game:
>
> $$
> \boxed{V(26,26)\approx 2.6245}
> $$
>
> so, under the standard win-one-dollar/lose-one-dollar payoff, the optimal strategy achieves an expected profit of about $2.6245$ dollars, rather than the $0$ obtained by stopping immediately.
