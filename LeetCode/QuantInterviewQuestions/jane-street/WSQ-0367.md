> [!example] Question 367 · Jane Street
> ## Trader Interview

You and I play a game: we both pick a number before rolling two dice. The one whose chosen number is closer to the sum of the dice wins. The sum is not uniformly distributed—there is a 40% chance of getting a sum of 12, and the remaining probability is distributed uniformly among all other possible sums. What is your optimal strategy, and should you go first or second?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=368)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let the possible sums be $2,3,\dots,12$.
>
> The game’s distribution is:
>
> - $P(12)=0.4$,
> - the remaining probability $0.6$ is split uniformly among $2,3,\dots,11$.
>
> Thus for each $s\in\{2,3,\dots,11\}$,
> $$
> P(s)=\frac{0.6}{10}=0.06.
> $$
>
> We assume each player chooses a distinct integer sum, and the winner is the one whose chosen number is closer to the rolled sum. If the distances are equal, the result is a tie.
>
> ## 1. Compare any two choices
>
> Suppose one player chooses $a$ and the other chooses $b$, with $a<b$.
>
> A rolled sum $s$ is won by $a$ when
> $$
> |s-a|<|s-b|,
> $$
> and won by $b$ when
> $$
> |s-b|<|s-a|.
> $$
>
> The boundary is the midpoint $\frac{a+b}{2}$:
>
> - if $s<\frac{a+b}{2}$, then $a$ wins;
> - if $s>\frac{a+b}{2}$, then $b$ wins;
> - if $s=\frac{a+b}{2}$ and this is an integer, the result is a tie.
>
> So the second player, after seeing the first player’s choice, can choose a number that captures more total probability mass on one side of the midpoint.
>
> Because $12$ has very large probability $0.4$, control of the upper end is extremely valuable.
>
> ## 2. If the first player does **not** choose $12$
>
> Suppose the first player chooses some $a<12$.
>
> Then the second player can choose $12$.
>
> Now compare $a$ versus $12$. Since $12$ is the largest possible sum, every sufficiently large sum favors $12$, and in particular the outcome $12$ itself—worth probability $0.4$—is won outright by the player choosing $12$.
>
> Let us check whether this is always favorable.
>
> For a fixed $a<12$, the sums closer to $12$ than to $a$ are those with
> $$
> s>\frac{a+12}{2}.
> $$
> This set always includes $12$, and often also some of $9,10,11$.
>
> Even in the worst case for choosing $12$, namely when $a=11$, we have:
>
> - choice $11$ wins on sums $2,3,\dots,10$,
> - choice $12$ wins on sum $12$,
> - sum $11$ is closer to $11$ than to $12$, so it goes to $11$.
>
> Thus:
>
> - player choosing $11$ gets probability
> $$
> 10\cdot 0.06 = 0.60,
> $$
> - player choosing $12$ gets probability
> $$
> 0.40.
> $$
>
> So against $11$, choosing $12$ loses. Hence it is not true that the reply $12$ always wins.
>
> Therefore we must solve the game more carefully.
>
> ## 3. Best response to each possible first move
>
> For each first choice, compute the second player’s best reply.
>
> Because probabilities for $2$ through $11$ are all equal, what matters is how many of these sums lie on each side, together with whether $12$ is captured.
>
> ### First player chooses $12$
>
> Then the second player should choose $11$, since this captures all sums $2$ through $11$.
>
> - $11$ wins for sums $2,\dots,11$, total probability
> $$
> 10\cdot 0.06=0.60.
> $$
> - $12$ wins only at sum $12$, probability
> $$
> 0.40.
> $$
>
> So the second player wins with probability $0.60$.
>
> ### First player chooses $11$
>
> Possible strong replies are $10$ or $12$.
>
> - Reply $12$ wins only on $12$, so gets $0.40$.
> - Reply $10$ wins on sums $2,\dots,10$, total
> $$
> 9\cdot 0.06=0.54,
> $$
> while $11$ wins on $11$ and $12$, total
> $$
> 0.06+0.40=0.46.
> $$
>
> So the best reply is $10$, giving the second player probability $0.54$.
>
> ### First player chooses $10$
>
> Reply $11$ is excellent.
>
> Against $10$ versus $11$:
>
> - $10$ wins on sums $2,\dots,10$, total
> $$
> 9\cdot 0.06=0.54,
> $$
> - $11$ wins on sums $11,12$, total
> $$
> 0.06+0.40=0.46.
> $$
>
> So reply $11$ loses.
>
> Try reply $12$:
>
> - $10$ wins on sums $2,\dots,10$, total $0.54$,
> - $12$ wins on $11,12$, total $0.46$.
>
> Also loses.
>
> Try reply $9$:
>
> - midpoint is $9.5$, so $9$ wins on $2,\dots,9$:
> $$
> 8\cdot 0.06=0.48,
> $$
> - $10$ wins on $10,11,12$:
> $$
> 0.06+0.06+0.40=0.52.
> $$
>
> Still loses.
>
> Thus choosing $10$ appears stronger than $11$ or $12$.
>
> ### First player chooses $9$
>
> Reply $10$:
>
> - $9$ wins on $2,\dots,9$:
> $$
> 8\cdot 0.06=0.48,
> $$
> - $10$ wins on $10,11,12$:
> $$
> 0.06+0.06+0.40=0.52.
> $$
>
> So the second player wins with reply $10$.
>
> ### First player chooses $8$
>
> Reply $10$:
>
> Midpoint is $9$.
>
> - $8$ wins on $2,\dots,8$:
> $$
> 7\cdot 0.06=0.42,
> $$
> - $10$ wins on $10,11,12$:
> $$
> 0.06+0.06+0.40=0.52,
> $$
> - sum $9$ is a tie, probability $0.06$.
>
> Thus the second player gets $0.52$.
>
> ### First player chooses $7$
>
> Reply $10$:
>
> Midpoint is $8.5$.
>
> - $7$ wins on $2,\dots,8$:
> $$
> 7\cdot 0.06=0.42,
> $$
> - $10$ wins on $9,10,11,12$:
> $$
> 0.06+0.06+0.06+0.40=0.58.
> $$
>
> Second player wins.
>
> ### Smaller first choices
>
> If the first player chooses anything $\le 9$, choosing $10$ or $11$ gives the second player access to the heavy mass at $12$ plus enough upper sums to exceed $1/2$.
>
> In particular, we have already seen:
>
> - first choice $9$ is beaten by $10$,
> - first choice $8$ is beaten by $10$,
> - and all lower choices are even worse.
>
> ## 4. Find the optimal first move
>
> From the cases above:
>
> - $12$ is beaten by $11$,
> - $11$ is beaten by $10$,
> - $9$ and below are beaten by $10$,
> - $8$ is beaten by $10$,
> - $7$ and below are beaten by $10$.
>
> The only candidate that survives is $10$.
>
> Against first choice $10$, every possible second choice does no better than $0.46$ or $0.48$ or $0.40$, except the first player keeps at least $0.52$ against nearby lower choices and $0.54$ against $11$ or $12$.
>
> Indeed:
>
> - versus $9$, first player with $10$ gets $0.52$;
> - versus $11$, first player with $10$ gets $0.54$;
> - versus $12$, first player with $10$ gets $0.54$.
>
> So $10$ is the optimal first choice.
>
> ## 5. First or second?
>
> Since the first player can choose $10$ and guarantee a win probability of at least
> $$
> 0.52,
> $$
> the first player has the advantage.
>
> The second player cannot do better than this against optimal play.
>
> ## Final answer
>
> The optimal strategy is:
>
> - **go first**,
> - **choose $10$**.
>
> With this choice, no response by the second player can beat you; in fact, the best the second player can do still leaves you with winning probability at least $0.52$.
>
> So the advantage is to **move first and pick $10$**.
