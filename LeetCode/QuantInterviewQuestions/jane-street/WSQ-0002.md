> [!example] Question 2 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

In a Rock-Paper-Scissors game where your opponent is not allowed to play rock, you win \$1 for each win, lose \$1 for each loss, and draw \$0 for a tie. What should you play to maximize your expected profit?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=3)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let the opponent’s strategy be restricted to the two actions $\{\text{Paper}, \text{Scissors}\}$, while your available actions are $\{\text{Rock}, \text{Paper}, \text{Scissors}\}$.
>
> Let
> $$
> p=\Pr(\text{opponent plays Paper}), \qquad 1-p=\Pr(\text{opponent plays Scissors}).
> $$
>
> Compute the expected profit for each of your pure strategies.
>
> ### 1. Play Rock
> - Against Paper: you lose $\text{\textdollar}1$
> - Against Scissors: you win $\text{\textdollar}1$
>
> Thus
> $$
> E_R = (-1)p + (1)(1-p)=1-2p.
> $$
>
> ### 2. Play Paper
> - Against Paper: draw, profit $0$
> - Against Scissors: lose $\text{\textdollar}1$
>
> Thus
> $$
> E_P = 0\cdot p + (-1)(1-p)=p-1.
> $$
>
> ### 3. Play Scissors
> - Against Paper: win $\text{\textdollar}1$
> - Against Scissors: draw, profit $0$
>
> Thus
> $$
> E_S = 1\cdot p + 0\cdot(1-p)=p.
> $$
>
> Now compare these three values:
>
> - $E_S - E_P = p-(p-1)=1>0$, so Scissors is always better than Paper.
> - $E_R - E_P = (1-2p)-(p-1)=2-3p$, which may be positive or negative.
> - $E_S - E_R = p-(1-2p)=3p-1$.
>
> Hence:
> - If $p>\tfrac13$, then $E_S>E_R$, so play Scissors.
> - If $p<\tfrac13$, then $E_R>E_S$, so play Rock.
> - If $p=\tfrac13$, then $E_R=E_S=\tfrac13$, so either Rock or Scissors is optimal.
>
> Therefore, the optimal play is:
> $$
> \boxed{
> \begin{cases}
> \text{Rock}, & \text{if } \Pr(\text{Paper})<\tfrac13,\\[4pt]
> \text{Scissors}, & \text{if } \Pr(\text{Paper})>\tfrac13,\\[4pt]
> \text{either Rock or Scissors}, & \text{if } \Pr(\text{Paper})=\tfrac13.
> \end{cases}
> }
> $$
>
> In particular, if no further information is given about how often the opponent chooses Paper versus Scissors, then there is no single universally optimal move; it depends on that probability.
