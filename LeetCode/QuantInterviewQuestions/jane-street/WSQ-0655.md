> [!example] Question 655 · Jane Street
> ## Quant Trader Intern Interview

You and an opponent are each given a number uniformly at random between 0 and 1. The player with the higher number wins 1 point from the other. After seeing your own number (but not your opponent's), you can choose to offer 'double' odds or just continue. If you offer double, your opponent, having seen their own number, can either reject the offer (losing 1) or accept it. If they accept, the winner takes 2 points from the loser according to who has the higher number. What is the optimal strategy for playing this game?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=656)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> Let your number be $x \in [0,1]$ and your opponent's number be $y \in [0,1]$, independent and uniformly distributed. If no double is offered, the payoff to you is $+1$ if $x>y$ and $-1$ if $x<y$ (ties have probability $0$).
>
> This is a signaling game: you choose whether to offer double based on $x$, and your opponent, upon seeing an offer, decides whether to accept based on $y$ and on what the offer reveals about the distribution of $x$.
>
> ## 1. Guessing the shape of the equilibrium
>
> If you always doubled with high $x$ only, your opponent would learn that an offer means you likely have a strong hand, and would simply fold except with their own very strong hands — making doubling with a weak $x$ pointless (you would always be called and lose big) but doubling with a strong $x$ still often just gets folded on, capping your gain.
>
> The standard resolution, familiar from simple poker-style models, is that you should double for two separate reasons:
>
> - **as a bluff**, with very *low* $x$ — you have little to lose (you were likely to lose the point anyway), and doubling gives your opponent a chance to fold and hand you the point instead;
> - **for value**, with very *high* $x$ — you are likely to win anyway, and doubling extracts more when you do.
>
> With *middling* $x$ you are best off just playing for the single point.
>
> So we look for an equilibrium of the form:
>
> - you offer double iff $x\le c_1$ or $x\ge c_2$, for some thresholds $0<c_1<c_2<1$;
> - your opponent, on seeing a double offered, accepts iff $y\ge d$, for some threshold $d$.
>
> ## 2. The opponent's threshold
>
> Suppose a double has been offered. Conditional on this, your $x$ is uniform on $[0,c_1]\cup[c_2,1]$, a set of total prior mass $w=c_1+(1-c_2)$.
>
> Your opponent's expected payoff from **accepting**, given their value $y$, is $2\Pr(y>x\mid\text{offer})-2\Pr(y<x\mid\text{offer})$. Using the mixture above, this payoff, as a function of $y$, increases for $y\in[0,c_1]$, stays constant for $y\in[c_1,c_2]$ (since $x$ never falls in that range, moving $y$ within it changes nothing), and increases again for $y\in[c_2,1]$. In particular, on the flat middle piece the payoff equals
> $$
> \frac{2}{w}\bigl(c_1+c_2-1\bigr).
> $$
>
> Rejecting always guarantees $-1$. Since the accept-payoff is nondecreasing in $y$, the opponent's best response is exactly a threshold rule, accept iff $y\ge d$ — and since we are guessing $d$ falls inside the flat piece $[c_1,c_2]$, the indifference condition becomes
> $$
> \frac{2}{w}\bigl(c_1+c_2-1\bigr)=-1.
> $$
>
> ## 3. Your threshold, given the opponent plays $d$
>
> Now fix the opponent's rule (accept iff $y\ge d$) and find your best response.
>
> If you do **not** offer double, your payoff is
> $$
> V_{\text{no}}(x)=\Pr(x>y)-\Pr(x<y)=2x-1.
> $$
>
> If you **do** offer double: with probability $d$ your opponent's $y<d$ and they fold, paying you the point; with probability $1-d$ they call, at which point you win $2$ if $x>y$ and lose $2$ if $x<y$.
>
> If $x\le d$: whenever the opponent calls ($y\ge d\ge x$), you always have $x<y$, so you lose every time you are called. Hence
> $$
> V_{\text{double}}(x)=d\cdot1+(1-d)\cdot(-2)=3d-2,
> $$
> a constant, independent of $x$.
>
> If $x\ge d$: among calls ($y\ge d$), you win against $y\in[d,x]$, of length $x-d$, and lose against $y\in[x,1]$, of length $1-x$. Hence
> $$
> V_{\text{double}}(x)=d\cdot1+2(x-d)-2(1-x)=4x-d-2.
> $$
>
> Comparing to $V_{\text{no}}(x)=2x-1$:
>
> - For $x\le d$, $V_{\text{double}}$ is the constant $3d-2$ while $V_{\text{no}}(x)=2x-1$ is increasing, so doubling is better exactly below the crossing point
> $$
> c_1=\frac{3d-1}{2}.
> $$
> - For $x\ge d$, $V_{\text{double}}(x)=4x-d-2$ crosses $V_{\text{no}}(x)=2x-1$ where $2x=d+1$, so doubling is better exactly above
> $$
> c_2=\frac{d+1}{2}.
> $$
>
> This confirms the guessed shape: double below $c_1$ as a bluff, do not double between $c_1$ and $c_2$, double above $c_2$ for value.
>
> ## 4. Solving the system
>
> We now have $c_1=\dfrac{3d-1}2$ and $c_2=\dfrac{d+1}2$ in terms of $d$, together with the indifference condition from Part 2, which rearranges to
> $$
> 3c_1+c_2=1.
> $$
>
> Substituting the expressions for $c_1,c_2$,
> $$
> 3\cdot\frac{3d-1}{2}+\frac{d+1}{2}=1,
> $$
> that is,
> $$
> 9d-3+d+1=2,
> $$
> so
> $$
> 10d=4,\qquad d=\frac25.
> $$
>
> Then
> $$
> c_1=\frac{3\cdot\frac25-1}{2}=\frac{\frac15}{2}=\frac1{10},\qquad c_2=\frac{\frac25+1}{2}=\frac{\frac75}{2}=\frac7{10}.
> $$
>
> Equivalently, if $F(d)$ denotes the posterior probability, given that a double is offered, that $x$ is below $d$, then $F(d)=c_1/w$ with $w=c_1+1-c_2=\frac1{10}+\frac3{10}=\frac25$, so
> $$
> F(d)=\frac{1/10}{2/5}=\frac14,
> $$
> which is exactly the condition making the opponent indifferent at $y=d$: accepting is then worth $2(2F(d)-1)=2\left(\tfrac12-1\right)=-1$, matching the payoff from folding.
>
> ## 5. Verification
>
> Check your indifference at both thresholds. At $x=c_1=0.1\le d=0.4$:
> $$
> V_{\text{no}}(0.1)=2(0.1)-1=-0.8,\qquad V_{\text{double}}(0.1)=3(0.4)-2=-0.8.
> $$
> At $x=c_2=0.7\ge d=0.4$:
> $$
> V_{\text{no}}(0.7)=2(0.7)-1=0.4,\qquad V_{\text{double}}(0.7)=4(0.7)-0.4-2=0.4.
> $$
> Both match exactly, confirming the thresholds. One can similarly check that $x=0.9$ strictly prefers doubling ($V_{\text{double}}=1.2>V_{\text{no}}=0.8$), that $x=0.05$ strictly prefers doubling as a bluff ($V_{\text{double}}=-0.8>V_{\text{no}}=-0.9$), and that a middling value such as $x=0.5$ strictly prefers not doubling ($V_{\text{no}}=0>V_{\text{double}}=-0.4$) — exactly as the equilibrium requires.
>
> ## Final answer
>
> The optimal strategy is:
>
> - **Offer double** exactly when your number satisfies $x\le0.1$ (a bluff with a weak hand) or $x\ge0.7$ (betting for value with a strong hand); otherwise just play for the single point.
> - **If offered double**, accept exactly when your number satisfies $y\ge0.4$, and reject otherwise.
>
> $$
> \boxed{c_1=0.1,\quad c_2=0.7,\quad d=0.4}
> $$
