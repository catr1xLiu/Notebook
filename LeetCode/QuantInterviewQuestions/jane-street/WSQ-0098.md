> [!example] Question 98 · Jane Street
> ## Trading Interview

Imagine you would receive \$100 if you made more than n percent of your free throws, and would have to pay \$100 if you made less than n percent of the throws. Would you prefer to have 10 throws or 100 throws?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=99)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let $p$ denote your true free-throw percentage, and let $n$ be the threshold percentage.
>
> You win \(\text{\textdollar}100\) if your observed percentage is greater than $n$, and lose \(\text{\textdollar}100\) if it is less than $n$. Thus, for a fixed number of throws, the preferable choice is the one that gives the larger probability of finishing above $n$.
>
> ## Model
>
> If you take $m$ throws, then the number of made shots $X$ is
>
> $$
> X \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(m,p).
> $$
>
> Your observed percentage is $X/m$. You win when
>
> $$
> \frac{X}{m} > \frac{n}{100}.
> $$
>
> So the probability of winning is
>
> $$
> P\!\left(\frac{X}{m} > \frac{n}{100}\right).
> $$
>
> The question is whether this probability is larger for $m=10$ or for $m=100$.
>
> ## Key idea
>
> The sample proportion $X/m$ is centered at $p$, and its variability is
>
> $$
> \operatorname{Var}\!\left(\frac{X}{m}\right)=\frac{p(1-p)}{m}.
> $$
>
> So:
>
> - with $100$ throws, your observed percentage is much less variable;
> - with $10$ throws, it is more variable.
>
> Therefore:
>
> - if your true percentage $p$ is **above** the threshold $n/100$, then less variability helps you, so $100$ throws is better;
> - if your true percentage $p$ is **below** the threshold $n/100$, then more variability helps you occasionally exceed the threshold, so $10$ throws is better;
> - if $p=n/100$, both choices are essentially fair, with probability about $1/2$ of being above the threshold.
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> Let $n/100$ be the target percentage and $p$ your actual free-throw probability.
>
> - If $p>\dfrac{n}{100}$, choose **100 throws**.
> - If $p<\dfrac{n}{100}$, choose **10 throws**.
> - If $p=\dfrac{n}{100}$, there is **no advantage** either way.
>
> So the answer depends on whether you think your true free-throw percentage is above or below $n\%$.
