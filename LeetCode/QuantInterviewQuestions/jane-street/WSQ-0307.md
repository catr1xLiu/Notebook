> [!example] Question 307 · Jane Street
> ## Analyst Interview

An unfair die with 12 faces has the number 11 with a probability of 40%, while the other faces are equally likely. You and another player are playing a game where whoever is closer to the correct answer wins. What is your optimal strategy, and should you choose to go first or second?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=308)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> Interpret the game as follows: a $12$-sided die is rolled once, and the two players each choose a number from $\{1,2,\dots,12\}$. The player whose chosen number is **closer** to the outcome wins.
>
> The die is unfair:
> - $P(11)=0.4$,
> - the other $11$ faces share the remaining probability $0.6$ equally, so for each $k\neq 11$,
>   $$
>   P(k)=\frac{0.6}{11}.
>   $$
>
> We seek the optimal strategy and whether it is better to play first or second.
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. Which guesses are strongest?
>
> For any chosen number, its chance to win depends on which outcomes are closer to it than to the opponent’s guess. Since $11$ has by far the largest probability, any strong strategy must focus on being near $11$.
>
> The key observation is that if one player chooses $11$, then any opponent’s best response should be a neighboring number, namely $10$ or $12$, because those are the closest alternatives to $11$.
>
> So the strategic core is to compare:
> - choosing $11$,
> - choosing $10$ or $12$ against $11$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. If one player chooses $11$, what happens?
>
> Suppose Player A chooses $11$.
>
> ### Opponent chooses $12$
> Compare distances to the rolled value.
>
> - If the die shows $12$, then $12$ is exact, so $12$ wins.
> - If it shows $11$, then $11$ is exact, so $11$ wins.
> - For values $1$ through $10$, the midpoint between $11$ and $12$ is $11.5$, so all these outcomes are closer to $11$.
>
> Thus:
> - $12$ wins only on outcome $12$,
> - $11$ wins on outcomes $1,2,\dots,11$.
>
> Therefore
> $$
> P(\text{$12$ beats $11$})=P(12)=\frac{0.6}{11},
> $$
> and
> $$
> P(\text{$11$ beats $12$})=1-\frac{0.6}{11}.
> $$
>
> Since
> $$
> \frac{0.6}{11}\approx 0.0545,
> $$
> choosing $12$ against $11$ is very poor.
>
> ### Opponent chooses $10$
> Now the midpoint between $10$ and $11$ is $10.5$.
>
> - Outcomes $1$ through $10$ are closer to $10$.
> - Outcomes $11$ and $12$ are closer to $11$.
>
> Hence
> $$
> P(\text{$10$ beats $11$})=10\cdot \frac{0.6}{11}=\frac{6}{11}\approx 0.5455,
> $$
> while
> $$
> P(\text{$11$ beats $10$})=P(11)+P(12)=0.4+\frac{0.6}{11}\approx 0.4545.
> $$
>
> So against $11$, the best reply is $10$, not $12$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Should you want to move first?
>
> This is now a simple minimax question.
>
> ### If you move first and choose $11$
> The opponent can respond with $10$, which gives them winning probability
> $$
> \frac{6}{11}\approx 54.55\%.
> $$
> So choosing first and taking $11$ is not safe.
>
> ### If you move first and choose something else
> Then the opponent can usually choose a number that dominates your choice even more effectively; in particular, since $11$ is the heavy-probability face, failing to control the region near $11$ is bad. Thus moving first is disadvantageous.
>
> ### If you move second
> Then you can respond optimally to the first player’s choice.
>
> In particular:
> - if the first player picks $11$, you answer $10$, and your win chance is
>   $$
>   \frac{6}{11}\approx 54.55\%;
>   $$
> - if the first player picks anything other than $11$, you can choose a nearby number on the side that captures the high-probability outcome $11$, giving at least as good a position.
>
> Thus the second player has the advantage.
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. Optimal strategy
>
> The most important case is when the opponent chooses $11$. Then your best response is to choose $10$.
>
> Why $10$ rather than $12$? Because:
> - $12$ only wins when the outcome is exactly $12$,
> - $10$ wins on all outcomes $1$ through $10$, whose total probability is
>   $$
>   10\cdot \frac{0.6}{11}=\frac{6}{11},
>   $$
> which is greater than the probability mass captured by $11$ on outcomes $11$ and $12$.
>
> So the equilibrium idea is:
>
> - **Prefer to play second.**
> - **If the first player chooses $11$, choose $10$.**
>
> ---
>
> ## Final answer
>
> The advantageous position is to **play second**.
>
> The key best response is:
>
> - if the first player chooses $11$, you should choose $10$.
>
> This gives you winning probability
> $$
> P(1\text{ through }10)=10\cdot \frac{0.6}{11}=\frac{6}{11}\approx 54.55\%,
> $$
> while the player choosing $11$ wins only when the die shows $11$ or $12$, with probability
> $$
> 0.4+\frac{0.6}{11}\approx 45.45\%.
> $$
>
> Therefore the optimal strategy is to **go second**, and in the critical case respond to $11$ with **$10$**.
