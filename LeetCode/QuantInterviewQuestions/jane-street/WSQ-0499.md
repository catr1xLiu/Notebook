> [!example] Question 499 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Trader Interview

You have a standard deck of 52 cards. I draw a card and tell you I drew a heart. Now you draw a card from the remaining cards. What is the probability that you will also draw a heart?
Again, you have a standard deck of 52 cards. I draw 13 cards and tell you I drew exactly 5 hearts. Now you draw 13 cards from the cards that are remaining. What is the expected number of hearts you will draw?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=500)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## 1. One card is drawn and known to be a heart
>
> After the first draw, there are $51$ cards remaining. Since the first card was a heart, the remaining deck contains $12$ hearts.
>
> Therefore, the probability that the next card is also a heart is
>
> $$
> \frac{12}{51}=\frac{4}{17}.
> $$
>
> ## 2. Thirteen cards are drawn and known to contain exactly $5$ hearts
>
> A standard deck has $13$ hearts total. If the first $13$ cards contain exactly $5$ hearts, then among the remaining $39$ cards there are
>
> $$
> 13-5=8
> $$
>
> hearts.
>
> Now $13$ cards are drawn from these remaining $39$ cards. Let $X$ be the number of hearts in this $13$-card hand. Then $X$ has a hypergeometric distribution with population size $39$, number of success states $8$, and sample size $13$.
>
> The expected value of a hypergeometric random variable is
>
> $$
> E[X]=n\cdot \frac{K}{N},
> $$
>
> where $N=39$, $K=8$, and $n=13$. Hence
>
> $$
> E[X]=13\cdot \frac{8}{39}=\frac{8}{3}.
> $$
>
> So the expected number of hearts is
>
> $$
> \frac{8}{3}.
> $$
>
> ## Final answers
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{4}{17}}
> \qquad\text{and}\qquad
> \boxed{\frac{8}{3}}.
> $$
