> [!example] Question 25 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Researcher Interview

There are 100 coins, each flipped and the results are hidden. You can ask one yes/no question, after which you must guess the outcome of each coin flip. For each correct guess, you earn 1 dollar; for each incorrect guess, you lose 1 dollar. What is the optimal strategy for maximizing your expected return, and what is the maximum expected value?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=26)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> Let the $100$ hidden coin results be a binary string of length $100$, where each position is either $H$ or $T$.
>
> You may ask **one yes/no question**, receive its answer, and then you must guess all $100$ outcomes. If your guess matches a coin, you gain $1$, and if it does not, you lose $1$.
>
> Thus, if your guess differs from the true outcome in $d$ positions, your total payoff is
>
> $$
> (100-d) - d = 100 - 2d.
> $$
>
> So maximizing expected payoff is equivalent to minimizing the expected Hamming distance between your guess and the true outcome.
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. What can one yes/no question do?
>
> A yes/no question has only two possible answers, so it partitions the set of all possible outcomes into two classes.
>
> There are
>
> $$
> 2^{100}
> $$
>
> possible coin-flip outcomes. After hearing the answer, you know only which of the two classes the true outcome lies in.
>
> Then you must choose one $100$-bit string as your final guess for that class.
>
> So the problem becomes:
>
> - partition the set $\{H,T\}^{100}$ into two parts,
> - for each part, choose one representative string to guess,
> - maximize expected payoff.
>
> Equivalently, after the answer you will guess one of two predetermined strings. Call them $x$ and $y$.
>
> For any actual outcome $z$, you can arrange your question so that the answer tells you whether to guess $x$ or to guess $y$, choosing whichever gives the better payoff for that $z$. Hence the best possible payoff for outcome $z$ is determined by the smaller of the two Hamming distances:
>
> $$
> 100 - 2\min\{d(z,x),\, d(z,y)\}.
> $$
>
> Therefore, the expected payoff is
>
> $$
> 100 - 2\,\mathbb{E}\big[\min\{d(Z,x),d(Z,y)\}\big],
> $$
>
> where $Z$ is a uniformly random outcome.
>
> So we must choose $x$ and $y$ to minimize the average distance from a random $Z$ to the nearer of the two strings.
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Best choice of the two possible guesses
>
> The optimal choice is to take $x$ and $y$ to be complements of each other. For example,
>
> $$
> x = HHH\cdots H, \qquad y = TTT\cdots T.
> $$
>
> Why is this optimal? If the Hamming distance between $x$ and $y$ is $k$, then on the $100-k$ coordinates where they agree, both guesses make the same prediction, so having two different guesses gives no advantage there. To maximize coverage of the space, the two guesses should differ in as many coordinates as possible, namely all $100$ coordinates.
>
> Thus we may take $y$ to be the bitwise complement of $x$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Expected payoff with complementary guesses
>
> Let $x = HHH\cdots H$ and $y = TTT\cdots T$.
>
> If the actual outcome has exactly $m$ heads, then its distance to $x$ is
>
> $$
> d(z,x)=100-m,
> $$
>
> and its distance to $y$ is
>
> $$
> d(z,y)=m.
> $$
>
> So after hearing the answer, you can choose the better of the two, giving distance
>
> $$
> \min(m,100-m).
> $$
>
> Hence the payoff is
>
> $$
> 100 - 2\min(m,100-m) = |2m-100|.
> $$
>
> Therefore the expected payoff is
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[\,|2M-100|\,],
> $$
>
> where $M \sim \operatorname{Bin}(100,\tfrac12)$.
>
> Equivalently,
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[\,|2M-100|\,]
> =
> 2^{-100}\sum_{m=0}^{100} \binom{100}{m}\,|2m-100|.
> $$
>
> This is the exact maximum expected value.
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. A simpler closed form
>
> For even $n=2r$, there is a standard identity
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}\big[\,|2M-n|\,\big]
> =
> n\,\frac{\binom{n}{n/2}}{2^n},
> \qquad M\sim \operatorname{Bin}(n,\tfrac12).
> $$
>
> With $n=100$, this gives
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[\,|2M-100|\,]
> =
> 100\,\frac{\binom{100}{50}}{2^{100}}.
> $$
>
> Numerically,
>
> $$
> 100\,\frac{\binom{100}{50}}{2^{100}} \approx 7.95892374.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 5. How to realize this with one yes/no question
>
> Ask:
>
> “Were there at least $50$ heads?”
>
> - If the answer is **Yes**, guess the sequence with all entries equal to the majority side, say all heads.
> - If the answer is **No**, guess all tails.
>
> More generally, fix any string $x$ and its complement $\bar x$, and ask whether the hidden outcome is closer to $x$ than to $\bar x$; then guess accordingly.
>
> For the all-heads/all-tails choice, the rule is especially simple: after learning whether heads are at least as numerous as tails, guess the majority outcome on every coin.
>
> ---
>
> ## Final answer
>
> The best strategy is to use the yes/no question to decide between **two complementary full-sequence guesses**. A concrete optimal strategy is:
>
> - ask whether the number of heads is at least $50$;
> - if yes, guess all $100$ coins were heads;
> - if no, guess all $100$ coins were tails.
>
> This yields payoff
>
> $$
> |2M-100|,
> \qquad M\sim \operatorname{Bin}(100,\tfrac12),
> $$
>
> so the maximum expected return is
>
> $$
> \boxed{100\,\frac{\binom{100}{50}}{2^{100}} \approx 7.959}.
> $$
>
> Thus the maximum expected value is approximately
>
> $$
> \boxed{\$7.96}.
> $$
