> [!example] Question 441 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Trader Interview

Two players each roll a die. Each wins if their number is higher than the other's, but they are not obligated to bet. If the first player declines the bet, that round ends. The second player knows whether the first player made a bet or not. Which player has a higher expected payout?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=442)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let the first player be $A$ and the second player be $B$. Each independently rolls a fair die, obtaining values $a,b \in \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$.
>
> If they bet, then:
>
> - $A$ wins if $a>b$,
> - $B$ wins if $b>a$,
> - a tie gives neither player a win.
>
> Player $A$ may choose whether to initiate the bet after seeing $a$. If $A$ declines, the round ends. Player $B$ knows whether $A$ chose to bet, so $B$ can condition on that information when deciding whether to accept.
>
> We determine the equilibrium behavior and expected payoff.
>
> ## 1. What should player $A$ do?
>
> Suppose $A$ bets only with some set of die values. If $B$ observes that $A$ bet, then $B$ updates his belief about $A$'s number and decides whether accepting is favorable.
>
> Thus $A$ cannot simply bet with weak numbers, because $B$ will infer something from the fact that a bet was offered.
>
> The natural candidate is a threshold strategy: $A$ bets when $a \ge t$ and declines when $a<t$.
>
> We test such strategies.
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. If $A$ bets only on high rolls
>
> ### Case $t=6$
>
> Then $A$ bets only when $a=6$. If $B$ sees a bet, he knows $A$ has $6$, so accepting yields payoff
>
> $$
> \mathbb{P}(b>6)-\mathbb{P}(b<6)=0-\frac{5}{6}=-\frac{5}{6},
> $$
>
> so $B$ will decline. Hence no bet is ever accepted, and both players get expected payoff $0$.
>
> ### Case $t=5$
>
> Then a bet means $a \in \{5,6\}$, each equally likely conditional on a bet.
>
> If $B$ has:
>
> - $b=6$, he surely beats $5$ and ties $6$, so accepting is favorable.
> - $b=5$, he ties against $5$ and loses to $6$, so accepting is unfavorable.
> - smaller $b$ are even worse.
>
> Thus only $b=6$ accepts.
>
> Then when $A$ bets, $B$ accepts only with probability $\frac16$, and in that case:
>
> - if $a=5$, $A$ loses,
> - if $a=6$, tie.
>
> So $A$'s expected payoff from betting is negative. Hence $A$ should not use this strategy.
>
> The same issue occurs for any threshold below $6$: whenever $A$ bets with more than one value, $B$ accepts only with sufficiently high values, exploiting the information conveyed by the bet.
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Best response logic
>
> For any betting rule of $A$, when $B$ observes a bet he compares
>
> $$
> \mathbb{P}(b>a \mid \text{$A$ bet}) \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbb{P}(b<a \mid \text{$A$ bet}).
> $$
>
> He accepts only when this gives nonnegative expected value.
>
> Since $A$ can always guarantee payoff $0$ by declining, in equilibrium $A$ will never choose to bet in a way that gives negative expected payoff.
>
> But if $A$ ever bets with any value below $6$, then there exists some high value of $b$ for which $B$ can accept profitably. Because $B$ conditions on the fact that $A$ offered the bet, this selection effect works against $A$.
>
> The only way for $A$ to avoid being exploited is to bet only when $a=6$. Yet then $B$ declines, so again the expected payoff is $0$.
>
> Thus the equilibrium outcome is:
>
> - $A$ offers a bet only with $6$ (or equivalently never offers, since accepted bets never occur),
> - $B$ declines whenever a bet is offered.
>
> So no money changes hands.
>
> ## 4. Expected payouts
>
> Under equilibrium play,
>
> $$
> E[\text{payoff of }A]=0,
> \qquad
> E[\text{payoff of }B]=0.
> $$
>
> Therefore neither player has a higher payout.
>
> ## Final answer
>
> $$
> \boxed{\text{Neither player has an advantage; the equilibrium expected payout is }0\text{ for both.}}
> $$
