> [!example] Question 291 · Jane Street
> ## Quant Trader Interview

There are 30 people, consisting of 15 couples. Everyone shakes hands with other people, except nobody shakes hands with their own partner. You are told that 29 people each shook hands with a different number of people (for example, one person shook hands with 4 people, another with 5, etc.). How many people did the 30th person shake hands with?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=292)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let the possible numbers of handshakes be the integers from $0$ to $28$, since each person refuses to shake hands with their own spouse and so can shake hands with at most $28$ people.
>
> We are told that among $29$ people, all handshake counts are different. Hence those $29$ counts must be exactly
>
> $$
> 0,1,2,\dots,28.
> $$
>
> The key observation is that if one person shook hands with $0$ people, then their spouse must have shaken hands with everyone else, hence with $28$ people. So the people with counts $0$ and $28$ form a couple.
>
> Similarly, the person with $1$ handshake must be married to the person with $27$ handshakes. For if the person with $1$ handshake were married to anyone else, that spouse would be forced into a contradiction with the distinctness of the counts. More generally, the counts pair off as
>
> $$
> (0,28),\ (1,27),\ (2,26),\ \dots,\ (13,15).
> $$
>
> Each pair sums to $28$, and each such pair must be a married couple.
>
> These account for $14$ couples, i.e. $28$ people. The remaining couple consists of:
>
> - one of the given $29$ people, whose handshake number must be the only unused value, namely $14$;
> - the $30$th person.
>
> Since spouses cannot have counts that duplicate any of the already used values among the $29$ distinct people, and the only value left to pair with $14$ is also $14$, the $30$th person must also have shaken hands with $14$ people.
>
> Therefore, the $30$th person shook hands with
>
> $$
> 14
> $$
>
> people.
