> [!example] Question 558 · Jane Street
> ## Assistant Trader Interview

We are playing a game with an unbiased 30-sided die. Before rolling, both players pick distinct numbers between 1 and 30. After rolling the die, if the number rolled is closer to your number, I pay you the number on the die in dollars. If it is closer to my number, you pay me the number on the die in dollars. If it is equidistant, neither pays. What is the optimal strategy: should you go first or second, and how should you pick your number? What is your expected value?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=559)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> Let the two chosen numbers be $a$ and $b$, with $a \neq b$, and let the die result be $X \in \{1,2,\dots,30\}$, uniformly distributed.
>
> If $X$ is closer to your number, you receive $X$ dollars; if it is closer to the opponent's number, you pay $X$ dollars; if it is equidistant, the payoff is $0$.
>
> We determine the optimal play and the resulting expected value.
>
> ## 1. Payoff for fixed choices
>
> Assume first that $a<b$.
>
> The midpoint is
> $$
> m=\frac{a+b}{2}.
> $$
>
> - If $X<m$, then $X$ is closer to $a$.
> - If $X>m$, then $X$ is closer to $b$.
> - If $X=m$ and $m$ is an integer, the result is a tie.
>
> Thus, if you chose $a$ and the opponent chose $b$, your payoff is
>
> - $+X$ for rolls closer to $a$,
> - $-X$ for rolls closer to $b$,
> - $0$ for a midpoint tie.
>
> So your expected payoff is
> $$
> \frac{1}{30}\left(\sum_{\substack{1\le x\le 30\\ x<m}} x-\sum_{\substack{1\le x\le 30\\ x>m}} x\right).
> $$
>
> This depends only on the midpoint between the two chosen numbers, not on their exact locations.
>
> ## 2. Reformulating in terms of the midpoint
>
> Let
> $$
> S(n)=1+2+\cdots+n=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}.
> $$
>
> Suppose first that $a+b$ is odd, so there is no tie and $m$ is a half-integer. Write
> $$
> m=k+\frac12
> $$
> for some integer $k$. Then rolls $1,\dots,k$ favor the smaller number, and rolls $k+1,\dots,30$ favor the larger number.
>
> Hence the expected payoff to the player who chose the smaller number is
> $$
> \frac{1}{30}\left(S(k)-\bigl(S(30)-S(k)\bigr)\right)
> =
> \frac{1}{30}\left(2S(k)-S(30)\right).
> $$
>
> Since
> $$
> S(30)=\frac{30\cdot31}{2}=465,
> $$
> this becomes
> $$
> \frac{1}{30}\left(k(k+1)-465\right).
> $$
>
> If instead $a+b$ is even, so $m$ is an integer $k$, then roll $k$ is a tie, and the smaller number wins on $1,\dots,k-1$, while the larger wins on $k+1,\dots,30$. The expected payoff to the player with the smaller number is
> $$
> \frac{1}{30}\left(S(k-1)-\bigl(S(30)-S(k)\bigr)\right).
> $$
> Using $S(k)=S(k-1)+k$, this simplifies to
> $$
> \frac{1}{30}\left(2S(k-1)+k-465\right)
> =
> \frac{1}{30}\left(k^2-465\right).
> $$
>
> In either case, the sign of the expectation is determined by whether one has chosen the smaller or larger number relative to the midpoint.
>
> ## 3. Which side of the midpoint is better
>
> The total sum of die values is $465$. Half of this is
> $$
> \frac{465}{2}=232.5.
> $$
>
> We compare the sum of values on the lower side with the sum on the upper side.
>
> Compute:
> $$
> S(21)=\frac{21\cdot22}{2}=231,
> \qquad
> S(22)=\frac{22\cdot23}{2}=253.
> $$
>
> Thus the lower values up to $21$ sum to $231$, still below half, while up to $22$ they sum to $253$, above half.
>
> So the balance point is between $21$ and $22$.
>
> This means:
>
> - if the cutoff between "lower-number wins" and "higher-number wins" lies below $21.5$, then the higher chosen number has the advantage;
> - if the cutoff lies above $21.5$, then the lower chosen number has the advantage.
>
> Therefore the game is governed by placing the midpoint as close as possible to $21.5$, and by which side of that midpoint one occupies.
>
> ## 4. The special pair 21 and 22
>
> Since $21.5$ is exactly the midpoint of the two integers $21$ and $22$, this adjacent pair achieves the closest possible approach to an even split, and therefore the smallest possible edge of any pair of numbers. Using the odd-sum formula with $k=21$, the player holding the smaller number $21$ has expected payoff
> $$
> \frac{1}{30}\bigl(21\cdot 22-465\bigr)=\frac{462-465}{30}=-\frac1{10},
> $$
> so the player holding $22$ has expected payoff $+\frac1{10}$.
>
> Any other pair of numbers places the midpoint farther from $21.5$, which only widens this edge in favor of whichever side is already ahead. So the two numbers $21$ and $22$ are the only ones that keep the edge this small, and whoever ends up holding $22$ in this pair is the one who profits.
>
> Since you move first, you can simply claim the number $22$ for yourself, before your opponent has any say in the matter.
>
> ## 5. Confirming that the opponent cannot do better than 21
>
> Suppose you choose $a=22$. Does your opponent have a reply better than $b=21$?
>
> - Trying $b=20$: here $a+b=42$ is even, so $m=21$ is an integer and roll $21$ is a tie. With $k=21$, the smaller number's payoff is $\frac{1}{30}(21^2-465)=\frac{441-465}{30}=-\frac{24}{30}=-0.8$, so the opponent (holding $20$) would get $-0.8$, worse than $-0.1$.
> - Trying $b=23$: here $a+b=45$ is odd, so $m=22.5$ and $k=22$. The smaller number here is $22$ (yours), whose payoff is $\frac{1}{30}(22\cdot23-465)=\frac{506-465}{30}=\frac{41}{30}\approx 1.367$ to you, so the opponent (holding $23$, the larger number) gets $-1.367$, far worse for them than $-0.1$.
>
> Moving further away from $22$ in either direction only makes the opponent's position worse, since it pushes the midpoint even farther from the balance point while leaving you on the favorable side. So the opponent's best available reply is indeed $b=21$, limiting their loss to $0.1$ and your gain to $0.1$.
>
> ## 6. Why 22 is the right number to claim, not some other
>
> If instead you chose $a=21$ and let the opponent take $22$, the roles above would reverse and you would be the one losing $0.1$. Likewise, choosing a number farther from the balance point, such as $13$, only invites the opponent to pick the complementary number $43-13=30$, giving midpoint $21.5$ with the opponent holding the larger, favorable number $30$: this nets the opponent $+0.1$ and costs you $0.1$, the mirror image of what happens when you correctly claim $22$. So among all possible first choices, $22$ is the one that secures the small available edge for yourself instead of ceding it.
>
> ## Final answer
>
> The optimal choice is to go first and choose $22$. Your opponent's best reply is $21$, and the resulting expected value is
>
> $$
> \boxed{+0.10 \text{ dollars to the first player (you).}}
> $$
