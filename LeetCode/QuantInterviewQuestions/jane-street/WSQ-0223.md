> [!example] Question 223 · Jane Street
> ## Internship Interview

What is the probability of getting an even number of heads when tossing 4 coins? How does this change with 100 coins? What if one coin is unfair? What if all coins are unfair? What if only one coin is fair? How many fair coins are needed to ensure a 50% chance of getting an even number of heads?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=224)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> For any collection of independent coin flips, let $H$ be the number of heads. We want the probability that $H$ is even.
>
> A standard parity argument is:
>
> - For a fair coin, flipping it changes the parity of the total number of heads with probability $\tfrac12$.
> - More generally, if coin $i$ has probability $p_i$ of heads, then the probability of an even number of heads is
> $$
> P(H \text{ is even})=\frac{1+\prod_{i=1}^n (1-2p_i)}{2}.
> $$
>
> This formula answers all parts.
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. Four fair coins
>
> For a fair coin, $p_i=\tfrac12$, so
> $$
> 1-2p_i = 1-2\cdot \frac12 = 0.
> $$
> Hence for $n=4$ fair coins,
> $$
> P(H \text{ is even})=\frac{1+0}{2}=\frac12.
> $$
>
> So the probability is
> $$
> \boxed{\frac12}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. One hundred fair coins
>
> Exactly the same reasoning applies. Since every fair coin has $1-2p_i=0$,
> $$
> P(H \text{ is even})=\frac{1+0}{2}=\frac12.
> $$
>
> So the probability is
> $$
> \boxed{\frac12}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. What if one of them is unfair?
>
> Assume there are $n$ coins total, with $n-1$ fair coins and one unfair coin having probability $p$ of heads.
>
> Since at least one coin is fair, one factor in the product is
> $$
> 1-2\cdot \frac12 = 0,
> $$
> so the whole product is $0$. Therefore
> $$
> P(H \text{ is even})=\frac{1+0}{2}=\frac12.
> $$
>
> Thus, even if one coin is unfair, as long as at least one other coin is fair,
> $$
> \boxed{P(H \text{ is even})=\frac12}.
> $$
>
> In particular:
>
> - for $4$ coins, if one is unfair and the other $3$ are fair, then the answer is $\boxed{\tfrac12}$;
> - for $100$ coins, if one is unfair and the other $99$ are fair, then the answer is $\boxed{\tfrac12}$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. What if all of them are unfair?
>
> Suppose coin $i$ has head probability $p_i$, with all coins independent. Then
> $$
> \boxed{P(H \text{ is even})=\frac{1+\prod_{i=1}^n (1-2p_i)}{2}}.
> $$
>
> This is the general answer.
>
> ### Special case: all coins have the same bias $p$
>
> Then
> $$
> P(H \text{ is even})=\frac{1+(1-2p)^n}{2}.
> $$
>
> For example:
>
> - if $p=\tfrac12$, this becomes $\tfrac12$;
> - if $p=1$, then all coins are always heads, so
>   $$
>   P(H \text{ is even})=
>   \begin{cases}
>   1, & n \text{ even},\\
>   0, & n \text{ odd}.
>   \end{cases}
>   $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 5. What if only one of them is fair?
>
> If among the $n$ independent coins, exactly one is fair and the others may be arbitrary, then again one factor in
> $$
> \prod_{i=1}^n (1-2p_i)
> $$
> is
> $$
> 1-2\cdot \frac12=0.
> $$
> So
> $$
> P(H \text{ is even})=\frac{1+0}{2}=\frac12.
> $$
>
> Therefore, if only one coin is fair,
> $$
> \boxed{P(H \text{ is even})=\frac12}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 6. How many fair coins are needed to ensure a $50\%$ chance of getting an even number of heads?
>
> From the formula
> $$
> P(H \text{ is even})=\frac{1+\prod_{i=1}^n (1-2p_i)}{2},
> $$
> we get exactly $\tfrac12$ whenever
> $$
> \prod_{i=1}^n (1-2p_i)=0.
> $$
> This happens as soon as at least one coin is fair, because for a fair coin
> $$
> 1-2\cdot \frac12=0.
> $$
>
> So the minimum number of fair coins needed is
> $$
> \boxed{1}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Final answers
>
> - $4$ fair coins: $\boxed{\tfrac12}$.
> - $100$ fair coins: $\boxed{\tfrac12}$.
> - One coin unfair, the rest fair: $\boxed{\tfrac12}$.
> - All coins unfair: 
>   $$
>   \boxed{P(H \text{ even})=\frac{1+\prod_{i=1}^n (1-2p_i)}{2}}.
>   $$
> - Only one coin fair: $\boxed{\tfrac12}$.
> - Number of fair coins needed to ensure a $50\%$ chance of even heads: $\boxed{1}$.
