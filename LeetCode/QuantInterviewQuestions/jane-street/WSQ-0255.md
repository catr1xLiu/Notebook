> [!example] Question 255 · Jane Street
> ## Trader Intern Interview

Basketball players A and B each play in Game 1 and Game 2. In both games, A has a higher shooting average than B. Is it possible for B to have a higher overall shooting average than A? If so, provide an example.

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=256)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Yes. This is possible because the two games may involve different numbers of shot attempts. A player can have a higher percentage in each game separately, yet the weighted average over both games can be lower. This is an instance of **Simpson’s paradox**.
>
> ## Example
>
> Suppose the shooting results are as follows.
>
> ### Game 1
> - Player $A$: $9$ made out of $10$ shots, so average $= \frac{9}{10} = 90\%$
> - Player $B$: $80$ made out of $100$ shots, so average $= \frac{80}{100} = 80\%$
>
> Thus, in Game $1$, $A$ has the higher shooting average.
>
> ### Game 2
> - Player $A$: $1$ made out of $10$ shots, so average $= \frac{1}{10} = 10\%$
> - Player $B$: $0$ made out of $1$ shot, so average $= \frac{0}{1} = 0\%$
>
> Thus, in Game $2$, $A$ again has the higher shooting average.
>
> ## Overall averages
>
> - Player $A$: total made $= 9+1 = 10$, total shots $= 10+10 = 20$
>   $$
>   \text{Overall average for } A = \frac{10}{20} = 50\%
>   $$
>
> - Player $B$: total made $= 80+0 = 80$, total shots $= 100+1 = 101$
>   $$
>   \text{Overall average for } B = \frac{80}{101} \approx 79.2\%
>   $$
>
> Therefore,
> $$
> \frac{10}{20} < \frac{80}{101},
> $$
> so $B$ has the higher overall shooting average even though $A$ had the higher shooting average in each individual game.
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> Yes, it is possible. One example is:
>
> - Game $1$: $A=\frac{9}{10}$, $B=\frac{80}{100}$
> - Game $2$: $A=\frac{1}{10}$, $B=\frac{0}{1}$
>
> Then $A$ has the better average in each game, but $B$ has the better overall average.
