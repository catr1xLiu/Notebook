> [!example] Question 654 · Jane Street
> ## Quant Trader Intern Interview

You roll a 4-sided die and sequentially accumulate the total score from each roll. What is the expected value of the first total that exceeds 100? Follow-up: What if you start at 96 instead of 0?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=655)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let $S_n$ be the running total after $n$ rolls of a fair $4$-sided die taking values in $\{1,2,3,4\}$. We stop at the first time the total exceeds $100$, and we seek the expected value of that first total above $100$.
>
> Equivalently, if the current total is $k \le 100$, define
> $$
> E_k = \text{expected final total when starting from total } k.
> $$
> Then the desired quantity is $E_0$.
>
> ## Step 1: Recurrence
>
> For $k > 100$, we have already exceeded $100$, so
> $$
> E_k = k.
> $$
>
> For $k \le 100$, one more roll is made, giving
> $$
> E_k = \frac14\bigl(E_{k+1}+E_{k+2}+E_{k+3}+E_{k+4}\bigr).
> $$
>
> This recurrence determines all $E_k$.
>
> ## Step 2: Work backward near the boundary
>
> Since $E_{101}=101$, $E_{102}=102$, $E_{103}=103$, $E_{104}=104$, we get
> $$
> E_{100}=\frac{101+102+103+104}{4}=102.5.
> $$
>
> Next,
> $$
> E_{99}=\frac{E_{100}+101+102+103}{4}=\frac{102.5+101+102+103}{4}=102.125.
> $$
>
> Similarly,
> $$
> E_{98}=\frac{E_{99}+E_{100}+101+102}{4}=\frac{102.125+102.5+101+102}{4}=101.90625,
> $$
> and
> $$
> E_{97}=\frac{E_{98}+E_{99}+E_{100}+101}{4}=\frac{101.90625+102.125+102.5+101}{4}=101.8828125.
> $$
>
> Now,
> $$
> E_{96}=\frac{E_{97}+E_{98}+E_{99}+E_{100}}{4}=\frac{101.8828125+101.90625+102.125+102.5}{4}=102.103515625.
> $$
>
> ## Step 3: Where the true limiting value comes from
>
> It is tempting to argue that once four consecutive values are known, the recursion propagates that same constant to every earlier state. But $E_{97},E_{98},E_{99},E_{100}$ are **not** equal to one another — they are $101.883,\,101.906,\,102.125,\,102.5$, four genuinely different numbers — so that shortcut never gets off the ground. More fundamentally, **any** constant $C$ trivially satisfies $C=\tfrac14(C+C+C+C)$, so the bare recurrence can never by itself tell us which constant $E_k$ approaches as $k$ moves far below $100$; that value has to come from matching the behavior of the sequence to the boundary, not from the recurrence alone.
>
> To find the correct limiting value, view the process as a renewal process: each step adds an independent increment uniform on $\{1,2,3,4\}$, with mean $\mu=2.5$. Once the starting point is many rolls away from the threshold $100$, the "phase" at which the walk eventually crosses $100$ stops depending on the exact starting point and depends only on the step distribution. Precisely, consider the roll that straddles the threshold: if the walk is at some total $s<100$ and the next roll of size $X\in\{1,2,3,4\}$ carries it past $100$, then, far from the starting point, that roll $X$ is *size-biased* — a jump of size $x$ is $x$ times more likely to be the one that straddles $100$ than a uniformly chosen jump would be, and the amount by which the threshold sits inside that jump is, in the long run, uniform over its $x$ possible positions. So, given that the straddling jump has size $x$, the overshoot (final total minus $100$) is uniform on $\{1,\dots,x\}$, with mean $\tfrac{x+1}2$.
>
> Averaging over the size-biased distribution of the straddling jump (a jump of size $x$ carries relative weight $x\cdot P(X=x)$), the limiting expected overshoot is
> $$
> L=\frac{E[X(X+1)]}{2E[X]}=\frac{E[X^2]+E[X]}{2E[X]}.
> $$
>
> For $X$ uniform on $\{1,2,3,4\}$: $E[X]=2.5$ and $E[X^2]=\dfrac{1^2+2^2+3^2+4^2}{4}=\dfrac{30}{4}=7.5$, so
> $$
> L=\frac{7.5+2.5}{2\times2.5}=\frac{10}{5}=2.
> $$
>
> So, far from the boundary, $E_k\to k+L=k+2$. Starting from $k=0$, which is $100$ rolls away from the threshold — far more than enough for this limit to take hold to overwhelming precision — we get
> $$
> E_0\approx 100+2=102.
> $$
>
> Carrying out the exact backward recursion all the way from $k=100$ down to $k=0$ confirms this numerically: $E_0$ equals $102$ to more than fifteen decimal places, with the tiny remaining deviation shrinking geometrically the farther the starting point sits from the boundary. So, to every precision that matters,
> $$
> E_0=102.
> $$
>
> ## Step 4: The follow-up, starting from $96$
>
> Starting from $96$ is a genuinely different question from starting at $0$: the point $96$ is close enough to the boundary $100$ that the asymptotic limit from Step 3 has not yet taken hold, and $E_{96}$ must instead be read directly from the boundary computation in Step 2:
> $$
> E_{96}=102.103515625.
> $$
>
> This is **not** the same number as $E_0$ — the two starting points sit at different distances from the boundary, and only $E_0$, far from the boundary, reaches the clean limiting value $102$.
>
> ## Final answers
>
> - Starting from $0$, the expected first total above $100$ is
> $$
> E_0=102.
> $$
>
> - Starting from $96$, the expected first total above $100$ is
> $$
> E_{96}=102.103515625=\frac{52277}{512}.
> $$
