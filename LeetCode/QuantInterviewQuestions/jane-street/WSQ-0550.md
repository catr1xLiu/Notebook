> [!example] Question 550 · Jane Street
> ## Assistant Trader Interview

You have two dice and two players. Each player picks a number. The winner is the player who picks the number closest to, or equal to, the sum of the rolls of the dice. What is the optimal strategy to maximize your chances of winning? Is it always better to pick the number that has the highest probability of being rolled? Discuss whether this strategy also applies if the two dice numbers are multiplied instead of summed.

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=551)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> Let the outcome of the two dice be a random variable $S$. In the usual version of the game with two fair six-sided dice added, the possible sums are
>
> $$
> 2,3,\dots,12,
> $$
>
> with probabilities
>
> $$
> \mathbb P(S=s)=\frac{1}{36}(1,2,3,4,5,6,5,4,3,2,1)
> $$
>
> for $s=2,3,\dots,12$ respectively.
>
> Two players choose numbers, say $a$ and $b$. The winner is the one whose chosen number is closer to $S$; if one of the chosen numbers equals $S$, that player certainly wins. The question is: what choice optimizes the chance of winning?
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. General principle
>
> Assume the two chosen numbers are distinct. Without loss of generality, let
>
> $$
> a<b.
> $$
>
> Then:
>
> - player $a$ wins when $S$ is closer to $a$ than to $b$,
> - player $b$ wins when $S$ is closer to $b$ than to $a$.
>
> The boundary is the midpoint
>
> $$
> m=\frac{a+b}{2}.
> $$
>
> Thus:
>
> - if $S<m$, then $a$ is closer;
> - if $S>m$, then $b$ is closer;
> - if $S=m$ and $m$ is an attainable outcome, then there is a tie.
>
> So the probability that $a$ wins is the total probability mass on outcomes on its side of the midpoint, and similarly for $b$.
>
> Therefore, **the game is not about choosing the most likely exact outcome**. It is about choosing a point that captures as much probability mass as possible on its side of the decision boundary.
>
> This is the key strategy in any such game, whether the dice are added, multiplied, or transformed in some other way.
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Best response when sums are used
>
> For sums of two dice, the distribution is symmetric about $7$ and unimodal. Hence the central values are best.
>
> To see the strategic choice clearly, suppose one player has already chosen $a$. The other player should choose a number $b$ that places the midpoint between $a$ and $b$ so that as much probability mass as possible lies on their side.
>
> Because the distribution is symmetric and concentrated near $7$, the optimal choices are the central numbers.
>
> Let us compare the strongest candidates.
>
> ### Choosing $7$
>
> If one player chooses $7$, then any other choice lies on one side of $7$. Since $7$ is the center of the distribution, the midpoint between $7$ and any other choice leaves $7$ with at least as much probability mass as the opponent, and in fact strictly more for any distinct legal choice.
>
> For example, if the opponent chooses $6$, then the midpoint is
>
> $$
> \frac{6+7}{2}=6.5.
> $$
>
> So:
>
> - $6$ wins for sums $2,3,4,5,6$,
> - $7$ wins for sums $7,8,9,10,11,12$.
>
> Hence
>
> $$
> \mathbb P(6\text{ wins})=\frac{1+2+3+4+5}{36}=\frac{15}{36},
> $$
>
> $$
> \mathbb P(7\text{ wins})=\frac{6+5+4+3+2+1}{36}=\frac{21}{36}.
> $$
>
> Thus $7$ beats $6$.
>
> Similarly, against $8$, the midpoint is $7.5$, so:
>
> - $7$ wins for $2,\dots,7$,
> - $8$ wins for $8,\dots,12$,
>
> and again
>
> $$
> \mathbb P(7\text{ wins})=\frac{21}{36},\qquad \mathbb P(8\text{ wins})=\frac{15}{36}.
> $$
>
> Against more extreme choices, $7$ does even better.
>
> Therefore, if players must choose distinct integers, **$7$ is the optimal choice**.
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. If the first player has already chosen a number
>
> Suppose the first player chooses $a$. What is the best response?
>
> Because the sum distribution is symmetric and peaks at $7$, the best response is the available number nearest the center, on the opposite side if possible. In practice:
>
> - if $a<7$, choose $a+1$ only when that moves you closer to the center, but more generally choose the integer near $7$ that gives the larger half of the distribution;
> - if $a>7$, similarly choose toward $7$;
> - if $a=7$, no other integer can do better than losing to $7$.
>
> A direct check shows the ranking of single picks is centered at $7$, then $6$ and $8$, then $5$ and $9$, etc. But this ranking is not the whole story: the best response depends on the opponent’s pick through the midpoint rule.
>
> Still, for the standard sum game, the equilibrium intuition is simple: **the closer your number is to $7$, the better**.
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. Why “pick the most likely outcome” is not the general rule
>
> The most likely exact sum is indeed $7$, but that is not why it is optimal. What matters is the probability of being **closer** than the opponent, not the probability of exact equality.
>
> A number with lower exact probability can still be a better strategic choice if it captures more total probability mass on its side of the midpoint.
>
> This becomes especially important in other games, such as when the two dice outcomes are multiplied.
>
> ---
>
> ## 5. General method for any rule (sum, product, etc.)
>
> Let the random outcome be $X$, with probability mass function $p(x)$. If two players choose numbers $a<b$, then:
>
> - player $a$ wins on outcomes $x$ with $x<\frac{a+b}{2}$,
> - player $b$ wins on outcomes $x$ with $x>\frac{a+b}{2}$,
> - outcomes exactly equal to $\frac{a+b}{2}$ are ties if that value is attainable.
>
> So the winning probabilities are
>
> $$
> \mathbb P(a\text{ wins})
> =
> \sum_{x<\frac{a+b}{2}} p(x)
> \;+\;
> \frac12\sum_{x=\frac{a+b}{2}} p(x)
> $$
>
> and
>
> $$
> \mathbb P(b\text{ wins})
> =
> \sum_{x>\frac{a+b}{2}} p(x)
> \;+\;
> \frac12\sum_{x=\frac{a+b}{2}} p(x),
> $$
>
> if ties are split evenly.
>
> Thus the optimal strategy is:
>
> 1. determine the distribution of the game outcome $X$;
> 2. for each possible opponent choice, compare the probability mass on either side of the midpoint;
> 3. choose the number that maximizes your winning probability.
>
> In particular, the optimal choice is generally near a **median** of the outcome distribution, not necessarily at its **mode**.
>
> - The **mode** is the most likely exact outcome.
> - The **median** is the point that splits total probability mass as evenly as possible.
>
> Since winning depends on closeness, medians are usually more relevant than modes.
>
> ---
>
> ## 6. Final answer
>
> For the usual game where the two dice are **added**, the optimal choice is
>
> $$
> \boxed{7}.
> $$
>
> More generally, the correct strategy is **not** simply to pick the number with the highest probability of being rolled. Instead, one should pick a number that maximizes the probability of being **closer than the opponent’s number**, which is determined by how the outcome distribution is split by the midpoint between the two chosen numbers.
>
> So:
>
> - for sums of two dice, the optimal play is to choose the central value $7$;
> - in other versions of the game, such as using the **product** instead of the sum, the best choice need not be the most likely exact outcome. The right choice is found by analyzing the distribution and using the midpoint rule above.
