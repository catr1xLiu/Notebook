> [!example] Question 284 · Jane Street
> ## Quant Trader Interview

You are playing a one-player game with two opaque boxes. At each turn, you can choose to either 'place' or 'take.' 'Place' puts \$1 from a third party into one box randomly, while 'take' empties out one box randomly and the money is yours. The game consists of 100 turns, and at each turn you must either place or take. Assuming optimal play, what is the expected payoff of this game? Note: You do not know how much money you have taken until the end of the game.

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=285)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> Let the amounts currently in the two boxes be $a$ and $b$, and let
> $$
> s=a+b
> $$
> be the total amount present.
>
> At each turn:
>
> - **Place**: a dollar is added to one of the two boxes, chosen uniformly at random.
> - **Take**: one of the two boxes is emptied, chosen uniformly at random, and its contents are added to your payoff.
>
> The crucial feature of this game is stated explicitly: **you do not know how much money you have taken until the end**. You never observe $a$, $b$, or your running total during play. So this is not a game where you can watch the state and react to it — you must commit, in effect, to a fixed plan for all $100$ turns before you start, since there is nothing to condition on along the way.
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. Why only the plan's *shape* matters, not real-time information
>
> Even though you cannot observe $a$ and $b$, we can still track their expected values turn by turn, and this is enough to evaluate any plan.
>
> **If a turn is "place":** a dollar is added to a uniformly random box, so the total in the boxes increases by exactly $1$, with certainty:
> $$
> s \to s+1.
> $$
>
> **If a turn is "take":** a uniformly random box is emptied. Conditional on the current contents $a,b$, the expected amount you gain is
> $$
> \mathbb E[\text{gain}\mid a,b]=\frac12 a+\frac12 b=\frac{s}{2},
> $$
> and whichever box is *not* taken retains its contents, so the total remaining in the boxes is exactly $s-\text{gain}$. Taking expectations,
> $$
> \mathbb E[\text{gain}]=\frac{\mathbb E[s]}{2},\qquad \mathbb E[s_{\text{after}}]=\frac{\mathbb E[s]}{2}.
> $$
>
> So a take turns the expected pot in half, banking the other half as expected gain — regardless of how the money happens to be split between the two boxes. Since your total expected payoff is just the sum of the expected gains from each take, and both operations act on $\mathbb E[s]$ in a completely deterministic way, **the expected value of any fixed sequence of places and takes is determined entirely by the sequence itself** — there is no benefit "lost" by being unable to observe the process, because the optimal plan does not depend on realized randomness in the first place.
>
> This reduces the problem to a clean deterministic optimization: choose a sequence of $100$ moves, each "P" (place: $s\to s+1$) or "T" (take: add $s/2$ to the running gain, then $s\to s/2$), starting from $s=0$, to maximize total gain.
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. Placing before taking is optimal
>
> Intuitively, a place is worth relatively more when the pot is small (adding $1$ to a small pot is a big proportional gain that a later take will fully capture), while a take is worth more the larger the pot already is. This suggests building the pot up first and harvesting it only at the end, and checking small cases confirms it:
>
> - $1$ turn: best is $\mathrm P$, gain $0$ (there's nothing to take yet).
> - $2$ turns: best is $\mathrm{PT}$, gain $0.5$.
> - $3$ turns: best is $\mathrm{PPT}$, gain $1$.
> - $4$ turns: best is $\mathrm{PPPT}$, gain $1.5$.
> - $5$ turns: best is $\mathrm{PPPTT}$, gain $2.25$.
> - $6$ turns: best is $\mathrm{PPPPTT}$, gain $3$.
>
> Exhaustively checking all $2^n$ sequences for small $n$ confirms in every case that the optimal order is a block of places followed by a block of takes — never an interleaving. This makes sense: swapping a take earlier than necessary only shrinks the pot sooner, and every dollar not yet placed misses out on being doubled-back-down by a subsequent take.
>
> So the optimal plan has the form: place $P$ times, then take $T$ times, with $P+T=100$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Value of a block plan, and the optimal split
>
> After $P$ places, the pot is exactly $s=P$ (with certainty — every place adds exactly $1$). Then each of the $T$ takes halves the (expected) pot and banks the other half:
> $$
> \text{gain from take }k = \frac{P}{2^k},\qquad k=1,\dots,T.
> $$
> Summing the geometric series,
> $$
> g(P,T)=\sum_{k=1}^{T}\frac{P}{2^k}=P\left(1-\frac{1}{2^T}\right).
> $$
>
> With $P=100-T$, we maximize
> $$
> g(T)=(100-T)\left(1-2^{-T}\right)
> $$
> over $T=0,1,\dots,100$. Computing this for $T$ near the optimum:
>
> - $T=4$: $g=96\cdot\frac{15}{16}=90$
> - $T=5$: $g=95\cdot\frac{31}{32}=92.03125$
> - $T=6$: $g=94\cdot\frac{63}{64}=92.53125$
> - $T=7$: $g=93\cdot\frac{127}{128}\approx 92.2734$
> - $T=8$: $g=92\cdot\frac{255}{256}\approx 91.6406$
>
> The value rises to $T=6$ and falls on either side, so the optimum is
>
> $$
> T=6,\qquad P=94.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 4. Expected payoff
>
> The optimal plan is: **place $94$ times, then take $6$ times.**
>
> The expected payoff is
> $$
> E = 94\left(1-\frac{1}{2^6}\right) = 94\cdot\frac{63}{64} = \frac{5922}{64} = \frac{2961}{32} = 92.53125.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 5. Conclusion
>
> Because you receive no information about the state of the boxes or your running total during play, the optimal strategy cannot be adaptive — it must be a fixed plan chosen in advance. That plan is to place $94$ times (building the expected pot up to $94$ with certainty), then take $6$ times in a row, each take collecting half of what remains.
>
> Hence the maximum expected payoff over the $100$-turn game is
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{2961}{32}=92.53125}.
> $$
