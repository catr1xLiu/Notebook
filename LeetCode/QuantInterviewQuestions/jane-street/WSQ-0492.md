> [!example] Question 492 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Trader Interview

If you have balls weighing 1g, 2g, ..., up to 40g, and you have a fair balance, what is the minimum number of their weights you need to know in advance so that you can measure all the others?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=493)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> To measure an unknown ball on a fair balance using only balls whose weights are already known, the unknown weight must be representable as a difference of two sums of known weights. Equivalently, if the known weights are $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_k$, then every unknown weight $m$ must be writable in the form
>
> $$
> m=\sum_{i=1}^k \varepsilon_i a_i,
> \qquad \varepsilon_i\in\{-1,0,1\}.
> $$
>
> Thus, with $k$ known balls, at most $3^k$ different values can be formed in this way, including $0$. Hence the number of positive measurable weights is at most
>
> $$
> \frac{3^k-1}{2}.
> $$
>
> We must be able to measure all $40$ weights from $1$ g to $40$ g, so we need
>
> $$
> \frac{3^k-1}{2}\ge 40.
> $$
>
> Check powers of $3$:
>
> $$
> k=3:\quad \frac{3^3-1}{2}=\frac{27-1}{2}=13<40,
> $$
>
> $$
> k=4:\quad \frac{3^4-1}{2}=\frac{81-1}{2}=40.
> $$
>
> So at least $4$ known weights are necessary.
>
> Now show that $4$ are sufficient. Take the known balls of weights
>
> $$
> 1,\ 3,\ 9,\ 27.
> $$
>
> Using a balance, each weight from $1$ g to $40$ g can be expressed as a balanced ternary combination of these, i.e. as
>
> $$
> m=\varepsilon_0\cdot 1+\varepsilon_1\cdot 3+\varepsilon_2\cdot 9+\varepsilon_3\cdot 27,
> \qquad \varepsilon_i\in\{-1,0,1\}.
> $$
>
> For example:
>
> - $2=3-1$,
> - $5=9-3-1$,
> - $8=9-1$,
> - $20=27-9+3-1$,
> - $40=27+9+3+1$.
>
> Hence every integer weight from $1$ g to $40$ g can be measured.
>
> Therefore, the minimum number of weights that must be known in advance is
>
> $$
> \boxed{4}.
> $$
