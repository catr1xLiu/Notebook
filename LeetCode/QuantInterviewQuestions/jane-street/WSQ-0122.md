> [!example] Question 122 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Trader Intern Interview

If you flip a fair coin twice, what is the expected value of the game, assuming you win \$1 for each head and lose \$1 for each tail?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=123)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> The game pays $\text{\textdollar}1$ for each head and costs $\text{\textdollar}1$ for each tail obtained in two fair coin flips, so the payoff is (number of heads) minus (number of tails).
>
> For flip $i$, define
>
> $$
> X_i =
> \begin{cases}
> +1, & \text{if flip } i \text{ is heads},\\
> -1, & \text{if flip } i \text{ is tails}.
> \end{cases}
> $$
>
> The total payoff is $X = X_1+X_2$.
>
> Since the coin is fair,
>
> $$
> E[X_i] = (+1)\left(\frac12\right)+(-1)\left(\frac12\right) = 0.
> $$
>
> By linearity of expectation,
>
> $$
> E[X] = E[X_1]+E[X_2] = 0+0 = 0.
> $$
>
> This also follows directly by symmetry: heads and tails are equally likely on each flip, and the payoff rule treats them as exact opposites, so every outcome is exactly canceled by an equally likely, oppositely-signed outcome. Explicitly, the sample space is
>
> $$
> \{HH, HT, TH, TT\},
> $$
>
> each with probability $\frac14$, giving payoffs
>
> $$
> +2,\ 0,\ 0,\ -2,
> $$
>
> respectively, so
>
> $$
> E[X] = \frac14(2)+\frac14(0)+\frac14(0)+\frac14(-2) = 0.
> $$
>
> Therefore, the game's expected value is
>
> $$
> \boxed{0}.
> $$
