> [!example] Question 642 · Jane Street
> ## Trader Assistant Interview

You roll a die repeatedly until the sum of the numbers rolled is greater than 13. On which number are you most likely to stop?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=643)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> We roll a fair six-sided die repeatedly and add up the results, stopping the first time the running total exceeds $13$. We want the value $k\in\{1,\dots,6\}$ of the **final roll** — the one that pushes the total past $13$ — that is most likely.
>
> ## 1. A cleaner way to organize the count
>
> Rather than tracking, for every running total $s\le13$ and every final value $k$, the probability that $k$ is eventually the stopping roll, it is simpler to first ask a single question: for each total $s$ from $0$ to $13$, what is the probability $u_s$ that the running total **ever equals exactly $s$** at some point before the process stops?
>
> Since the process starts at $0$, we have $u_0=1$. For $s\ge1$, the total can only reach $s$ by arriving from one of $s-1,s-2,\dots,s-6$ (whichever are nonnegative) via a single die roll, so
> $$
> u_s=\frac16\sum_{i=1}^{\min(s,6)}u_{s-i}.
> $$
>
> Once $u_0,\dots,u_{13}$ are known, the probability that the stopping roll equals $k$ is obtained by summing, over every total $s$ that is actually visited, the chance that the next roll is $k$ and that this roll overshoots $13$, that is, $s+k>13$:
> $$
> P(\text{final roll}=k)=\frac16\sum_{s=14-k}^{13} u_s.
> $$
>
> ## 2. Computing $u_0,\dots,u_6$ in closed form
>
> For $1\le s\le6$, every term $u_0,\dots,u_{s-1}$ appears in the sum, so
> $$
> u_s=\frac16\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}u_j.
> $$
> Let $T_s=\sum_{j=0}^{s}u_j$ denote the partial sum through $u_s$, so $T_0=u_0=1$. Then $u_s=\tfrac16 T_{s-1}$, and therefore
> $$
> T_s=T_{s-1}+u_s=T_{s-1}\left(1+\frac16\right)=\frac76\,T_{s-1}.
> $$
> So $T_s=(7/6)^s$ for $s=0,1,\dots,6$, and consequently, for $s=1,\dots,6$,
> $$
> u_s=\frac16T_{s-1}=\frac16\left(\frac76\right)^{s-1}=\frac{7^{\,s-1}}{6^{\,s}}.
> $$
>
> Explicitly:
> $$
> u_1=\frac16,\quad u_2=\frac{7}{36},\quad u_3=\frac{49}{216},\quad u_4=\frac{343}{1296},\quad u_5=\frac{2401}{7776},\quad u_6=\frac{16807}{46656}.
> $$
>
> For $s\ge7$, the sum in the recursion only ever includes the **last six** $u$-values, so the closed form above no longer applies, and each further term is computed directly from the recursion:
> $$
> u_7\approx0.2536,\quad u_8\approx0.2681,\quad u_9\approx0.2804,\quad u_{10}\approx0.2893,\quad u_{11}\approx0.2934,\quad u_{12}\approx0.2908,\quad u_{13}\approx0.2793.
> $$
>
> ## 3. Assembling the answer
>
> Using $P(\text{final roll}=k)=\dfrac16\sum_{s=14-k}^{13}u_s$, each probability is $\dfrac16$ times a sum of a different number of the $u_s$'s: $k=1$ uses only $u_{13}$, while $k=6$ uses all of $u_8,\dots,u_{13}$. Carrying out these sums gives
> $$
> P(1)\approx0.0465,\quad P(2)\approx0.0950,\quad P(3)\approx0.1439,\quad P(4)\approx0.1921,\quad P(5)\approx0.2389,\quad P(6)\approx0.2835.
> $$
>
> These six probabilities sum to $1$, and they increase steadily with $k$. This makes sense: a larger final roll $k$ can be produced from more different prior totals (any $s$ with $s\ge14-k$), so larger values of $k$ have more ways to be the roll that finally pushes the sum past $13$.
>
> ## 4. Most likely stopping roll
>
> The largest of the six probabilities is
> $$
> P(6)=\frac16\sum_{s=8}^{13}u_s\approx0.2835,
> $$
> so the most likely number to stop on is
>
> $$
> \boxed{6}.
> $$
