> [!example] Question 109 · Jane Street
> ## Trading Interview

There are 99 lions and 1 sheep on an island. The lions want to eat the sheep but also want to stay alive. When a lion eats the sheep, it turns into a sheep. Lions can survive on other foods available on the island. The sheep cannot escape, and all creatures are rational. After some time, how many lions and how many sheep will be left?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=110)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let $L(n)$ denote the outcome when there are $n$ lions and $1$ sheep on the island, assuming all creatures are perfectly rational.
>
> The key observation is:
>
> - If a lion eats the sheep, that lion becomes a sheep.
> - Then the remaining lions may choose whether to eat this new sheep.
> - Since every lion wants to eat sheep but values staying alive more, a lion will eat a sheep only if doing so does **not** eventually lead to its own death.
>
> Thus the problem is solved by backward induction.
>
> ## Step 1: Small cases
>
> ### Case $n=1$
> There is $1$ lion and $1$ sheep.
>
> - If the lion does not eat, the sheep remains.
> - If the lion eats the sheep, it becomes a sheep, and there are no lions left to threaten it.
>
> So the lion safely eats.
>
> Outcome: $0$ lions and $1$ sheep.
>
> ### Case $n=2$
> There are $2$ lions and $1$ sheep.
>
> - Suppose one lion eats the sheep.
> - It becomes a sheep, leaving $1$ lion and $1$ sheep.
> - From the case $n=1$, that remaining lion would eat this sheep.
>
> So the first lion would die. Since lions prefer survival, no lion eats.
>
> Outcome: $2$ lions and $1$ sheep.
>
> ### Case $n=3$
> There are $3$ lions and $1$ sheep.
>
> - If one lion eats the sheep, it becomes a sheep, leaving $2$ lions and $1$ sheep.
> - From the case $n=2$, in that situation no lion eats.
> - Therefore the lion that ate survives as the sheep.
>
> So a lion will eat.
>
> Outcome: $2$ lions and $1$ sheep.
>
> ## Step 2: General pattern
>
> The pattern alternates:
>
> - If $n-1$ lions would **not** eat a sheep, then with $n$ lions one lion can safely eat.
> - If $n-1$ lions **would** eat a sheep, then with $n$ lions no lion dares eat.
>
> Hence:
>
> - For **odd** $n$, a lion eats.
> - For **even** $n$, no lion eats.
>
> ## Step 3: Apply to $n=99$
>
> Since $99$ is odd, one lion will eat the sheep.
>
> After doing so:
>
> - that lion becomes a sheep,
> - the remaining $98$ lions face $1$ sheep.
>
> Because $98$ is even, no further lion eats.
>
> Therefore the final state is:
>
> $$\boxed{98 \text{ lions and } 1 \text{ sheep}}$$
