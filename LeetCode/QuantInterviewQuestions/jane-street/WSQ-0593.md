> [!example] Question 593 · Jane Street
> ## Assistant Trader Interview

If you have a 6 by 6 matrix where each element is either 1 or -1, how many unique matrices are there such that the product of the elements in each row and each column is 1?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=594)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> Let the entries of the $6 \times 6$ matrix be in $\{1,-1\}$, and require that the product of the entries in each row and in each column is $1$.
>
> ## Step 1: Count all unconstrained matrices
>
> A $6 \times 6$ matrix has $36$ entries, each of which can be chosen independently as either $1$ or $-1$. Thus there are
>
> $$
> 2^{36}
> $$
>
> such matrices in total.
>
> ## Step 2: Interpret the row and column conditions
>
> For a row, the product of its entries is $1$ exactly when the row contains an even number of $-1$'s. The same is true for each column.
>
> Thus we seek the number of $\{\pm 1\}$-matrices such that every row and every column has an even number of $-1$'s.
>
> ## Step 3: Determine the number of independent constraints
>
> There are $6$ row conditions and $6$ column conditions, apparently $12$ conditions in total. However, they are not all independent.
>
> Indeed, the product of all row products equals the product of all $36$ entries. Likewise, the product of all column products equals the product of all $36$ entries. Therefore,
>
> $$
> \prod_{i=1}^6 (\text{row } i \text{ product})
> =
> \prod_{j=1}^6 (\text{column } j \text{ product}).
> $$
>
> So once any $11$ of the $12$ conditions are satisfied, the last one is automatically satisfied. Hence there are only
>
> $$
> 12 - 1 = 11
> $$
>
> independent constraints.
>
> ## Step 4: Count the valid matrices
>
> Each independent condition cuts the number of possibilities by a factor of $2$. Therefore the number of valid matrices is
>
> $$
> 2^{36-11} = 2^{25}.
> $$
>
> Equivalently, one may choose the entries in the first $5$ rows and first $5$ columns arbitrarily, giving
>
> $$
> 5 \times 5 = 25
> $$
>
> free choices, and then the remaining entries are forced by the parity conditions.
>
> Thus the number of such matrices is
>
> $$
> \boxed{2^{25}}.
> $$
>
> Numerically,
>
> $$
> 2^{25} = 33,\!554,\!432.
> $$
