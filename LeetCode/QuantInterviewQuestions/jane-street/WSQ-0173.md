> [!example] Question 173 · Jane Street
> ## Intern Interview

You have a tricycle and plan to travel one thousand miles. You also have two spare tires with you. If you want each of the five tires to be worn equally by the end of the journey, what is the minimum number of stops you must make to achieve this?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=174)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> To have all $5$ tires worn equally after a $1000$-mile trip, each tire must be used for the same total mileage.
>
> A tricycle uses $3$ tires at any moment, so over the whole trip the total tire-mileage is
>
> $$
> 3 \cdot 1000 = 3000.
> $$
>
> Since there are $5$ tires altogether, each tire must therefore be used for
>
> $$
> \frac{3000}{5} = 600
> $$
>
> miles.
>
> So the problem is to schedule the tires so that each one is on the tricycle for exactly $600$ miles, using as few stops (tire changes) as possible. Let the tires be $A,B,C,D,E$.
>
> ## A first attempt: equal segments
>
> A convenient way to think about this is to divide the trip into segments, each using exactly $3$ of the $5$ tires. Suppose we try $4$ equal segments of $250$ miles each:
>
> - Segment $1$: tires $A,B,C$
> - Segment $2$: tires $A,D,E$
> - Segment $3$: tires $B,D,E$
> - Segment $4$: tires $C,D,E$
>
> Then the total mileage of each tire is:
>
> - $A$: segments $1,2$, so $250+250=500$
> - $B$: segments $1,3$, so $250+250=500$
> - $C$: segments $1,4$, so $250+250=500$
> - $D$: segments $2,3,4$, so $750$
> - $E$: segments $2,3,4$, so $750$
>
> So equal $250$-mile segments do not work: tires $D$ and $E$, used in three segments each, end up overused relative to $A$, $B$, $C$. This makes sense, since $600$ is not a multiple of $250$, so no tire used in $2$ or $3$ equal $250$-mile segments can land on exactly $600$.
>
> ## Using unequal segment lengths
>
> Since tires $D$ and $E$ are used in all three of segments $2$–$4$ while $A,B,C$ are each used in only two segments (segment $1$ plus one more), segment $1$ should be made **longer** than the other three, so that its extra length compensates $A$, $B$, and $C$ for being in fewer segments overall.
>
> Try:
>
> - Segment $1$, length $400$: tires $A,B,C$
> - Segment $2$, length $200$: tires $A,D,E$
> - Segment $3$, length $200$: tires $B,D,E$
> - Segment $4$, length $200$: tires $C,D,E$
>
> Check the total length: $400+200+200+200=1000$, correct. Now compute each tire's mileage:
>
> - $A$: segments $1,2$, so $400+200=600$
> - $B$: segments $1,3$, so $400+200=600$
> - $C$: segments $1,4$, so $400+200=600$
> - $D$: segments $2,3,4$, so $200+200+200=600$
> - $E$: segments $2,3,4$, so $200+200+200=600$
>
> Every tire gets exactly $600$ miles. This uses $4$ segments, which requires
>
> $$
> 4-1=3
> $$
>
> stops.
>
> ## Why fewer than $3$ stops is impossible
>
> It remains to show that $2$ stops — that is, only $3$ segments — cannot work.
>
> With $3$ segments of lengths $L_1,L_2,L_3$ (summing to $1000$), each using $3$ of the $5$ tires, consider how many segments each tire participates in. A tire used in all $3$ segments would rack up $L_1+L_2+L_3=1000\ne 600$ miles, and a tire used in $0$ segments would get $0\ne 600$ miles; both are impossible, so **every tire must be used in either exactly $1$ or exactly $2$ of the $3$ segments.**
>
> Now count "sit-outs" (segments a tire is *not* used in). Since each segment uses $3$ of the $5$ tires, each segment is sat out by the other $2$ tires, giving
>
> $$
> 3\text{ segments}\times 2\text{ sit-outs} = 6
> $$
>
> total sit-outs distributed among the $5$ tires. Since every tire sits out either $1$ segment (if used in $2$) or $2$ segments (if used in $1$), and never $0$ or $3$, let $p$ tires sit out exactly $1$ segment and $q$ tires sit out exactly $2$. Then
>
> $$
> p+q=5, \qquad p+2q=6,
> $$
>
> which gives $q=1$ and $p=4$. So **exactly one tire is used in only a single segment, and the other four are each used in exactly two segments** — this split is forced, not a choice.
>
> The lone tire used in only one segment gets mileage equal to that segment's length alone, which must equal $600$; call this segment $S$, so $L_S=600$. Since the total is $1000$, the other two segments satisfy
>
> $$
> L_1+L_2+L_3-L_S = 400,
> $$
>
> i.e. the remaining two segment-lengths sum to $400$.
>
> Now consider any of the four tires used in exactly two segments. Its two segments are some pair drawn from $\{1,2,3\}$, and its mileage is the sum of those two segments' lengths, which must also equal $600$. But any pair of segments either includes $S$ (length $600$) together with one other segment of positive length — giving a sum strictly greater than $600$ — or consists of the *other two* segments, whose lengths sum to only $400\ne 600$. Neither possibility equals $600$, so **no valid two-segment pattern exists** for the remaining four tires once the first tire forces $L_S=600$.
>
> This is a contradiction, so $3$ segments (equivalently, $2$ stops) can never achieve equal wear on all $5$ tires.
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> Therefore, the minimum number of stops is
>
> $$
> \boxed{3}.
> $$
