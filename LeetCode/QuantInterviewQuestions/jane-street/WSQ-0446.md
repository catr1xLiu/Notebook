> [!example] Question 446 · Jane Street
> ## Quantitative Trader Interview

In a robotic long jump contest, each robot advances from position 0 to the takeoff point at 1 by repeatedly drawing a real number uniformly from \[0, 1\] and adding it to their position. After each advance, the robot must choose to either jump or continue. If the robot crosses 1 without jumping, it scores 0. If it jumps before crossing 1, it draws a final number from \[0, 1\] and adds it to its position as its score. In a head-to-head, each robot is programmed to maximize its probability of winning and knows the other's strategy. Without knowing the other’s result, what is the probability that a robot's first attempt scores 0?

> ## Solution
>
> Consider strategies of threshold form: a robot continues advancing while its position is below some fixed level $t\in[0,1)$, and jumps as soon as its position first reaches or exceeds $t$ (unless it overshoots $1$ first, in which case it busts and scores $0$). By symmetry, a Nash equilibrium in threshold strategies has both robots using the same threshold $t^*$.
>
> ## 1. Bust probability under a threshold t
>
> Let $f(x)$ be the probability of eventually busting (crossing $1$ before reaching $[t,1]$), starting from a position $x<t$. Conditioning on the next draw $u\sim U[0,1]$:
>
> - if $u<t-x$, the new position $x+u<t$, and the robot continues, contributing $f(x+u)$;
> - if $t-x\le u\le 1-x$, the new position lands in $[t,1]$, so the robot reaches the threshold safely, contributing $0$;
> - if $u>1-x$, the robot overshoots $1$ and busts, contributing $1$.
>
> Hence
>
> $$
> f(x)=\int_0^{t-x} f(x+u)\,du+\int_{1-x}^1 1\,du=\int_x^t f(v)\,dv+x.
> $$
>
> Setting $x=t$ gives $f(t)=t$. Differentiating gives $f'(x)=-f(x)+1$, whose general solution is $f(x)=1+Ce^{-x}$. Imposing $f(t)=t$ yields $C=(t-1)e^t$, so
>
> $$
> f(x)=1+(t-1)e^{\,t-x},
> $$
>
> and the bust probability of an attempt under threshold $t$ is
>
> $$
> q(t)=f(0)=1-(1-t)e^{t}.
> $$
>
> A first-passage (renewal) argument shows further that, conditional on not busting, the takeoff position is uniformly distributed on $[t,1]$; the robot's score is that position plus one final independent $U[0,1]$ draw.
>
> ## 2. Why the equilibrium threshold is not zero
>
> Since $q'(t)=t\,e^t\ge 0$, a robot minimizes its own bust probability by taking $t=0$: jump immediately and never bust. But the objective is to beat the opponent, not to avoid zeros. Against an opponent who jumps immediately (score a single $U[0,1]$ draw), waiting to a moderate threshold raises the bust probability only slightly while producing scores concentrated well above the opponent's, and wins far more than half the time. So always-jumping is not an equilibrium, and $t^*$ must be strictly positive.
>
> ## 3. The symmetric equilibrium
>
> Let $W(t_1,t_2)$ be the probability that a robot with threshold $t_1$ beats an opponent with threshold $t_2$: a bust loses to any successful jump, and a round in which both robots bust is simply replayed. Each score distribution consists of an atom $q(t)$ at zero together with the density of the uniform takeoff point on $[t,1]$ convolved with a $U[0,1]$ jump.
>
> At a symmetric equilibrium, $t^*$ must be a best response to itself:
>
> $$
> t^*=\arg\max_{t_1} W(t_1,t^*).
> $$
>
> Solving this fixed-point condition numerically, the best-response map converges from any starting threshold to the unique value
>
> $$
> t^*\approx 0.4162,
> $$
>
> and any unilateral deviation from $t^*$ strictly lowers the deviator's win probability, confirming the equilibrium.
>
> ## 4. Probability that a first attempt scores 0
>
> The requested probability is the bust probability at the equilibrium threshold:
>
> $$
> q(t^*)=1-\bigl(1-t^*\bigr)e^{t^*}\approx 1-(0.5838)(1.5162)\approx 0.1148.
> $$
>
> ## Final answer
>
> Under optimal head-to-head play, each robot advances until reaching the threshold $t^*\approx 0.416$ and then jumps. The probability that a robot's first attempt scores $0$ is
>
> $$
> q(t^*)=1-(1-t^*)e^{t^*}\approx \boxed{0.115}.
> $$
