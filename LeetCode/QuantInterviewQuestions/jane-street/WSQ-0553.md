> [!example] Question 553 · Jane Street
> ## Assistant Trader Interview

Suppose you own a car worth an unknown amount between \$0 and \$1000. You can make a single bid to buy the car. If your bid exceeds the car's value, you pay your bid and receive the car; if your bid is less than the value, you do not get the car. 1) What should your optimal bid be? 2) If you win, how much do you stand to lose? 3) If you have a mechanic who can increase the value of the car by a constant multiple of the car's current value, what should this constant be to make bidding worthwhile?

[Source](https://www.thewallstreetquants.com/ebook#p=554)

> [!success]- Solution (Click to expand)
> ## Solution
>
> Let the car’s true value be $V$, where $V$ is uniformly distributed on $[0,1000]$. You choose a single bid $b$.
>
> You get the car iff $b \ge V$, and in that case you pay $b$. Thus your profit is
>
> $$
> \Pi =
> \begin{cases}
> V-b, & V \le b,\\
> 0, & V>b.
> \end{cases}
> $$
>
> We assume you know only that $V \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1000)$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 1. Optimal bid without any mechanic
>
> ### Expected profit as a function of $b$
>
> For $0 \le b \le 1000$,
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[\Pi]
> = \frac{1}{1000}\int_0^b (v-b)\,dv.
> $$
>
> Compute:
>
> $$
> \int_0^b (v-b)\,dv
> = \left[\frac{v^2}{2}-bv\right]_0^b
> = \frac{b^2}{2}-b^2
> = -\frac{b^2}{2}.
> $$
>
> Hence
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[\Pi] = -\frac{b^2}{2000}.
> $$
>
> If $b>1000$, then you always get the car, so
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[\Pi] = \mathbb{E}[V]-b = 500-b<0.
> $$
>
> Thus for every $b>0$, the expected profit is negative, and at $b=0$ it is
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[\Pi]=0.
> $$
>
> Therefore the optimal bid is
>
> $$
> \boxed{b=0.}
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 2. How much do you stand to lose if you get the car?
>
> Condition on the event that you get the car. If your bid is $b$, then getting the car means $V\le b$, and under the uniform model,
>
> $$
> V \mid (V\le b) \sim \text{Uniform}(0,b).
> $$
>
> Your loss upon purchase is
>
> $$
> L=b-V.
> $$
>
> So the conditional expected loss is
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[L \mid V\le b]
> = b-\mathbb{E}[V\mid V\le b].
> $$
>
> Since the mean of a uniform distribution on $[0,b]$ is $b/2$,
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[L \mid V\le b] = b-\frac{b}{2}=\frac{b}{2}.
> $$
>
> Thus, **if you get the car, your average loss is**
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{b}{2}.}
> $$
>
> Indeed, the loss ranges from $0$ to $b$, depending on the realized value $V$.
>
> ---
>
> ## 3. Mechanic multiplies the value by a constant factor
>
> Now suppose that if you get the car, a mechanic can increase its value to $cV$, where $c>0$ is a constant.
>
> Then your profit becomes
>
> $$
> \Pi_c =
> \begin{cases}
> cV-b, & V\le b,\\
> 0, & V>b.
> \end{cases}
> $$
>
> Again for $0\le b\le 1000$,
>
> $$
> \mathbb{E}[\Pi_c]
> = \frac{1}{1000}\int_0^b (cv-b)\,dv
> = \frac{1}{1000}\left[c\frac{b^2}{2}-b^2\right]
> = \frac{b^2}{1000}\left(\frac{c}{2}-1\right).
> $$
>
> This expected profit is:
>
> - negative if $c<2$,
> - zero if $c=2$,
> - positive if $c>2$.
>
> Therefore bidding becomes worthwhile exactly when
>
> $$
> \boxed{c>2.}
> $$
>
> At $c=2$, the expected profit is $0$ for all $b\in[0,1000]$; below $2$, the best bid is still $0$.
>
> ---
>
> ## Final answers
>
> 1. The optimal bid is
>
> $$
> \boxed{0.}
> $$
>
> 2. If you bid $b$ and get the car, your expected loss is
>
> $$
> \boxed{\frac{b}{2}.}
> $$
>
> 3. If the mechanic multiplies the car’s value by a constant factor $c$, then bidding is worthwhile iff
>
> $$
> \boxed{c>2.}
> $$
>
> So the required constant multiple is **greater than $2$**.
