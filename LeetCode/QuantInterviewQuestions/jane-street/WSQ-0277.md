> [!example] Question 277 · Jane Street
> ## Junior Trader Interview

You have 3,000 apples to transport from city A to city B, which are 1,000 miles apart. When there are apples on the truck, one apple is consumed per mile driven. Assume unlimited gasoline. What is the maximum number of apples that can be transported to city B? Given your answer is x, what market would you make on a lottery: you win \$10 if your answer is correct, otherwise \$0. (All calculations must be done without pen and paper.)

> ## Solution
>
> This problem hinges on an assumption the statement does not actually make explicit: how many apples the truck can carry at once. The two readings give very different answers, and it is worth stating both.
>
> ### Reading 1: no capacity limit is given
>
> Taken literally, the problem tells us apples are consumed at $1$ per mile while any are on the truck, and gasoline is unlimited — but it never states a limit on how many apples the truck can carry. If the truck can carry all $3000$ apples at once, the optimal (and only sensible) strategy is trivial: load all $3000$ apples and drive straight from $A$ to $B$, $1000$ miles, with no backtracking. This consumes
> $$
> 1000 \text{ apples (one per mile)},
> $$
> delivering
> $$
> 3000-1000=2000 \text{ apples.}
> $$
> There is no shuttling problem at all under this reading, since backtracking can only waste additional apples for no benefit.
>
> ### Reading 2: the classic "jeep problem" reading (capacity $1000$)
>
> The problem is almost certainly intended as the classic jeep/shuttle transport puzzle, which requires a stated carrying capacity to be interesting — the standard version of this puzzle fixes the capacity at $1000$ apples (one third of the load), and that is the assumption used below. Under this reading, the truck must shuttle back and forth whenever more than one truckload remains, and the effective loss rate depends on how many full loads still need to move forward.
>
> We begin with $3000$ apples, so initially there are $3$ truckloads.
>
> ---
>
> #### 1. Cost per mile while transporting $3000$ apples
>
> To move all apples forward while $3$ loads remain, the truck must, for every mile of net progress of the stockpile, make $3$ forward trips and $2$ return trips — $5$ traversals of that mile in total, each consuming $1$ apple. So the loss rate is
> $$
> 5 \text{ apples per mile.}
> $$
>
> We continue this until only $2000$ apples remain, since then only $2$ loads are left. To reduce from $3000$ to $2000$, we lose
> $$
> 1000 \text{ apples.}
> $$
> At $5$ apples per mile, this takes
> $$
> \frac{1000}{5} = 200 \text{ miles.}
> $$
>
> So after $200$ miles, we can consolidate to $2000$ apples.
>
> ---
>
> #### 2. Cost per mile while transporting $2000$ apples
>
> Now there are $2$ loads. For each mile of net progress, the truck makes $2$ forward trips and $1$ return trip — $3$ traversals, so the loss rate is
> $$
> 3 \text{ apples per mile.}
> $$
>
> We continue until only $1000$ apples remain, since then only one load is left. To reduce from $2000$ to $1000$, we lose
> $$
> 1000 \text{ apples.}
> $$
> At $3$ apples per mile, this requires
> $$
> \frac{1000}{3} = 333\tfrac{1}{3} \text{ miles.}
> $$
>
> So the position where $1000$ apples remain is
> $$
> 200 + 333\tfrac{1}{3} = 533\tfrac{1}{3} \text{ miles from A.}
> $$
>
> ---
>
> #### 3. Final stage with $1000$ apples
>
> Now the truck carries the remaining $1000$ apples directly to city $B$.
>
> Distance left:
> $$
> 1000 - 533\tfrac{1}{3} = 466\tfrac{2}{3} \text{ miles.}
> $$
>
> Since only one truckload remains, the loss rate is
> $$
> 1 \text{ apple per mile,}
> $$
> so apples lost on the final leg equal the distance,
> $$
> 466\tfrac{2}{3}.
> $$
>
> So apples delivered to $B$:
> $$
> 1000 - 466\tfrac{2}{3} = 533\tfrac{1}{3}.
> $$
>
> Therefore, under the capacity-$1000$ reading, the maximum number of apples that can be transported is
> $$
> x = 533\tfrac{1}{3}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Lottery valuation
>
> The lottery pays $10$ dollars if the answer given is exactly correct, and $0$ dollars otherwise.
>
> The trouble is that "the correct answer" is genuinely ambiguous here, for two reasons. First, there are two defensible readings of the problem itself: the literal one (no stated capacity, answer $2000$) and the classic shuttle-puzzle reading (capacity $1000$, answer $533\tfrac13$) — and a market-maker cannot be certain which one the question-setter has in mind. Second, even within the intended reading, this kind of multi-stage calculation is easy to get slightly wrong (an arithmetic slip in any one of the three stages), so there is real risk of being confidently wrong.
>
> Given that, treating the ticket as worth a certain $10$ dollars is not sound market-making: it assumes away exactly the uncertainty the question is testing. A better approach is to quote a two-sided market that reflects your confidence rather than certainty — for instance, a market around $5$ to $8$ dollars (out of a possible $10$), reflecting a substantial but not overwhelming degree of confidence in the answer $533\tfrac13$, while leaving room for the chance that the intended answer is something else (such as the literal-reading answer of $2000$, or a different assumed capacity), or that the arithmetic has an error.
>
> ---
>
> ## Final Answer
>
> - Literal reading (no capacity limit stated): drive straight, delivering
>   $$
>   \boxed{2000 \text{ apples.}}
>   $$
> - Classic jeep-problem reading (capacity $1000$, the intended version of this puzzle):
>   $$
>   \boxed{x = 533\tfrac{1}{3} \text{ apples}}.
>   $$
> - For the lottery, rather than asserting a certain $10$ dollars, quote a market reflecting the ambiguity — for example, around $5$ to $8$ dollars — rather than treating $533\tfrac13$ as certainly correct.
