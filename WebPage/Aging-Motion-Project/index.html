Abstract

We present an age-conditioned human motion generation framework for studying aging signatures in the context of assistive robotics. Our pipeline converts clinical gait recordings into learning-ready motion representations while addressing defects from legacy motion-capture data.

Using 420 clips from 138 subjects across young, mid-age, and elderly groups, we train a Spatial-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network (ST-GCN) to recognize age group from motion clips, achieving 64.6% classification accuracy. We then fine-tune a Motion Diffusion Model (MDM) with Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) to generate text-conditioned human motion with age-group control.

To evaluate whether generated motions preserve aging-related characteristics, we analyze clinical and kinematic gait metrics on 9,000 generated clips. Our results show that the model successfully reproduces spatial kinematic constraints in the Elderly condition, but spatiotemporal consistency remains unresolved — generated stride variability massively overshoots clinical baselines, which we attribute to the capacity ceiling of low-rank, discrete-token adaptation. These findings highlight dataset size, motion quality, and conditioning strength as key bottlenecks for demographic-aware motion prediction and assistive robot planning.

Motivation

Population aging is creating increasing demand for assistive robotic systems that can support older adults in daily living, mobility, rehabilitation, and safe human–robot interaction. For such systems, human motion models are not merely tools for animation or prediction; they provide a foundation for anticipating human movement, evaluating robot behaviors in simulation, and designing assistance strategies that are sensitive to functional diversity.

Recent Motion Diffusion Models (MDMs) synthesize realistic, language-conditioned human motion, but widely used motion-capture datasets such as AMASS are heavily biased toward young and healthy actors. As a result, current generative models can produce diverse actions and styles, yet often overlook the population-specific biomechanical factors that govern how different people actually move — a gap that is particularly consequential for robots interacting with older adults.

Clinical gait analysis provides a complementary foundation. Decades of biomechanics research have shown that gait encodes important information about aging, mobility, and balance, and modern sensor-based methods now allow quantification of physiological and spatiotemporal gait signatures. However, clinical gait data are rarely integrated with modern generative motion models.

Our work bridges this gap with a proof-of-concept framework that asks: can age-related motion signatures be learned from real clinical recordings, and reproduced by a modern text-conditioned diffusion model under explicit demographic control?

Data Processing Pipeline

Transforming raw optical marker data into a format suitable for diffusion model training requires a carefully designed multi-stage pipeline. We convert the Van Criekinge marker recordings into the HumanML3D 263-dimensional feature representation through SMPL body-model fitting followed by feature extraction, addressing defects from legacy motion-capture data along the way.

Multi-stage conversion from raw C3D marker recordings to HumanML3D-compatible features.

Stage Representation Shape Key Operation
0 Raw C3D marker trajectories (T, ~60, 3) @ 100 Hz Vicon optical capture
1 SMPL parametric body model θ: (T, 72), β: (10,), t: (T, 3) VPoser-regularized marker fitting with artifact correction
2 HumanML3D skeleton (22 joints) (T20Hz, 22, 3) Coordinate frame change + temporal resampling (100→20 Hz)
3 HumanML3D feature vector (T−1, 263) Root trajectory + joint positions/velocities/rotations + foot contacts

The pipeline achieves an overall 85× compression from raw marker data to training features while preserving clinically relevant kinematic cues. Motion is represented in a body-centered coordinate frame, making the learned representations invariant to global position and enabling better generalization across environments.

A key implementation fix involved correcting a coordinate transformation bug in the C3D-to-SMPL stage (Vicon Z-up → body-centered Y-up), which previously introduced systematic lean and descent artifacts that were incorrectly filtered as noise.

SMPL Fitting & Artifact Correction

Fitting SMPL to optical marker data introduces systematic artifacts that, if uncorrected, corrupt the age-related kinematic signal. We adapt VPoser's prior-space optimization with a two-stage L-BFGS schedule: a shape-free stage that recovers a local minimum in pose, followed by a shape-and-prior refinement stage. This staging ensures individual biomechanical details are preserved before generic pose priors take over.

Left: crouch-walking artifact induced by standard pose priors. Middle: our optimized pipeline recovers a natural posture. Right: mesh inflation artifact from naive marker mapping.

Approach & Results

Age Classification (ST-GCN++)

A Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network (ST-GCN++) operates directly on the 22-joint skeletal graph, learning to discriminate between three age groups: Young (<35), Mid-age (35–60), and Elderly (≥60). We fine-tune from an NTU-RGB+D-120 pretrained backbone, unfreezing the last two ST-GCN blocks (51.5% of parameters trainable), with a class-weighted cross-entropy loss to compensate for the mild age-group imbalance. The classifier serves to verify that the processed pipeline preserves age-discriminative kinematic signatures end-to-end.

The classifier reaches 64.6% validation accuracy on a strict subject-disjoint split. The confusion matrix shows the model consistently segregates the kinematic extremes (Young vs. Elderly); the primary failure mode is fragmentation of the Mid-age category — consistent with clinical findings that the Young/Mid-age kinematic gap is small relative to within-group variance.

Age-Conditioned Generation (LoRA-adapted MDM)

For motion synthesis, we adapt a pre-trained Motion Diffusion Model (MDM) using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), following the recipe of Sawdayee et al. End-to-end retraining is impractical given our compute budget and would also risk overwriting the strong text-to-motion prior already learned on HumanML3D. The LoRA update accepts discrete age-group style tokens (young, mid, old) at rank 12, with a prior-preservation loss to keep the base MDM motion distribution intact. The forward diffusion process is:

q(xt | x0) = 𝒩(xt; √āt x0, (1 − āt) I)

and the model is trained with the standard noise-prediction objective conditioned on both the CLIP text embedding and the age-group token.

We generate 9,000 motion clips (3,000 per age condition) from the walking prompt at classifier-free guidance scale s = 1.0. The qualitative samples below already show well-known coarse aging cues: the Elderly sample exhibits a noticeably hunched back, and the stride-to-stride foot-clearance height differs visibly between Young and Elderly conditions.

Quantitative Clinical Analysis

To rigorously evaluate whether aging signals are preserved end-to-end, we compute standard, training-free gait-analysis metrics on both real and generated clips: spatiotemporal parameters (walking speed, stride length, cadence, computed via zero-velocity crossings of the ankle joints), gait variability via the Coefficient of Variation (CV = (σ / μ) × 100%), and joint Range of Motion (peak flexion / extension of the hip and knee over the gait cycle).

Analysis of the ground-truth data confirms that the expected aging signal is preserved end-to-end through our conversion pipeline. We observe monotonic Young→Elderly trends consistent with clinical literature: walking speed decreases (1.22 → 1.10 m/s), stride length decreases (1.34 → 1.26 m), and knee RoM decreases (54.9° → 51.5°). Stride-time CV moves in the opposite direction, increasing from 8.9% to 15.0%.

Qualitatively, comparative heatmaps of joint angular velocity over a normalized gait cycle show that the elderly subject (Age 78) exhibits reduced peak velocities and constrained range of motion, whereas the young subject (Age 24) demonstrates distinct, high-intensity kinematic bursts.

Kinematic signature heatmap. Joint angular velocities over the normalized gait cycle. Young subject (left) shows higher peak velocities and more pronounced hip extension; the elderly subject (right) shows reduced amplitude across most joints — consistent with the compensatory kinematics documented in aging gait research.

When evaluating the generated clips conditioned on age, the metrics reveal both the potential and the limitations of the LoRA-adapted model. Generated motions successfully reproduce the expected age-related change in Range-of-Motion: the old token produces significantly constrained hip RoM (21.5°) compared to the young and mid tokens (24–25°), indicating that the LoRA adapter learns and imposes spatial kinematic constraints.

However, the stride Coefficient of Variation is uniformly elevated (16–19%) across all generated clips, far exceeding clinical reference values. We attribute this to the stochastic noise introduced in the diffusion process overwhelming stride consistency — a capacity ceiling of low-rank, discrete-token adaptation.

Discussion & Future Work

Our preliminary findings reveal both the promise and the current limitations of data-driven models in learning age-related motion signatures. The ST-GCN age-signature detector successfully segregates kinematic extremes (Young vs. Elderly), confirming that motion clips contain discriminative biomechanical information related to age. The LoRA-adapted MDM successfully imposes spatial kinematic constraints — reducing hip RoM in Elderly-conditioned clips — yet spatiotemporal consistency remains unresolved.

We attribute this failure to the capacity ceiling of low-rank, discrete-token adaptation: a rank-12 LoRA update can push a few high-leverage statistics such as hip RoM toward an age-specific mean, but cannot coherently re-shape stride timing, leaving the base MDM prior to dominate the temporal axis. This gap highlights an important challenge for assistive robotics in eldercare: producing visually plausible age-conditioned motion is not sufficient; motion prediction systems must also preserve biomechanical characteristics that affect anticipation, safety, and interaction.

Current limitations:

  • Dataset size — all currently available age-diverse motion datasets are too small to train modern deep generative models without overfitting.
  • Conditioning strength — discrete age-group tokens through LoRA are parameter-efficient but may not be expressive enough to capture gradual, continuous age-related change or individual variability.
  • Evaluation metrics — standard motion-generation metrics (FID, diversity) are not designed to measure biomechanical aging fidelity; new frameworks are needed.

Future Architecture: Continuous Latent Age Conditioning

Future work will move beyond discrete age-group conditioning toward a continuous latent conditioning architecture. A biomechanical style encoder is pretrained on AMASS via supervised contrastive learning to capture per-subject kinematic identity. An age head is then fine-tuned on top of the frozen encoder using clinical age labels. Finally, the MDM transformer is retrained end-to-end with conditioning formed by concatenating a CLIP text embedding ztext and the biomechanical style embedding zstyle (containing the latent age vector zage).

At inference, a scalar age combined with a text prompt enables continuous interpolation across the age axis and personalization toward individual functional ability rather than coarse demographic bins.
