On the Role of Haptic Feedback in Demonstration Collection for Visuomotor Policy Learning

Joanna Peng1,2*, Aidan Schreder1,2*, Yiran Liu1,2*, Manjot Dola1,2, Benjamin Tran1,2, Yue Hu2, Pengcheng Xi1

Standard teleoperation for imitation learning is force-blind: operators cannot perceive contact, leading to suboptimal demonstrations on contact-rich tasks. We propose a haptic-fused framework using a wrist force–torque sensor and haptic controller, which reduces peak contact force by 64% and improves surface contact maintenance by 45%—yet these data-quality improvements do not directly translate to task success, identifying task selection and open-loop execution as the key bottlenecks.

Motivation

Contact-rich manipulation tasks—such as compliant object pressing, surface wiping, and part insertion—require continuous regulation of interaction forces, not merely precise end-effector positioning. Imitation learning methods such as Diffusion Policy and ACT achieve strong performance on spatial manipulation tasks, yet their demonstrations are typically collected through force-blind teleoperation: the human operator never perceives the contact forces measured at the robot's wrist.

This gap matters in practice. Without haptic feedback, operators must infer contact state purely from visual cues, which can lead to excessive force application, intermittent surface contact, and generally suboptimal interaction behavior. Our work investigates whether closing this sensory loop—rendering measured contact forces back to the operator in real time—produces higher-quality demonstrations and, ultimately, better-performing policies.

System

The hardware platform comprises a Universal Robots UR10e manipulator controlled via RTDE for low-latency command execution, a Robotiq parallel gripper, two Intel RealSense RGB cameras (one wrist-mounted, one external), a SensuReal 6-DoF force–torque sensor at the wrist, and a Haply Inverse3 controller with VerseGrip attachment for 6-DoF haptic input and force rendering.

At each control cycle, the system synchronously acquires robot state, operator input from the haptic device, multi-camera visual observations, and wrist force–torque measurements. These are timestamp-aligned onto a unified time grid and stored as a Zarr replay buffer. In the bilateral teleoperation condition, measured contact forces are conditioned with low-pass filtering and rate limiting, regulated by a unilateral spring–damper virtual contact model, and rendered back to the operator via the haptic device, closing the force-feedback loop in real time.

Haptic Teleoperation Loop

The UR10e streams end-effector force, torque, position, and velocity into a force-alignment and processing module. The signal is conditioned via low-pass filtering and rate limiting, passed through a virtual contact model (unilateral spring–damper), scaled, and rendered as haptic feedback on the Haply Inverse3. In the reverse direction, the operator's position, velocity, and button states are integrated to generate target 6-DoF end-effector commands, closing the bilateral teleoperation cycle.

Policy Architecture

The policy is based on the Diffusion Policy framework. Multi-view RGB images are encoded using ResNet-18 backbones; the resulting visual features are concatenated with low-dimensional proprioceptive and force–torque inputs to form a global conditioning vector. A 1D U-Net denoising network predicts action sequences conditioned on a four-step observation horizon. During inference, Real-Time Chunking (RTC) is applied to smooth trajectory execution in real-world settings.

Tasks and Evaluation Metrics

Two contact-rich manipulation tasks are evaluated under two teleoperation conditions: unilateral control (no haptic feedback) and bilateral control (real-time force feedback rendered to the operator).

Demonstration Quality

Demonstration quality is assessed through interaction-based metrics derived from the wrist force–torque signal and end-effector kinematics. In both tasks, haptic feedback produces demonstrably more task-appropriate force behavior.

Across both tasks, haptic feedback enables operators to regulate contact forces in a context-sensitive manner—reducing excessive peak forces when avoidance is required, and maintaining higher sustained forces when continuous contact is needed.

Policy Performance

We evaluate whether the improvements in demonstration quality translate into downstream policy performance. Policies are trained separately on datasets collected under each teleoperation condition, with all other training hyperparameters held constant.

While the haptic-feedback policy achieves a marginally higher task success rate on both tasks, the improvement is not statistically significant. The force profiles from policy inference (Fig. 1, bottom rows) confirm that the learned policies exhibit different force behaviors reflecting their respective training distributions; however, neither policy produces mean contact forces consistently within the task-appropriate reference range. The use of Real-Time Chunking during inference smooths the executed trajectories, which may attenuate the transient force differences observed during demonstration collection.

The central bottleneck is task selection. In both evaluated tasks, the contact state is partially inferable from visual observations—the camera provides sufficient positional and surface-deformation cues to guide contact. Under such conditions, force-enriched demonstrations offer limited marginal benefit to the policy.

Discussion

Our results show that haptic feedback enables context-sensitive force regulation during teleoperation—operators reduce peak forces when avoidance is appropriate and maintain higher sustained forces when continuous contact is required. These improvements are reflected in the learned policy's force profiles. However, they do not yield statistically significant task success improvements for the tasks evaluated.

We introduce force-observability as a principled criterion for task selection in contact-rich imitation learning benchmarks. A task is force-observable if its contact state and force demands can be reliably inferred from visual observations alone. Tasks with low force-observability—such as peg-in-hole insertion, torque-limited cap tightening, or multi-material slip detection—are expected to benefit more directly from haptic-augmented demonstrations, as the visual stream cannot substitute for proprioceptive contact information during policy learning.

A complementary direction for future work is deeper force integration within the policy architecture. Beyond concatenating force readings into the observation vector, outputting a stiffness signal alongside the motion trajectory—executed via an admittance controller—would decouple high-level motion planning from low-level force regulation, potentially enabling more robust contact execution under uncertainty.
