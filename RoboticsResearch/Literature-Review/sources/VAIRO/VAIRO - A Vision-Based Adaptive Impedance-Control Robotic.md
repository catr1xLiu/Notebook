# VAIRO: A Vision-Based Adaptive Impedance-Control Robotic Framework

Jeffrey Lee<sup>1</sup> , Alexander Wong<sup>1</sup> , Yue Hu<sup>1</sup>

*Abstract*— In this work, we present VAIRO, a Vision-based Adaptive Impedance-control RObotic framework for the purpose of manipulating soft materials, centered around the use case of rolling croissant dough for use in artisanal bakeries. Traditional automated processes for the industrial production of croissants consist of overly bulky equipment and fail to preserve the artisanal quality of hand-rolled croissants, with one of the major challenges being the high variability in the dough properties. VAIRO addresses these challenges by introducing a novel vision-based adaptive Cartesian impedance control strategy for collaborative robot arms to regulate rolling forces in realtime without the need for estimating the properties of the soft material. As such, VAIRO mimics the tactile adjustments made by human pastry chefs, ensuring consistent layer thickness and eliminating gaps. Using a Kinova Gen3 robotic arm and a custom-designed end-effector, we demonstrate that VAIRO can successfully manipulate various "doughs" without estimating any material properties. These results are promising and offer a cost-effective, small-scale alternative for local craft bakeries to leverage automation while maintaining high artisanal quality.

## I. INTRODUCTION

While robotic automation can offer precise solutions to repetitive tasks, leading to time and cost savings in production lines, such precise repetition is not always desired. This is especially the case with artisanal craft goods, where consumers seek the artisanal touch, believed to be of higher quality [1]. An example is baked goods, where machinemade pastries are cheaper and perceived as lower-grade with respect to more expensive but artisanal hand-made ones [2].

Despite this, there is interest from the small-scale craft industry in adopting recent technologies such as robotics [3], especially in a human-robot collaboration fashion. Current industrial machinery for high-volume batch production of goods tends to be bulky and costly, limiting its flexibility and making it less suitable for small-scale or customized production. Collaborative robots (cobots) represent a smallscale versatile solution that can be adopted in a humancentered environment such as current craft laboratories and/or kitchens. Large robot manufacturers also showed interest in this domain, with the KUKA 2024 Innovation Award [4] calling for solutions applied to craft, tailored to small-medium enterprises. In this context, we investigated the problem of artisanal baking with local pastry chefs at Duo

The chefs have expressed the need to automate several highly repetitive baking procedures with versatile machines such as cobots, with the requirement that the cobots must reproduce the artisanal quality of the baked goods. For puff pastry specifically, [6] found that the dough resistance continuously increased while dough extensibility varied with increasing levels of salt and [7] observed a variation of rheological properties from just the variation in fat that is used in the puff pastry and a general softening behavior with increasing sheeting steps used for puff pastry. This variability of physical properties poses a difficult problem for manipulation as estimations on material properties cannot be confidently relied upon for force regulation. Therefore, the problem of dough manipulation is restricted to using only sensor measurements for the state estimation of the dough.

The deformability of soft materials creates a complex problem of tracking a soft object's shape and pose. One popular approach in current literature is to estimate the state of a soft object during manipulation using its physical properties [8] [9], but for materials where there is a high variation in physical properties, such as puff pastry dough, these model-based methods are not a viable option. Thus, we rely solely on model-free approaches, using sensors to estimate the soft material's state.

Model-free approaches in literature use machine learning to directly map sensor measurements to control the robot, as demonstrated in the works of [10], [11], [12], and [13]. These studies utilize vision to capture real-time point-cloud or RGBD data of the soft object being manipulated. This data is then processed for feature extraction. The difference between the current pose and the desired pose of the features is then used to calculate reference velocities for the visual servoing of robot manipulators.

For the robot action, an adaptive force-based control must be used to regulate and maintain the contact between the end-effector and the dough. Since its introduction, Cartesian impedance control [14] has been successfully utilized in many contact-based applications [15], [16], with many opensource implementations tested on cobots such as the KUKA LBR iiwa [17], Franka Emika Panda [17], and Kinova Gen3<sup>2</sup> .

However, to the best of our knowledge, existing works have not used vision feedback for force regulation with impedance control, which is essential to create a craft bakery robot helper that can automate baked goods production mimicking the techniques of pastry chefs. With the great variability of puff pastry rheology, the vision-based feedback solution allows us to steer away from the estimation of the physical properties of the dough. Furthermore, current automated solutions to croissant production make use of large machinery in precise environments with high cost, resulting in synthetic quality croissants, while our approach would allow small-scale manufacturing with artisanal quality.

# *B. Contributions*

In this work, we introduce a low-cost, small-scale solution using a robotic manipulator with vision-based real-time force regulation for craft bakeries, with a focus on rolling croissants into their iconic shape, while maintaining artisanal qualities. To achieve this, a small-size collaborative manipulator will be used with an adaptive Cartesian impedance controller to regulate the rolling force to form the dough into the croissant shape. The stiffness parameters of the Cartesian impedance controller are adapted in real time to maintain consistent layer thickness and eliminate gaps through vision measurements. Specifically, this work presents the VAIRO framework, which consists of the following:

- A computer vision process to effectively segment and estimate the size of croissant parts with RGBD data;
- A vision feedback loop to adjust for an adaptive Cartesian impedance controller's stiffness parameters for force regulation.

The framework is experimentally verified with the Kinova Gen3 using a custom end-effector designed to mimic a human's hand for puff pastry dough rolling (as shown in Figs. 1c and 1d), with four different types of dough. It is worth noting that while the VAIRO framework was motivated and centered around the shaping of croissants, this paper contributes a novel framework to the manipulation of diverse soft materials whose physical properties are difficult or impossible to estimate in real time, which is the case of many real-world applications.

# II. METHODOLOGY

The proposed framework uses vision to measure both the thickness of the rolled croissant layers and the gaps between layers during the croissant rolling process as feedback to adjust the stiffness and damping parameters of a Cartesian impedance controller in real-time. To achieve this, only a robotic manipulator and a depth camera are needed. The approach consists of four parts: end-effector design, adaptive impedance control, computer vision model for segmentation, and integration of visual measurements to adjust the stiffness and damping parameters of the impedance controller. In this section, we present all the components along with the experimental setup and implementation details.

# *A. Experimental Setup*

The setup used for experimentally validating VAIRO consists of the Kinova Gen3 for the robotic manipulator (and not a KUKA arm as the framework was extended and results were collected after the Innovation Award 2024, furthermore, the Gen3 is a more compact and smaller size robot) and the Intel RealSense D405 camera for the close-range vision. Fig. 3 shows the setup, where the camera faces perpendicularly to the rolling direction of the robotic manipulator to capture the cross-section of the puff-pastry dough during the rolling process. Additionally, the camera is placed 7 [cm] away from the dough as this is the minimum depth measurement for the D405, and is centered at the same height as the dough to achieve the best measurement accuracy as possible. This setup allows physical isolation of the camera from the robot to reduce measurement noise from the robot's movement.

## *C. Cartesian impedance control*

The commanded torque to achieve Cartesian impedance behavior on a 7 degrees of freedom (DoF) manipulator is as described by Eq. 1 [14]:

$$\tau_{ci} = J(q)^T (K_p(x_{des} - x) + K_d J(q) \dot{q}) \quad (1)$$

where  $\tau_{ci} \in \mathbb{R}^7$  are the joint torques needed for the robotic manipulator to exhibit Cartesian impedance behavior,  $K_p, K_d \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times 6}$  specifies the desired Cartesian stiffness and damping, respectively,  $J \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times 7}$  represents the robot Jacobian,  $q, \dot{q} \in \mathbb{R}^7$  represent the joint positions and velocities respectively, and  $x_{des} \in \mathbb{R}^6$  and  $x \in \mathbb{R}^6$  are the desired and current end-effector pose respectively.

The development of the adaptive impedance controller relies on Eq. 1, where the  $K_p$  and  $K_d$  stiffness and damping parameters are adapted in real-time. For the purposes of dough rolling, only the z-directional (vertical axis perpendicular to the working surface, as in Fig. 3) stiffness needs to be adapted for force regulation. As a result, all other axes (x, y)

and orientations (roll pitch, yaw) should use high stiffnesses for improved tracking performances. Further, damping does not have a significant impact for applied force regulation as compared to stiffness, so it can be computed to critical damping with the adapted stiffness using Eq. 2:

$$K_d = 2\zeta\sqrt{K_p} \quad (2)$$

where the damping factor  $\zeta$  is set to 1 for critical damping.

To achieve the desired Cartesian impedance behavior on the Kinova Gen3, the Pinocchio rigid body dynamics library [18] is used to compute the required commanded torques using the dynamic model of the robot. The dynamics of the robot in joint space is defined as in [19]:

$$M(q)\ddot{q} + C(q, \dot{q})\dot{q} + g(q) + \tau_{ext} = \tau_c \quad (3)$$

where  $M(q) \in \mathbb{R}^{7 \times 7}$  is the inertia matrix,  $C(q, \dot{q}) \in \mathbb{R}^{7 \times 7}$  captures the effects of Coriolis and centripetal forces,  $g \in \mathbb{R}^7$  represents the gravity effects, and  $\tau_c \in \mathbb{R}^7$  and  $\tau_{\text{ext}} \in \mathbb{R}^7$  represents the commanded and external torques respectively. Using this model, the Cartesian impedance controller on the robot is achieved by using the commanded torque  $\tau_c$ :

$$\tau_c = \tau_{ci} + M(q)\ddot{q} + C(q,\dot{q})\dot{q} + g(q) \quad (4)$$

summing  $\tau_{ci}$ , defined in Eq. 1, with compensation for the inertial, coriolis, and gravity torques.

To implement the controller on the physical robot as described by Eqs. 1 and 4, Kinova's Kortex API<sup>3</sup> is used for real-time communication with the Gen3 in torque control mode, and is also used to retrieve the joint positions  $(q)$ , velocities  $(\dot{q})$ , and torques  $(\tau)$ . The state information is fed to Pinocchio to calculate the Jacobian  $(J)$ , Coriolis matrix  $(c(q, \dot{q}))$ , and gravity compensation  $(g(q))$ .

# *D. Croissant Parts Instance Segmentation*

Although this application involves the manipulation of a 3D object, the problem can be reduced to a 2D one through tool design. The designed end-effector allows for the same force to be applied throughout the width of the croissant during the rolling process with its symmetric design (see Sec. II-B). Leveraging on this symmetric manipulation, more robust, open-source computer vision tools designed for 2D images, such as Roboflow<sup>4</sup> and YOLO<sup>5</sup>, can be used.

The overview of the entire computer vision process is outlined in Fig. 2. To determine the croissant layer thickness and gaps, a two-stage object detection and segmentation technique is employed. First, object detection is used to detect a bounding box for the manipulated croissant dough. Second, using the cropped image of this bounding box, image segmentation is used to detect different parts of the croissant. Specifically, instance segmentation is used to detect the croissant's core, top layer, and any gaps in between the rolled layers. This two-stage detection architecture significantly improves instance segmentation accuracy by constraining the segmentation area to the croissant's bounding box. This reduces the number of pixels that need classification and directs training toward positive detection, as the model is certain it is analyzing only a croissant. For example, performing image segmentation only on the croissant allows dark spots to be classified as gaps between its layers, whereas segmenting the whole scene would require additional training to reduce false positive detection of shadows as croissant gaps.

A linear trajectory following the top of the rolling dough is calculated using the roll length formula:</p><p block-type="Equation"><math display="block">l = \frac{\pi h^2}{4t} \quad (5)</math></p><p>where <math>l</math> is the length of the rolled dough, <math>t</math> is the nominal thickness of the dough, and <math>h</math> is the height of the croissant. Creating a linear trajectory in the <math>x</math> direction (Fig. 3), Eq. 5 can be used to calculate the needed <math>z</math> to maintain contact</p><p>with the croissant by substituting <math>l</math> withe <math>x</math> and <math>h</math> with <math>z</math>:</p><p block-type="Equation"><math display="block">z = \sqrt{\frac{4xt}{\pi}} \quad (6)</math></p><p>From this trajectory, a manipulator should be able to roll the croissant by using position control with slight compression. However, any slip in the contact between the endeffector and the surface of the dough will cause rolling to fail, as the manipulator cannot recover the contact. Therefore, an adaptive force-based control is necessary.</p><p>At the start of the rolling process, the rolling tool is positioned at <math>z = 0</math>, in contact with the tip of the croissant dough. As the tool follows the aforementioned linear trajectory to roll the dough, it should maintain consistent contact to ensure proper croissant shaping. However, due to slipping and high static friction needed for rolling, maintaining this contact requires adjusting the tool's position to a z-value 10% lower (determined experimentally) than the calculated trajectory. To maintain accurate trajectory tracking, the impedance controller applies a high stiffness in all directions except the adaptive z direction. Additionally, the high orientation stiffness helps maintain a uniform rolling force across the width of the dough. In the z direction, the nominal stiffness is used until defects in the rolled dough are detected with the vision module. The following equation is used for determining the z stiffness:</p><p block-type="Equation"><math display="block">K_p = K_{p\text{-nom}} - K_{p\text{-layer}} + K_{p\text{-gap}} \quad (7)</math></p><p>where <math>K_{p,nom}</math> is the nominal stiffness, <math>K_{p,layer}</math> is the stiffness adjustment based on the top layer's thickness deviation and <math>K_{p,gap}</math> is the stiffness adjustment based on the gaps in between the croissant layers. <math>K_{p,layer}</math> is calculated as the nominal stiffness multiplied by the croissant's top layer thickness percentage deviation from the nominal dough layer thickness. This is calculated using:</p><p block-type="Equation"><math display="block">K_{p, \text{layer}} = \begin{cases} 0 &amp; \text{if } t_{\text{layer}} \leq t_{\text{nom}} \\ K_{p, \text{nom}} \times \left(1 - \frac{t_{\text{nom}} - t_{\text{layer}}}{t_{\text{nom}}}\right) &amp; \text{if } t_{\text{layer}} &gt; t_{\text{nom}} \end{cases} \quad (8)</math></p><p>where <math>t_{nom}</math> is the nominal dough layer thickness and <math>t_{layer}</math> is the estimated croissant's top layer thickness during the rolling process from Sec.II-D. The rationale behind this calculation is that detecting compression in the croissant's top layer indicates excessive rolling pressure, necessitating a reduction in the applied force. Consequently, this stiffness adjustment functions solely as a reduction from the nominal value in Eq. 7. Moreover, this decrease in stiffness is only applied at the instantaneous moment that the croissant layer is detected to be smaller than nominal until the next measurement is received.</p><p>To get a relative size reference of the measured gap, a fractional comparison of the gap compared to the nominal layer thickness is used. This relative value is then used for the <math>K_{p,gap}</math> stiffness gain calculation as a product with the nominal stiffness as follows:</p><p block-type="Equation"><math display="block">K_{p\text{-gap}} += K_{p\text{-nom}} \times \frac{t_{\text{gap}}}{t_{\text{nom}}} \div 100 \quad (9)</math></p><p>where <math>t_{gap}</math> is the estimated height of the gap from Sec.II-II-D. The <math>K_{p,gap}</math> stiffness adjustment is applied to increase the stiffness of the controller, as shown in Eq. 7. The detection of a gap indicates that the dough is stiffer than the nominal value, necessitating an increase in downward rolling pressure to eliminate the gap and ensure proper dough formation. Furthermore, the gap stiffness adjustment division by 100 in Eq. 9 was determined experimentally to moderate the rate of stiffness adaptation. Experimental observations revealed that without this scaling, the gain would increase too rapidly and compress the croissants before the vision feedback can take measurements to correct for this error.</p><p>In contrast to the instantaneous <math>K_{p,layer}</math> gains, the <math>K_{p,gap}</math> gain here is cumulative, i.e. stiffness will continue to increase until gaps are eliminated and its value is then kept for the rest of the rolling process. Through experimentation with instantaneous <math>K_{p,gap}</math> gains, it was observed that rolling higher stiffness materials resulted in periodic stiffness increases in an oscillatory pattern. This formed many gaps in the croissants when the stiffness decreased. This behavior indicated that stiffness should be kept higher when rolling stiffer material, thus this cumulative increase in stiffness scheme was used instead. Additionally, with this cumulative approach, the <math>K_{p,layer}</math> adjustment can still provide instantaneous pressure relaxation during rolling to correct for overcompensation in stiffness gain or variations in the puff pastry dough's stiffness.</p><h3>III. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK</h2><p block-type="Text">VAIRO, a Vision-based Adaptive Impedance-control RObotic framework to estimate soft material measurements for manipulation was presented. The framework was developed using a cobot-based dough rolling task as a proof of concept for artisanal croissant production. It employed a two-stage computer vision process with dough detection followed by croissant part segmentation to estimate layer and gap thicknesses. These values informed a Cartesian impedance controller that adjusted stiffness by increasing it for gaps and reducing it for compressions to shape the dough. Experiments with a physical cobot on four different types of "dough": puff-pastry, Play-Doh, Creatology sensory dough, and Crayola modeling clay, show the effectiveness of VAIRO, highlighting how the adaptation of the controller stiffness in real-time using visual measurements aids in maintaining layer thickness and eliminating gaps, leading to the final tightly rolled croissant shape without flattening.</p><p block-type="Text">Due to the high joint friction of the chosen physical robot, the Kinova Gen3, the resultant force regulation was not as precise as desired and with latency. This resulted in the manipulator exerting undesired forces that do not correlate to the desired stiffness, inhibiting proper analysis of the force regulation. Future work to investigate this adaptive Cartesian impedance controller includes the development of a more reactive and precise controller such that stiffness modulation schemes can be investigated to determine better measurement to stiffness adjustment mappings for soft material manipulation. VAIRO implemented on manipulators with higher precision force sensing or friction compensation on the Gen3 may also lead to improved outcomes.</p><p block-type="Text">Croissant parts segmentation could also be improved upon in future work with the integration of object tracking techniques such as optical flow. The current vision framework performs croissant detection on each frame independently, so failed detection in certain frames results in inconsistent feedback to the controller. Using object tracking can assist in providing consistent estimation throughout the entire rolling motion and provide more reactive feedback to the controller.</p><p block-type="Text" class="has-continuation">The VAIRO framework can be easily translated to the manipulation of other soft materials where rheological properties are unpredictable or a general purpose solution is</p><p block-type="Text">desired. The only translation needed to use this adaptive impedance control scheme is to define visual indications and metrics of the soft material's stiffness. Other application examples span a wide domain including, but not limited to dough kneading, automation of cleaning tasks using sponges or fabrics, or surgical operations on soft tissue.</p><h2>REFERENCES</h2><p block-type="Text">[1] G.
